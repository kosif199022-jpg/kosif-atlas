### Dual Process Model of Grief (Stroebe & Schut) — Applied · #1458 · `high` · behavioral · INSTRUCTION
- **What it is:** The Dual Process Model (DPM) proposes that healthy grieving involves oscillation between two orientations: loss-oriented coping (confronting the grief itself—crying, yearning, dwelling on the deceased) and restoration-oriented coping (attending to life changes that result from loss—learning new skills, forming new identities, managing daily tasks). The critical insight is that neither orientation alone constitutes healthy grief. People who only do loss-oriented processing become stuck in chronic grief; those who only do restoration work may experience delayed grief complications. The oscillation between the two—sometimes within the same hour—is the adaptive mechanism. This model also explains gender differences: many men lean toward restoration orientation (practical tasks), which others may misread as 'not grieving properly.'
- **How it helps:** This model is exceptionally useful for knowledge workers navigating major transitions because it validates both emotional processing AND practical forward motion. After a job loss, you might spend the morning updating your LinkedIn (restoration) and the afternoon mourning your lost team (loss-orientation)—the DPM says this oscillation IS the healthy response. Use it to: (1) stop judging yourself for 'not being productive' during grief or for 'avoiding your feelings' when you're being practical, (2) understand why some team members seem to 'move on too fast' while others seem stuck, (3) design reintegration timelines after organizational loss.
- **When to use:** When grieving individuals (including yourself) seem to swing between deep sadness and productive functioning and you're unsure if this is healthy. When designing organizational support after layoffs or major changes. When you notice someone is stuck exclusively in one orientation.
- **Unique value:** Replaces the idea that grief is a linear progression with a dynamic oscillation model backed by strong empirical evidence. Uniquely explains why distraction and avoidance can be adaptive, not pathological. The only major grief model that explicitly accounts for the practical life-rebuilding dimension.
- **Limits:** Developed primarily in Western bereavement contexts. May not fully capture grief in collectivist cultures where communal processing dominates. The oscillation pattern may not apply equally to traumatic or violent loss. Doesn't specify optimal ratios of loss vs. restoration orientation.
- **Foundations:** Founded on Stroebe and Schut's oscillation theory from grief psychology, integrating coping theory (Lazarus) and attachment theory. Draws on bereavement research distinguishing loss-orientation from restoration-orientation as adaptive oscillating processes.

### Emotional Regulation Toolkit (DBT-Informed TIPP Protocol) · #2311 · `high` · behavioral · INSTRUCTION
- **What it is:** A four-technique emergency protocol from Dialectical Behavior Therapy for rapidly reducing emotional intensity when you're in crisis or emotional flooding. TIPP: T — Temperature: Apply cold water or an ice pack to your face (especially forehead and cheeks) for 30 seconds. This activates the dive reflex, immediately lowering heart rate and calming the nervous system. I — Intense Exercise: Do 20-30 minutes of vigorous exercise (running, jumping jacks, pushups) to metabolize stress hormones. Even 5 minutes helps. P — Paced Breathing: Exhale longer than you inhale (4-count inhale, 7-count exhale). Extended exhales activate the parasympathetic nervous system. Do for 2-5 minutes. P — Progressive Muscle Relaxation: Starting from your feet, tense each muscle group for 5 seconds, then release. Work up to your face. The full cycle takes 10-15 minutes. Use these techniques IN ORDER of urgency: Temperature for crisis-level emotion, then exercise, then breathing, then relaxation. Each technique takes the edge off enough to access the next one.
- **How it helps:** Provides the fastest evidence-based methods for reducing emotional intensity when thinking-based strategies are impossible (because the prefrontal cortex is offline during emotional flooding). The temperature technique alone can reduce heart rate by 10-25 BPM in 30 seconds — faster than any other technique. These are not coping strategies; they are physiological interventions that work whether or not you believe in them. Immediately actionable: the next time you feel overwhelmed, splash cold water on your face and notice the immediate shift.
- **When to use:** When emotions are at 8/10 or above and you cannot think clearly. During panic attacks, rage episodes, intense grief waves, or overwhelming anxiety. Before making any decision while emotionally flooded. When you need to calm down FAST to function in a meeting, conversation, or crisis.
- **Unique value:** The only emotional regulation protocol that works through physiology rather than cognition. All other regulation techniques (reframing, mindfulness, journaling) require the thinking brain to be online. TIPP works even when thinking is impossible — it calms the body first, which then allows the mind to come back online.
- **Limits:** These are emergency tools, not long-term solutions — they manage the crisis but don't address root causes. Cold water immersion may not be practical in all settings. Intense exercise requires physical ability and appropriate environment. Doesn't replace therapy for chronic emotional dysregulation.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in Dialectical Behavior Therapy (Linehan, 1993), developed for borderline personality disorder and extended broadly. TIPP draws on neuroscience of arousal regulation — temperature, exercise, and breathing directly modulate the autonomic nervous system. Reflects behavioral medicine research on physiological intervention in emotional dysregulation.

### Habit Stacking Protocol (James Clear) · #2312 · `high` · behavioral · INSTRUCTION
- **What it is:** A behavior design protocol for building new habits by linking them to existing habits, leveraging the brain's existing neural pathways. The formula: 'After I [CURRENT HABIT], I will [NEW HABIT].' Step 1 — List your existing daily habits (brush teeth, pour coffee, sit at desk, eat lunch, walk through door after work). Step 2 — Match the new habit you want to build with the existing habit that best fits in terms of timing, location, and energy level. Step 3 — Make the new habit tiny: the initial version should take less than 2 minutes ('After I pour my coffee, I will write one sentence in my journal'). Step 4 — Execute the stack consistently for 2-3 weeks before expanding. Step 5 — Chain multiple new habits together once each link is established: 'After I pour coffee → journal one sentence → review today's priorities → do 5 pushups.' Advanced technique: Create habit stacks for different parts of your day — morning stack, work-arrival stack, post-lunch stack, evening stack. The existing habit serves as the cue, eliminating the need for reminders, willpower, or motivation.
- **How it helps:** Solves the #1 reason new habits fail: there's no reliable trigger. Motivation fades, reminders get ignored, and willpower depletes. An existing habit provides a built-in, automatic trigger that fires every day without effort. The 2-minute rule prevents the overwhelm that kills most habit attempts. Success rate for habit stacking is dramatically higher than for habits that rely on time-based triggers ('I'll meditate at 7 AM') because existing habits are more reliable cues than clock times.
- **When to use:** When you want to build any new daily habit. When previous habit attempts have failed due to forgetting or lack of motivation. When designing a morning routine, evening routine, or work routine. When you want to build multiple habits simultaneously by chaining them.
- **Unique value:** The only habit formation protocol that eliminates the need for external reminders or internal motivation. By piggybacking on existing neural pathways, it makes habit initiation automatic rather than effortful — which is the defining characteristic of habits.
- **Limits:** Requires a stable existing habit to anchor to — chaotic schedules undermine the approach. The 2-minute starter may feel too small for ambitious goals, leading people to skip the tiny version. Habit chains can break if one link fails. Doesn't address the deeper motivational and identity-level changes that sustain long-term behavior change.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in James Clear's synthesis of behavior change research and habit formation science. Draws on Fogg's behavior model and Duhigg's habit loop (cue-routine-reward). Habit stacking exploits existing neural pathways — attaching new behaviors to established habits reduces the implementation intention burden.

### Business Crisis Management Protocol (Incident Command) · #2371 · `high` · business · INSTRUCTION
- **What it is:** A structured protocol for managing business crises — server outages, product failures, supply chain disruptions, PR disasters, or security breaches — adapted from the Incident Command System used by emergency services. Step 1 — Declare the incident: Someone must explicitly say 'This is a crisis, and I am taking command.' Ambiguity about whether something is a crisis causes the most damaging delays. Step 2 — Assign roles: Incident Commander (makes decisions), Communications Lead (internal and external messaging), Technical Lead (diagnosis and fix), Scribe (documents timeline and decisions in real-time). Step 3 — Establish a cadence: Hourly (or more frequent) situation updates following a fixed format: Current status → What we know → What we don't know → Next actions → ETA. Step 4 — Parallel workstreams: Diagnosis and fix happen simultaneously with stakeholder communication. Never go silent while working on the problem — silence generates fear. Step 5 — Escalation triggers: Pre-define conditions that require executive involvement, customer notification, or regulatory disclosure. Step 6 — Resolution and stand-down: Incident Commander declares the crisis resolved. All participants attend a blameless post-mortem within 48 hours.
- **How it helps:** Provides military-grade crisis structure for business situations where most organizations improvise badly. The 'declare the incident' step alone saves hours — in most organizational crises, the first 30-60 minutes are wasted because no one explicitly acknowledges that a crisis is happening. The role assignment prevents the chaos of everyone working on everything. The communication cadence prevents the information vacuum that causes stakeholder panic. The blameless post-mortem ensures organizational learning.
- **When to use:** Any business event that disrupts normal operations: system outages, data breaches, product safety issues, supply chain failures, major client escalations, regulatory actions, or media crises. The protocol should be pre-established and practiced before a crisis occurs — learning it during a crisis is too late.
- **Unique value:** The only business crisis protocol that adapts the proven Incident Command System to corporate contexts. While many crisis frameworks describe what to communicate, this one structures how to organize the response team and decision-making authority — which is where most organizational crisis responses actually fail.
- **Limits:** Requires pre-established roles and practice to execute effectively under pressure. Small organizations may not have enough people to fill all roles. The hierarchical command structure may conflict with collaborative organizational cultures. Not all business crises fit the 'incident' model — slow-moving strategic crises (market shifts, talent exodus) require different frameworks.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in incident command system (ICS) theory developed from wildfire management and extended to organizational crisis management. Draws on high-reliability organization research (Weick, Rochlin) and organizational behavior under stress. Reflects research showing clear command structures reduce coordination failures that amplify crisis severity.

### Career Bridges Strategy · #1782 · `llm-high` · career · INSTRUCTION
- **What it is:** Career bridges are deliberate, time-limited activities connecting your current position to your desired future career, specifically addressing the credibility gap: you want to move to X but have no experience in X. Bridge strategies include: (1) volunteer projects building a portfolio in the target domain, (2) consulting engagements practicing in the new field while maintaining income, (3) teaching or writing about the target domain (forcing deep learning and building credibility simultaneously), (4) board or advisory roles providing exposure and credentials, (5) strategic education providing both learning and signaling. Each bridge delivers capability building and visible credentialing that makes your transition legible to others.
- **How it helps:** For knowledge workers facing a credibility gap: instead of hoping someone takes a chance on you, systematically build bridges. Practically: (1) identify the specific gap between your current profile and target role, (2) choose 2-3 bridge activities addressing both capability and credibility, (3) commit 10-15 hours per week for 6-18 months, (4) document and publicize bridge activities. The model is powerful for people transitioning between industries where direct experience transfer isn't obvious. Each bridge makes the final leap shorter.
- **When to use:** When you want to transition to a field where you have no formal experience, when your resume doesn't reflect developing capabilities, when the gap between current and desired career feels too large for a single leap, or when you need both skills and credentials.
- **Unique value:** Specifically addresses the credibility gap making transitions difficult—the 'no experience, no opportunity, no experience' loop. Provides five concrete bridge types and distinguishes between capability building and credentialing, recognizing transitions require both.
- **Limits:** Bridge activities require discretionary time and energy not everyone has. Some transitions face structural barriers (licensing, mandatory degrees) bridges can't overcome. Bridge activities can become procrastination—avoiding the scary final leap. Works best for knowledge work transitions.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in career counseling, adult learning theory (Knowles's andragogy), and credentialing sociology. Draws from Ibarra's identity transition research, the credential signaling theory (Spence), competency-based education research, and Kolb's experiential learning cycle. Influenced by apprenticeship and legitimate peripheral participation theory (Lave & Wenger).

### Job Crafting (Wrzesniewski) · #1785 · `llm-high` · career · INSTRUCTION
- **What it is:** Amy Wrzesniewski's research shows employees actively reshape their jobs through three dimensions: (1) task crafting—changing what you do, taking on new tasks or dropping others; (2) relational crafting—changing who you interact with and how; (3) cognitive crafting—reframing the meaning of your work without changing the work itself. Hospital janitors who crafted their jobs as 'helping patients heal' and proactively interacted with patients reported significantly higher satisfaction and performance. The model shows meaningful work is partly constructed by the worker, not solely determined by the job description.
- **How it helps:** This empowers anyone stuck in a job they can't immediately leave. Instead of waiting for a new role, start crafting your current one. Practically: (1) task craft—volunteer for projects aligned with your interests, minimize draining tasks; (2) relational craft—build relationships with colleagues in departments that interest you; (3) cognitive craft—rewrite your internal job description emphasizing purpose. For managers, understanding job crafting helps create conditions where employees craft rather than enforcing rigid roles.
- **When to use:** When feeling bored or disengaged but unable to leave your current role, when managing employees who seem checked out, when trying to increase meaning in routine work, or when a job change isn't feasible for financial or personal reasons.
- **Unique value:** Identifies three specific, actionable levers for improving work experience without changing jobs. Based on rigorous organizational psychology, it shifts agency from employer to employee and shows that job satisfaction is partly self-created.
- **Limits:** Some roles genuinely have no room for customization (assembly line, strict protocols). Job crafting can create conflict when employees reshape roles in ways conflicting with organizational needs. Cognitive crafting risks self-deception if the work is genuinely harmful or pointless.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in organizational psychology, positive organizational behavior, and meaning-making research. Draws from Hackman and Oldham's job characteristics model, Wrzesniewski's calling research, self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan), role theory (Biddle), and Bakker's job demands-resources model. Influenced by proactive behavior research.

### Intrapreneurship Path · #1795 · `llm-high` · career · INSTRUCTION
- **What it is:** Intrapreneurship develops entrepreneurial ventures within existing organizations, leveraging corporate resources while managing innovation risk. The path follows a pattern: (1) identifying an opportunity the organization ignores, (2) building a proof of concept using discretionary time ('bootlegging'), (3) finding an executive sponsor to shield the project from corporate antibodies, (4) securing formal resources after demonstrating traction, (5) scaling while navigating politics. Successful intrapreneurs at 3M (Post-it Notes), Google (Gmail), and Sony (PlayStation) followed this pattern. The advantage over entrepreneurship: existing resources and safety net. The disadvantage: organizational resistance and less equity upside.
- **How it helps:** For knowledge workers with entrepreneurial instincts but risk aversion: intrapreneurship lets you innovate with a safety net. Practically: (1) identify problems your organization ignores, (2) build a prototype on your own time, (3) find a senior sponsor before going public, (4) frame the venture as organizational benefit, not personal ambition, (5) track metrics obsessively to build the case. The model also helps organizations: intrapreneurship programs retain innovative talent who would otherwise leave to compete.
- **When to use:** When you have an entrepreneurial idea but don't want to leave your employer, when your organization ignores an opportunity you can address, when wanting to test entrepreneurial skills in lower-risk settings, or when designing corporate innovation programs.
- **Unique value:** Provides a five-stage playbook for innovating within existing organizations, including the critical but overlooked step of finding executive sponsorship. Bridges the gap between employee and entrepreneur identities.
- **Limits:** Many organizations suppress intrapreneurship despite claiming to support it. Corporate antibodies can kill projects regardless of merit. IP issues mean you may not own what you create. Success often depends more on political skill than innovation quality.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in entrepreneurship theory (Schumpeter, Drucker), organizational behavior, and innovation management. Draws on resource dependency theory, corporate entrepreneurship literature (Pinchot), and principal-agent theory. Related to ambidexterity research and internal corporate venturing frameworks.

### Networking Conversation Framework (FORD Method) · #2341 · `high` · career · INSTRUCTION
- **What it is:** A four-topic conversation framework for building genuine professional connections without the awkwardness and inauthenticity that plague most 'networking.' FORD provides four safe, engaging topic areas: F — Family: 'Do you have kids? Where did you grow up? How's your family handling the move?' O — Occupation: 'What are you working on right now that excites you? How did you get into your field?' R — Recreation: 'What do you do for fun? Read anything good lately? Planning any trips?' D — Dreams: 'What's next for you? What's your big goal for this year?' The protocol: (1) Open with genuine curiosity, not a pitch. Ask a question from any FORD category. (2) Listen actively and follow up on what interests you genuinely. (3) Share reciprocally — networking is conversation, not interrogation. (4) Find the 'give': Before asking for anything, offer something — a connection, a resource, an idea. (5) Follow up within 48 hours: a brief message referencing something specific from your conversation. The key principle: Networking isn't about collecting contacts — it's about creating genuine connections that may eventually become professionally useful.
- **How it helps:** Eliminates the 'What do I even say?' paralysis that prevents most people from networking. The FORD topics provide a reliable conversation structure that works in any professional or social context. The 'find the give' step transforms networking from extractive (what can you do for me?) to generous (how can I help you?) — which, paradoxically, is far more effective at building the relationships that eventually produce professional opportunities. Immediately usable at your next event.
- **When to use:** At conferences, industry events, meetups, and professional gatherings. When meeting new colleagues, clients, or collaborators. During informational interviews. At social events where professional connections might emerge. When you want to deepen acquaintanceships into real professional relationships.
- **Unique value:** The only networking framework that provides specific conversational topics rather than abstract principles. While other frameworks say 'be genuine' and 'add value,' FORD tells you exactly what to talk about, making it immediately actionable even for introverts or networking novices.
- **Limits:** Can feel formulaic if you march through FORD like a checklist rather than following natural conversation flow. Cultural contexts vary — some topics (family, dreams) may be too personal for first conversations in certain cultures. The framework addresses conversation but not the strategic question of who to connect with. Doesn't help with maintaining large networks over time.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in social network theory (Granovetter's weak ties research) showing network diversity predicts career outcomes. FORD draws on conversation analysis and reciprocal self-disclosure research (Altman & Taylor's social penetration theory). Structured conversation protocols reduce social anxiety by providing cognitive scaffolding for interaction.

### Job Search Campaign Protocol · #2347 · `high` · career · INSTRUCTION
- **What it is:** A structured protocol for running a job search as a strategic campaign rather than a reactive, ad hoc process. Step 1 — Define targets: Identify 15-20 target companies based on industry, culture, growth stage, and role availability. Create a spreadsheet tracking each. Step 2 — Activate your network: Tell 20+ trusted contacts you're searching. Be specific: 'I'm looking for a [role] at a [company type] doing [specific work].' Specific requests get 5x more referrals than 'Let me know if you hear of anything.' Step 3 — Warm introductions: For each target company, identify who in your network can introduce you. Referral hires get interviews at 10x the rate of cold applicants. Step 4 — Direct outreach: For target companies where you lack connections, reach out to the hiring manager (not HR) with a brief, specific message showing you've researched the company and can solve a problem they have. Step 5 — Application rhythm: Apply to 5-10 positions per week, but invest 80% of search time in networking and warm outreach, 20% in applications. Step 6 — Interview preparation: For each interview, prepare 5 stories using the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) that demonstrate the key skills for the role. Step 7 — Track everything: Update your spreadsheet weekly. Follow up on every application within 1 week.
- **How it helps:** Prevents the two most common job search failures: (1) applying to hundreds of jobs online and hoping for the best (this produces a response rate under 2%) and (2) waiting passively for opportunities to appear. The 80/20 networking split reflects the research reality: 70-80% of jobs are filled through networking, not online applications. The target company approach gives the search strategic direction rather than a scatter-shot feel. The spreadsheet tracking creates accountability and prevents opportunities from falling through cracks.
- **When to use:** When actively job searching. When considering a job change and wanting to explore the market. When relocating to a new city. When an industry shift makes your current role unstable. When returning to work after a career break.
- **Unique value:** The only job search protocol that explicitly inverts the common time allocation — 80% networking, 20% applications — reflecting how jobs are actually filled rather than how most people search for them.
- **Limits:** Networking-heavy approaches favor people with strong existing networks, creating inequity for those new to an industry or geography. The 15-20 target companies approach works best for experienced professionals — entry-level candidates may need a broader search. Job markets vary by industry — some fields are heavily online (tech) while others are relationship-driven. The campaign approach requires significant time investment, which may conflict with a current demanding job.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in labor economics and social network theory (Granovetter) showing most jobs are obtained through weak ties. Draws on search theory (Stigler) — systematic search outperforms sequential evaluation. Reflects research on job search intensity and the behavioral economics of job market matching as a bilateral search process.

### Decision Hygiene · #732 · `llm-high` · cognitive · INSTRUCTION
- **What it is:** Kahneman's framework for reducing noise (unwanted variability) in judgment through procedural discipline—the decision-making equivalent of surgical hygiene. Just as surgeons don't rely on individual skill to prevent infection (they follow sterilization protocols), organizations shouldn't rely on individual judgment quality for important decisions—they should follow structured decision protocols. Decision hygiene practices: independent evaluation before group discussion, structured criteria applied systematically, reference class forecasting, and aggregation of multiple independent judgments.
- **How it helps:** Implement decision hygiene for all important, repeatable judgments. The core practices: (1) Before group discussion, have each person evaluate independently (prevents anchoring and groupthink). (2) Use structured evaluation criteria rather than holistic judgment. (3) Use reference classes ('how did similar situations turn out?') rather than inside-view prediction. (4) Aggregate multiple independent judgments rather than relying on one expert. (5) Delay intuitive judgment until structured analysis is complete. These practices don't require special skill—they're procedures anyone can follow.
- **When to use:** When important decisions are made by different people with inconsistent outcomes; when hiring, pricing, diagnosis, or evaluation processes need to be more reliable; when you suspect judgment variability but haven't addressed it structurally; when designing decision processes for organizations.
- **Unique value:** Shifts decision improvement from 'get smarter people' to 'implement better procedures.' Decision hygiene works even with average decision-makers because it addresses the structural sources of noise. The surgical hygiene analogy is precise: individual brilliance doesn't prevent infection—protocol does. Individual brilliance doesn't prevent noise—decision hygiene does.
- **Limits:** Structured procedures can feel bureaucratic and slow. Some decisions genuinely benefit from holistic, intuitive judgment. Over-structuring can prevent novel insights that don't fit the template. Decision hygiene reduces noise but doesn't address bias. Implementation requires organizational discipline that's hard to maintain.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in Kahneman, Sibony, and Sunstein's noise research (2021) and behavioral economics' debiasing literature. Draws on statistical decision theory, clinical prediction rules research, and organizational behavior on structured analytic procedures. Opposes ad hoc intuitive judgment.

### Decision Fatigue Reduction Strategies · #1433 · `llm-high` · cognitive · INSTRUCTION
- **What it is:** Decision fatigue is the deterioration of decision quality after a long session of decision-making, documented in Shai Danziger's famous study showing that Israeli judges granted parole at 65% in morning sessions but dropped to nearly 0% just before breaks, rebounding to 65% after eating. Each decision — no matter how trivial — consumes cognitive resources from the same pool used for consequential decisions. The implication is stark: the person who chooses between 50 email responses before a strategic meeting is making that strategic decision with a depleted cognitive budget. Reduction strategies include: (1) Decision elimination (automate or standardize routine choices — Obama's gray suits, Zuckerberg's gray t-shirts), (2) Decision batching (group similar decisions into a single session), (3) Decision scheduling (place high-stakes decisions in your peak cognitive window), (4) Decision precommitment (make decisions in advance when you're fresh — tomorrow's clothes, weekly meal plans, pre-set if-then rules for routine situations), and (5) Decision delegation (identify decisions where your input doesn't meaningfully improve the outcome and delegate them).
- **How it helps:** Knowledge workers make an estimated 35,000 decisions per day, most of them trivial. The intervention is to reduce that number dramatically. Audit your daily decisions: which ones don't meaningfully improve with your personal judgment? Automate those (subscription services, standing orders, template responses, default calendar blocks). Which ones could be made in advance? Batch those into a weekly planning session. Which ones are high-stakes? Protect those by scheduling them first and arriving fresh. A CEO who eliminates wardrobe decisions, lunch decisions, and commute route decisions saves cognitive resources for decisions that actually matter.
- **When to use:** When end-of-day decisions are consistently worse than morning decisions. When trivial choices consume disproportionate mental energy. When designing personal systems for productivity. When decision quality matters and you want to maximize it by reducing quantity.
- **Unique value:** Provides specific, actionable strategies for reducing the number of decisions rather than the vague advice to 'make better decisions.' The five reduction strategies (eliminate, batch, schedule, precommit, delegate) each address a different type of unnecessary decision load.
- **Limits:** Not all decisions can be eliminated or automated without losing beneficial variation. Over-automating life can create rigidity and reduce serendipity. ### Nonviolent Communication (Rosenberg): Observation, Feeling, Need, Request · #133 · `llm-high` · communication · INSTRUCTION
- **What it is:** Marshall Rosenberg's communication framework designed to express needs and resolve conflicts without blame, criticism, or coercion. The four-step structure: (1) Observation: state what happened factually without evaluation ('When you arrived 30 minutes after we agreed'—not 'when you were late again'). (2) Feeling: name the emotion it triggered ('I felt frustrated'). (3) Need: identify the underlying need ('because I need reliability in our plans'). (4) Request: make a specific, actionable, positive request ('would you be willing to text me if you're going to be more than 10 minutes late?'). NVC's power comes from separating OBSERVATION from EVALUATION and connecting FEELINGS to NEEDS rather than to blame.
- **How it helps:** Practice each step separately before combining them. The most difficult steps: (1) Observation without evaluation—'you're always late' is evaluation; 'you arrived at 7:30 when we agreed on 7:00' is observation. (2) Connecting feelings to needs rather than to the other person's actions—'I feel frustrated because I need reliability' rather than 'you make me frustrated.' The complete formula: 'When [observation], I feel [feeling], because I need [need]. Would you be willing to [request]?' For leaders: NVC transforms feedback from accusation to connection—addressing the issue without attacking the person.
- **When to use:** When communication defaults to blame, criticism, or demand; when conflicts need de-escalation through needs-based rather than blame-based dialogue; when feedback needs to address behavior without attacking character; when the connection between feelings and underlying needs would transform defensive conversations into collaborative ones.
- **Unique value:** Provides the specific linguistic structure that converts blame into connection: by routing communication through NEEDS rather than JUDGMENTS, NVC transforms adversarial exchanges into collaborative problem-solving. The observation/evaluation distinction alone eliminates most conversational defensiveness.
- **Limits:** NVC can feel formulaic and unnatural when first practiced. Some situations require directness that NVC's structure may slow down. The framework assumes both parties are willing to engage. Cultural context affects whether NVC's style is appropriate. Some critics argue NVC can be used manipulatively.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in humanistic psychology (Rogers' person-centered therapy) and needs theory (Maslow). Draws on conflict resolution research, empathic listening traditions, and Gandhi's nonviolent philosophy adapted to interpersonal communication.

### Double-Entry Bookkeeping · #187 · `llm-high` · economics · INSTRUCTION
- **What it is:** The accounting system invented in medieval Italy (codified by Luca Pacioli) where every financial transaction is recorded as TWO entries: a debit and a credit. Assets = Liabilities + Equity. Every transaction must balance. This system provides: automatic error detection (if debits don't equal credits, something is wrong), comprehensive financial visibility (both sides of every transaction are captured), and fraud prevention (harder to hide transactions when both sides must balance). As a mental model: every action has TWO sides, and accounting for both sides produces a more complete and accurate picture than tracking only one side. Costs have hidden benefits; benefits have hidden costs.
- **How it helps:** Apply double-entry thinking to any analysis: for every apparent benefit, ask 'what's the offsetting cost?' and for every apparent cost, ask 'what's the offsetting benefit?' In business decisions: a cost reduction is also a capability reduction (both sides of the entry). A revenue increase requires a resource investment (both sides). In personal decisions: every 'yes' is simultaneously a 'no' to something else. The practice: before concluding that something is purely positive or purely negative, find the other entry—the offsetting entry that completes the picture.
- **When to use:** When financial analysis needs the completeness and error-checking of double-entry; when decisions are being evaluated on only one side (costs without benefits, or benefits without costs); when understanding that every action has an offsetting entry would improve analysis; when fraud prevention or error detection requires systematic cross-referencing.
- **Unique value:** Provides both the practical accounting system that enabled modern capitalism AND the mental model that every transaction has two sides. The error-detection property (debits must equal credits) is the most powerful feature: it AUTOMATICALLY reveals mistakes and omissions that single-entry systems miss.
- **Limits:** Double-entry accounting doesn't capture non-financial values. The system requires training and discipline. Some transactions resist clean categorization. Financial statements still require interpretation. The analogy to non-financial 'double entry' is metaphorical.
- **Foundations:** Developed in Renaissance accounting (Pacioli, 1494); rooted in mathematical duality and transaction theory. Connects to systems thinking's conservation principles, financial accounting theory, and information theory's requirement for internal consistency checks.

### After-Action Review Protocol (AAR) · #2310 · `high` · organizational · INSTRUCTION
- **What it is:** A structured team learning protocol developed by the U.S. Army and adapted for business contexts, conducted immediately after any significant event, project, or milestone. - **How it helps:** The most proven organizational learning protocol in existence, battle-tested across military and business contexts for decades. The 24-48 hour window captures lessons while memory is accurate. Organizations that conduct AARs consistently report significantly fewer repeated mistakes and faster adaptation to changing conditions.
- **When to use:** After any significant event: project completions, product launches, incidents, crises, organizational changes, failed initiatives, and notable successes. After negotiations, presentations, or sales pitches. Weekly during long-running projects. The lower the threshold for triggering an AAR, the faster the team learns.

This seemingly simple structural choice eliminates hindsight bias — the cognitive distortion that makes every review less useful by rewriting what people 'expected' to match what actually happened.
- **Limits:** Requires psychological safety — in blame-oriented cultures, people will not share honestly. The 'no-blame' rule can be hard to maintain when failures had real consequences. - **Foundations:** Developed from U.S. Army Center for Army Lessons Learned. Rooted in organizational learning theory (Argyris, Schon) and action research. ### Pomodoro Technique Protocol · #2315 · `high` · productivity · INSTRUCTION

The Pomodoro count provides an honest measure of actual focus time, which is almost always far less than people estimate.
- **When to use:** When you need to focus on deep work and keep getting distracted. When procrastinating on a specific task. When you need to estimate how long tasks actually take. When your workday feels long but unproductive. When working from home where distractions abound. NOT for meetings, collaborative work, or creative sessions that need open-ended time.
- **Unique value:** The only focus protocol that combines time-boxing, interruption management, AND honest productivity tracking into a single integrated system. The tracking component distinguishes it from simple timer-based approaches — it builds self-knowledge about your actual work patterns.

External interruptions (colleagues, calls) can make consistent Pomodoros impossible in open offices. The technique addresses focus but not prioritization — you can Pomodoro efficiently on the wrong tasks.
- **Foundations:** Developed by Francesco Cirillo, rooted in time management research and behavioral psychology. The middle—moderate risk with moderate return—is the worst of both worlds: insufficient safety for protection and insufficient upside for growth. The barbell ensures survival (the safe side) while maintaining exposure to positive Black Swans (the risky side).

- **When to use:** When operating in Extremistan environments where outlier outcomes dominate; when you need both survival assurance and growth exposure; when moderate strategies are producing moderate results with moderate risk (the dangerous middle); when designing portfolios of any kind—investments, products, projects, experiments.
- **Unique value:** Provides a specific structural alternative to the standard 'diversified moderate risk' approach. Barbell structure protects against ruin while preserving access to outsized gains—something balanced diversification cannot do.
- **Limits:** The barbell requires discipline to maintain—the temptation to move resources from the safe side to the risky side undermines the strategy. Identifying 'truly safe' and 'uncapped upside' positions is harder in practice than in theory. In Mediocristan domains, conventional diversification may outperform the barbell. ### Algorithmic Feed Management · #1762 · `llm-high` · technology · INSTRUCTION
- **What it is:** Algorithmic feed management deliberately curates your algorithmic inputs to shape what platforms show you. Every click, pause, like, and scroll speed trains the algorithm. You can 'train' it intentionally: engage with high-quality content you want more of, ignore content you want less of (don't hate-click), use 'not interested' features aggressively, follow sources improving your feed, and unfollow those degrading it. The key insight: algorithms respond to revealed preferences (what you click), not stated preferences (what you say you want). Your YouTube recommendations reflect actual viewing, not aspirations. Managing this gap is the core skill.
- **How it helps:** Spend 30 minutes auditing: unfollow outrage-without-insight accounts, mute wasteful topics, follow learning-oriented accounts, and like content representing what you want more of. On YouTube, create educational playlists to train recommendations. On Twitter/X, use lists instead of the algorithmic timeline. The meta-principle: every interaction votes 'show me more like this,' so vote intentionally.
- **When to use:** When social media feeds feel low-quality, outrage-driven, or irrelevant. When wanting to turn algorithmic platforms into learning tools rather than attention traps. When algorithm-served content is affecting your mood or worldview negatively. When seeking to optimize information intake without abandoning platforms entirely.
- **Unique value:** Shifts the relationship with algorithmic platforms from passive consumption to active curation, leveraging the same behavioral learning mechanisms that platforms use to capture attention, but redirecting them toward your own goals rather than the platform's engagement metrics.
- **Limits:** Algorithms change constantly, making specific tactics perishable. Even curated feeds still optimize for engagement over genuine value. Active management requires ongoing effort—feeds degrade without maintenance. The 'revealed preference' problem means accidental clicks and curiosity browsing can derail intentional curation.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in machine learning theory, persuasive technology design, and behavioral psychology. Draws from recommender system research, reinforcement learning theory (operant conditioning in algorithm form), Fogg's behavior design principles, and Cialdini's influence research applied to platform engagement optimization.

### Digital Legacy Planning · #1767 · `llm-high` · technology · INSTRUCTION
- **What it is:** Digital legacy planning manages your digital presence, accounts, and data after death or incapacitation. The average person has 100+ accounts containing financial assets, valuable data, ongoing obligations, and potential vulnerabilities—often inaccessible to survivors behind unknown passwords. The framework: (1) Inventory all accounts and assets, (2) Designate digital executors, (3) Create secure access instructions via password manager with master password shared through sealed letter or attorney, (4) Set up platform legacy features (Google Inactive Account Manager, Facebook Legacy Contact, Apple Digital Legacy), (5) Document wishes per account, (6) Review annually.
- **How it helps:** Start with a password manager if you don't use one—it's both daily convenience and legacy tool. Add a trusted family member as emergency contact within it. Document cryptocurrency holdings separately (hardware wallet PINs, seed phrases are permanently unrecoverable without credentials). For entrepreneurs, digital legacy planning for business accounts is fiduciary responsibility—what happens to your company's social media, domains, and cloud infrastructure if you're unavailable?
- **When to use:** When you have digital assets that others would need to access in an emergency. When creating or updating estate planning documents. When setting up new important accounts (add them to the legacy plan). When transitioning business responsibilities.
- **Unique value:** Addresses the entirely neglected domain of post-death digital asset management, preventing the permanent loss of irreplaceable data, financial assets, and the ability to properly memorialize or close digital presences—a modern estate planning essential.
- **Limits:** Digital estate law varies by jurisdiction and is rapidly evolving. Some platform terms of service prohibit sharing login credentials even for estate purposes. The plan requires regular updates as accounts change. Security and accessibility are in tension—making accounts accessible for legacy purposes also makes them more vulnerable currently.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in estate law, clinical psychology of death and dying (Kübler-Ross), and digital identity research. Draws from Worden's grief tasks model, information systems research on data persistence, privacy law, and the emerging field of digital afterlife studies (Kasket). Influenced by legal traditions of fiduciary duty.
