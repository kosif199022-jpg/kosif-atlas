### Neuroception (Unconscious Threat Detection) · #240 · `llm-high` · behavioral · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Stephen Porges's concept from Polyvagal Theory describing the nervous system's unconscious, automatic scanning for safety and danger signals in the environment—occurring BELOW conscious awareness before any cognitive evaluation. Neuroception evaluates: facial expressions, tone of voice, body posture, movement patterns, and environmental cues to determine safety, danger, or life-threat—and triggers the appropriate nervous system state (social engagement for safety, fight/flight for danger, freeze/collapse for life-threat) BEFORE the conscious mind has registered the assessment. Neuroception explains why you feel uncomfortable around certain people before you can explain why, and why some environments feel safe or threatening without obvious reason.
- **How it helps:** Attend to your neuroceptive signals rather than overriding them with cognition. The practice: notice your body's response to people and environments—tension, relaxation, constriction, openness—as INFORMATION rather than irrational feelings. For leaders: understand that your team's neuroception of you matters as much as your words. If your body language, tone, and facial expressions signal threat (even unintentionally), no amount of verbally communicating safety will override the neuroception. For environment design: create spaces that signal safety to the nervous system (adequate lighting, clear sightlines, comfortable temperature, reduced noise).
- **When to use:** When physical discomfort in certain situations doesn't have a cognitive explanation; when leadership presence needs to signal safety to others' nervous systems; when environmental design needs to account for unconscious threat detection; when understanding why 'I don't know why but this doesn't feel right' is valid information.
- **Unique value:** Identifies the unconscious, pre-cognitive mechanism by which the nervous system evaluates safety—explaining why body-level reactions often precede and override cognitive assessment. This validates 'gut feelings' as genuine information from a sophisticated threat-detection system rather than irrational emotion.
- **Limits:** Neuroception can be miscalibrated (trauma produces false threat detection). Not all body signals are accurate assessments of actual danger. Overreliance on neuroception can produce avoidance of safe situations. The science of Polyvagal Theory is debated in some aspects. Cultural context shapes what signals safety vs. threat.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in Stephen Porges' Polyvagal Theory and autonomic nervous system neuroscience. Draws on evolutionary biology (threat detection circuits), affective neuroscience, and trauma research. Connected to LeDoux's fear conditioning work and Damasio's research on body-based emotion processing.

### Emotional Bank Account (Covey/Gottman) · #372 · `llm-high` · behavioral · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Stephen Covey's metaphor (paralleled by Gottman's research on positive-to-negative interaction ratios) for the accumulated trust and goodwill in a relationship. Every positive interaction (kindness, follow-through, appreciation, listening) is a DEPOSIT that builds the balance; every negative interaction (criticism, broken promises, dismissiveness) is a WITHDRAWAL that depletes it. Gottman's research quantifies it: stable relationships maintain a 5:1 ratio of positive to negative interactions. At 5:1, the relationship has a high balance that can absorb occasional withdrawals; below 1:1, the relationship is in 'debt' and every interaction feels threatening.
- **How it helps:** Actively manage the deposit-to-withdrawal ratio in your most important relationships. The practices: (1) Make small deposits consistently (appreciation, listening, small acts of kindness)—frequency matters more than magnitude. (2) Minimize unnecessary withdrawals (criticism, defensiveness, contempt). (3) Track the ratio intuitively: does this relationship feel like it has a positive or negative balance? (4) After unavoidable withdrawals (difficult conversations, disagreements): make intentional deposits to restore the balance. In leadership: the emotional bank account with your team determines how much change, feedback, and challenge they can absorb.
- **When to use:** When relationships feel fragile despite no single major breach; when leadership capital is needed for difficult changes; when understanding why some relationships absorb conflict well and others don't; when the deposit-to-withdrawal ratio has been neglected.
- **Unique value:** Converts abstract 'relationship health' into a quantifiable ratio (5:1 positive to negative) with concrete deposit and withdrawal behaviors. The metaphor makes relationship management as concrete as financial management: track the balance, make regular deposits, minimize unnecessary withdrawals, and don't overdraw.
- **Limits:** Not all deposits are equal (a betrayal can't be offset by five nice gestures). The 5:1 ratio is an average, not a universal rule. Some positive interactions can feel manipulative if the motive is transparent. The banking metaphor can make relationships feel transactional. Quality of interaction matters as much as quantity.
- **Foundations:** Synthesizes Covey's trust-building framework with Gottman's relational research. Rooted in social exchange theory (Homans, Blau), reciprocity norms research (Cialdini), and organizational psychology on psychological safety. Draws on trust literature (Mayer et al.) and behavioral economics on reciprocal altruism.

### Repair Attempt Theory · #1412 · `llm-high` · behavioral · CLAIM
- **What it is:** John Gottman's research identified 'repair attempts' as the single most important factor predicting relationship success — more important than the severity or frequency of conflicts. A repair attempt is any statement or action — serious or silly — that prevents negativity from escalating out of control. Examples range from humor ('Can we start over? I didn't mean to launch a missile'), to metacommunication ('I think we're getting off track'), to vulnerability ('I'm feeling defensive and I don't want to be'), to physical affection (touching a partner's hand during an argument). What matters isn't the sophistication of the repair attempt but whether it's received. In healthy relationships, even clumsy repair attempts are accepted; in distressed relationships, even skillful attempts are rejected. Gottman found that the ability to make and receive repair attempts accounts for the difference between 'master' and 'disaster' relationships. The mechanism is de-escalation before physiological flooding makes productive dialogue impossible.
- **How it helps:** In professional settings, practice making explicit repair attempts when conversations start going sideways: 'I think I'm not saying this well, let me try again,' 'I can feel this getting tense — can we take a step back?' 'I think we're actually on the same side here.' The key for knowledge workers is lowering the bar: repair attempts don't need to be perfect or eloquent. A simple 'Wait, that came out wrong' is a repair attempt. Build a repertoire of go-to repair phrases and practice using them early in escalation cycles, before flooding makes them impossible.
- **When to use:** When you notice a conversation beginning to escalate. When you've said something you immediately regret. When a colleague's body language signals withdrawal or anger. When teaching conflict skills — repair attempts are the highest-leverage intervention because they prevent escalation.
- **Unique value:** Identifies the single highest-leverage behavior in conflict resolution: the early repair attempt. While most conflict models focus on what to do during or after conflict, this model focuses on the critical moment where escalation can still be prevented.
- **Limits:** Repair attempts must be received to work — in deeply damaged relationships, attempts are rejected regardless of skill. Power dynamics affect whose repair attempts are taken seriously. Chronic repair without addressing root causes can become avoidance. Cultural variation in acceptable repair behaviors.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in Gottman's couples research and the interpersonal process research tradition. Draws on attachment theory, cognitive appraisal theory of emotion (Lazarus), and the social psychology of forgiveness (McCullough). Repair attempts are coded behavioral sequences that interrupt negative interaction cycles in couple conflict.

### Attachment Theory in Sexuality · #1514 · `llm-high` · behavioral · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Attachment theory, applied to sexuality, reveals how early attachment patterns shape adult sexual behavior, desire, and satisfaction. Securely attached individuals tend toward comfortable, playful, mutually responsive sexual interactions. Anxiously attached individuals often use sex for reassurance, fear rejection if they don't perform, and may confuse sexual intensity with emotional connection. Avoidantly attached individuals may enjoy sex without emotional engagement, use physical distance during intimacy, or suppress desire as a way of maintaining independence. Disorganized attachment produces the most conflicted sexuality—simultaneously craving and fearing intimate contact. Research by Deborah Davis and others shows attachment style predicts sexual satisfaction, orgasm frequency, communication about sex, response to desire discrepancy, and recovery from sexual problems.
- **How it helps:** For knowledge workers who apply analytical frameworks to personal life, this model provides a specific mechanism for understanding sexual dynamics. Use it to: (1) identify your attachment pattern's specific impact on your sexual relationship—not just general relationship dynamics, (2) understand that sexual problems may be attachment problems expressing through the body, (3) recognize that creating safety and secure attachment is a precondition for satisfying sexuality, not a separate issue, (4) distinguish between sexual chemistry (often anxious-attachment intensity) and sustainable sexual satisfaction (usually associated with secure attachment), (5) work on attachment security as the most.
- **When to use:** When sexual problems seem connected to broader relationship dynamics. When intensity is being confused with genuine intimacy. When one partner uses sex for reassurance while the other uses it for distance. When building a foundation for sexual satisfaction in a new or existing relationship.
- **Unique value:** Connects the well-established attachment framework to sexuality specifically, providing a root-cause analysis for many sexual difficulties. Explains why sexual chemistry with anxiously-attached partners can feel intoxicating but ultimately unsatisfying. Identifies secure attachment creation as the most effective sexual intervention.
- **Limits:** Attachment style is not destiny—people can earn secure attachment through relationships and therapy. The model can pathologize anxious or avoidant sexual expression rather than just describing it. Not all sexual issues have attachment origins—medical, hormonal, and situational factors also matter. The four-category framework oversimplifies the continuous nature of attachment variation.
- **Foundations:** Founded on Bowlby and Ainsworth's attachment theory extended to adult sexuality by Shaver, Hazan, and later Diamond. Draws on psychodynamic object relations theory, developmental neuroscience, and clinical research on how early attachment patterns shape adult sexual intimacy.

### Dopamine Reward Prediction Error · #1737 · `llm-high` · behavioral · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Wolfram Schultz discovered that dopamine signals the difference between expected and actual rewards, not pleasure itself. Better-than-expected rewards produce dopamine spikes (strengthening the behavior). Expected rewards produce flat dopamine. Worse-than-expected rewards produce dopamine dips (weakening the behavior). This explains why novelty motivates (new rewards exceed zero expectation), habits feel less rewarding over time (expectation matches reality), and variable rewards are more compelling than fixed ones (unpredictability maintains prediction error). Drugs of abuse hijack this system with supraphysiological surges that overwhelm natural reward calibration.
- **How it helps:** For sustained work engagement, introduce variability: rotate project types, change locations, vary routines to maintain prediction errors. For habit building, front-load novelty early, then shift to identity-based maintenance. For entrepreneurs, this explains why gamification works (variable rewards) and why subscriptions face churn (expected rewards flatten dopamine). For breaking compulsive tech habits, recognize that variable notifications exploit your prediction error system—making them predictable reduces their pull.
- **When to use:** When trying to understand why initially exciting activities become boring. When designing engagement and motivation systems. When analyzing why some products create compulsive use. When building sustainable motivation rather than relying on novelty.
- **Unique value:** Reveals that dopamine drives learning and anticipation rather than pleasure, explaining the counterintuitive neuroscience of why we pursue things that no longer make us happy and why unpredictability is more motivating than reliability.
- **Limits:** Reducing complex motivation to dopamine oversimplifies the multi-neurotransmitter reality. The prediction error model is best established for simple rewards—complex human motivations involve additional systems. Individual dopamine system sensitivity varies genetically and experientially. Pop-neuroscience 'dopamine hacks' often distort the actual science.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in computational neuroscience, learning theory, and addiction neurobiology. Draws from Schultz's electrophysiological dopamine recordings, Rescorla-Wagner computational learning model, temporal difference learning algorithms (Sutton, Barto), and Berridge's distinction between wanting (incentive salience) and liking.

### Edge Effects (Boundaries Create Unique Conditions) · #8 · `llm-high` · biology · CLAIM
- **What it is:** In ecology, the phenomenon where the boundary between two ecosystems (forest and meadow, land and water) produces unique conditions that support species and interactions found in neither ecosystem alone—often with greater biodiversity and productivity than either adjacent zone. As a mental model: the most interesting, innovative, and productive work happens at the boundaries between disciplines, departments, cultures, or frameworks. The edge between biology and computing produced bioinformatics. The edge between psychology and economics produced behavioral economics. Innovation lives at the edges, not in the centers.
- **How it helps:** Deliberately position yourself and your teams at disciplinary, organizational, or cultural edges. Hire people who bridge two domains. Create roles that sit between departments. Attend conferences outside your field. Read books from adjacent disciplines. The edge effect predicts that the most valuable innovations will emerge from these boundary zones—not from deeper specialization within a single domain. For organizations: invest in boundary-spanning roles and cross-functional teams, because the edge is where recombination produces novelty.
- **When to use:** When innovation has stalled within a domain (look at the edges instead); when organizational silos are preventing the cross-pollination that produces new ideas; when hiring and need people who bridge multiple domains; when designing teams for maximum creative output.
- **Unique value:** Provides the ecological basis for interdisciplinary innovation—explaining WHY boundary zones are more productive, not just observing that they are. The mechanism: edges combine elements from both adjacent systems in novel configurations that neither system produces alone. This makes edge-seeking a deliberate innovation strategy rather than accidental serendipity.
- **Limits:** Not all edges are productive—some boundaries exist for good reasons (security, specialization, focus). Edge positions can be uncomfortable and professionally risky ('not belonging' to either domain). The model works better for innovation than for execution, which often benefits from focused specialization. Some deep domain expertise can only develop away from edges.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in landscape ecology and habitat fragmentation research. Draws on ecotone theory, species diversity gradient studies, and boundary layer physics. Informed by Wilson and MacArthur's island biogeography. Applied in organizational theory through boundary-spanning roles and innovation at disciplinary intersections.

### Invasive Species Dynamics · #9 · `llm-high` · biology · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The ecological pattern where species introduced to new environments without their natural predators, parasites, or competitors undergo explosive growth, outcompete native species, and fundamentally restructure the ecosystem. Invasive species succeed through: lack of natural checks, competitive advantages evolved in different contexts, and exploitation of niches that native species don't occupy. As a mental model: any agent, product, or practice introduced to a new environment without the constraints that limited it in its native environment can dominate and restructure the system. Uber disrupting taxi markets, digital news disrupting print media, and remote work disrupting office culture all exhibit invasive species dynamics.
- **How it helps:** When introducing anything new to an established system (new products, practices, people, technologies): assess whether it will behave like an invasive species. The diagnostic: does it have competitive advantages that the existing system can't counter? Are the natural constraints from its origin absent in the new environment? Is it exploiting an unoccupied niche? If yes to all three: expect explosive growth and ecosystem restructuring. For defenders: develop adaptive responses quickly—native species that evolve to compete with invaders survive; those that don't, don't. For attackers: understand that invasive success is often temporary—the ecosystem eventually adapts.
- **When to use:** When new entrants are disrupting established markets faster than incumbents can respond; when introduced technologies are restructuring workflows and organizations; when understanding why some innovations spread explosively in certain environments; when designing competitive responses to disruptive entrants.
- **Unique value:** Maps the specific ecological mechanism behind market disruption: competitive advantage without natural constraints produces explosive, ecosystem-restructuring growth. This provides a more precise and predictive framework than general 'disruption' theory—including the prediction that ecosystems eventually adapt to invaders.
- **Limits:** Not all new entrants behave like invasive species—many fail to establish. The ecological metaphor is approximate; businesses can intentionally adapt in ways species can't. Some 'invasive' disruptions are net positive for the ecosystem. The model better describes the pattern than predicts which specific entrants will invade successfully.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in community ecology, competition theory, and biogeography. Draws on Elton's foundational invasion ecology work (1958), propagule pressure theory, and enemy release hypothesis. Informed by evolutionary biology and disturbance ecology. Applied in business via disruption and market entry theory.

### Island Biogeography (Size and Isolation Determine Diversity) · #10 · `llm-high` · biology · CLAIM
- **What it is:** MacArthur and Wilson's ecological theory that the number of species on an island is determined by two factors: size (larger islands support more species) and isolation (more isolated islands have fewer species because colonization is harder). The equilibrium species count balances immigration rate (new species arriving) against extinction rate (existing species dying out). Applied as a mental model: any 'island'—a team, market, community, or organization—has diversity determined by its size (resources for supporting variety) and its isolation (barriers to new ideas, people, and influences entering). Diversity is a function of access, not just intention.
- **How it helps:** If you want more diversity in any system (ideas, perspectives, team composition): increase size and decrease isolation. For teams: larger teams with more outside connections naturally develop more cognitive diversity. For innovation: reduce the barriers between your organization and outside ideas (attend conferences, hire from diverse backgrounds, engage with adjacent industries). For markets: reducing barriers to entry increases competitive diversity. The model also explains homogeneity: small, isolated teams naturally converge to fewer perspectives—not through bias but through the same ecological dynamics that determine species counts on islands.
- **When to use:** When organizational diversity is lower than desired despite good intentions; when innovation has stalled in an isolated team; when understanding why some markets or communities have more variety than others; when designing environments that naturally produce and sustain diversity.
- **Unique value:** Provides the ecological mechanism explaining WHY isolation reduces diversity and connectivity increases it—turning diversity management from an intention ('we value diversity') into an architectural practice (reduce barriers, increase connections). Diversity isn't primarily about values; it's about the structural conditions that allow variety to enter and survive.
- **Limits:** The biological model doesn't map perfectly to organizations—people aren't species. Too much immigration can overwhelm existing culture. Some isolation is valuable for focus and coherence. Size has diminishing returns for diversity beyond a point. The model explains equilibrium diversity but not how to manage the transition.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in MacArthur and Wilson's equilibrium theory of island biogeography (1967). Draws on species-area relationships, metapopulation theory, and colonization-extinction dynamics. Informed by evolutionary biology and conservation science. Applied organizationally through network theory and knowledge cluster research.
- *Secondary roles:* PATTERN · *Collection:* risk-management|evolutionary-principle

### Signaling · #32 · `llm-high` · biology · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The biological and economic concept that actions, attributes, or investments serve as reliable indicators of hidden qualities—because they're costly enough that only entities with the genuine quality can afford them. A peacock's tail signals genetic fitness (only healthy birds can waste resources on it). A university degree signals intelligence and conscientiousness (only capable students can obtain one). Luxury goods signal wealth. Startup founders working 80-hour weeks signal commitment. Signals work because they're costly to fake—the cost is what makes them credible. When signals become cheap to fake, they lose their informational value.
- **How it helps:** Identify what you're signaling (intentionally and unintentionally) and what others are signaling to you. In hiring: degrees, past employers, and project portfolios are signals—but ask what they signal and how reliable each signal is. In business: quality guarantees, money-back promises, and premium pricing are signals of product confidence. In leadership: what you spend time on signals what you value more reliably than what you SAY you value. Design your signals deliberately: what costly-to-fake actions demonstrate the qualities you want to communicate?
- **When to use:** When evaluating others' claims (look for costly signals rather than cheap talk); when designing your own credibility signals (choose actions that are hard to fake); when information asymmetry exists and you need to communicate hidden qualities; when analyzing markets where signaling drives behavior (luxury goods, credentials, branding).
- **Unique value:** Provides the economic framework for why actions speak louder than words: costly signals are credible precisely because they're expensive to fake. This transforms vague advice ('show, don't tell') into a testable principle: a signal's credibility is proportional to its cost of fabrication.
- **Limits:** Not all costly signals are informative—some are just waste. Signal inflation (credential creep, spending escalation) can make previously reliable signals useless. Some genuine qualities are hard to signal (integrity, creativity). The theory explains equilibrium signaling but doesn't always predict which signals will emerge in practice.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in Zahavian handicap principle (1975) and costly signaling theory in evolutionary biology. Draws on Spence's signaling model in information economics (1973), game theory, and semiotics. Informed by Veblen's conspicuous consumption and social psychology of status. Bridges biology, economics, and communication theory.

### Simple Physiological Reward-Seeking · #33 · `llm-high` · biology · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The biological baseline of all motivation: organisms move toward reward signals (dopamine-mediated pleasure, comfort, safety) and away from pain signals (threat, discomfort, uncertainty). This is the operating system underneath all more complex motivational models—before purpose, meaning, and self-actualization, there's the dopaminergic reward system that shapes behavior at the most fundamental level. In modern environments: social media, junk food, gambling, and addictive products all hijack the simple reward-seeking system by delivering supernormal stimuli that the evolutionary system wasn't designed to handle.
- **How it helps:** Understand your reward-seeking system rather than fighting it. The practice: align your reward environment with your goals. If you want to read more: make reading physically accessible and phones physically inaccessible (proximity = reward accessibility). If you want to eat well: stock healthy food and remove unhealthy food from your environment. Don't rely on willpower to override reward-seeking—redesign the reward landscape so that the desired behavior IS the path of least resistance. For product design: understanding that users are fundamentally reward-seeking means reducing friction to the rewarding experience and adding friction to the non-rewarding alternatives.
- **When to use:** When behavior change efforts are fighting against rather than working with reward-seeking; when environmental design could redirect reward-seeking toward desired behaviors; when understanding addiction or habitual behaviors at the biological level; when product design needs to leverage rather than fight natural motivational systems.
- **Unique value:** Provides the biological foundation for all behavioral design: before complex motivation, there's simple reward-seeking. This means the most effective behavior change strategies work WITH the reward system (environmental design, habit stacking, immediate rewards) rather than against it (willpower, motivation, guilt).
- **Limits:** Humans are not merely reward-seekers—higher-order motivation (purpose, meaning, values) can override immediate reward-seeking. Over-optimizing for reward can produce addiction and hedonic adaptation. Environmental design has limits in complex social contexts. The model can justify manipulation through supernormal stimuli.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in Skinner's operant conditioning and behaviorism. Draws on dopaminergic reward pathway neuroscience (Schultz), drive reduction theory, and Maslow's physiological needs. Informed by addiction neuroscience and evolutionary psychology of hedonic motivation. Foundation for behavioral economics and habit formation research.

### Adaptation Rate and the Red Queen Effect · #34 · `llm-high` · biology · CLAIM
- **What it is:** In co-evolutionary environments, you must keep running (adapting) just to stay in place relative to competitors who are also adapting. Named after the Red Queen in Alice in Wonderland: 'It takes all the running you can do, to keep in the same place.' The rate of adaptation, not just the fact of it, determines survival. Falling behind the adaptation rate of your competitors or environment is a death sentence.
- **How it helps:** Explains why standing still equals falling behind in competitive markets. Helps calibrate the speed of innovation and adaptation required in your specific industry. Prevents complacency after success—your competitors are adapting too.
- **When to use:** When a previously winning strategy starts losing effectiveness. When evaluating competitive position over time. When deciding how much to invest in R&D and innovation vs. exploiting current advantages.
- **Unique value:** Shifts the competitive frame from absolute performance to relative adaptation speed. The question isn't 'are we improving?' but 'are we improving faster than our environment demands?'
- **Limits:** Endless adaptation is exhausting and can prevent deepening existing strengths. Not all environments change at Red Queen speeds—some are relatively stable. The model can create false urgency.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in Van Valen's Red Queen hypothesis in evolutionary biology (1973). Draws on coevolutionary arms race theory, predator-prey dynamics, and fitness landscape research. Informed by Ridley's popular synthesis. Applied in competitive strategy to describe environments where continuous adaptation is required merely to maintain relative position.

### Cargo cult · #39 · `llm-high` · business · CLAIM
- **What it is:** A phenomenon where people imitate the surface forms of a successful practice without understanding the underlying mechanisms that make it work. Named after Pacific Islanders who built replica airstrips hoping to attract cargo planes after WWII. Richard Feynman extended this to 'cargo cult science'—research that follows all the forms of scientific investigation but is missing the essential ingredient of intellectual honesty. In business, cargo culting manifests as copying competitors' visible tactics while missing the invisible strategy.
- **How it helps:** Directly applicable to building, launching, and scaling products and businesses. Helps prioritize actions, identify market opportunities, and build sustainable competitive advantages.
- **When to use:** When building or iterating on products; when evaluating market opportunities; when deciding resource allocation; when scaling operations.
- **Unique value:** Directly actionable for product and business decisions. 'Cargo cult' provides a specific framework that has been validated in real-world startup and enterprise contexts.
- **Limits:** Business models are context-dependent—what worked in one market may fail in another. Survivorship bias colors our understanding. Execution matters as much as strategy.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in anthropological studies of Melanesian cargo cult movements (post-WWII). Draws on Feynman's cargo cult science concept (1974), mimicry theory in organizational sociology, and DiMaggio and Powell's institutional isomorphism. Informed by epistemology of causation and the distinction between correlation and mechanism.

### Absence Blindness · #702 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The systematic human failure to notice things that DON'T happen—events that were prevented, disasters that were averted, problems that didn't occur because someone solved them proactively. A good IT department is invisible because nothing breaks. A good risk manager is invisible because crises don't happen. Absence blindness creates a structural undervaluation of prevention: we reward firefighting (visible, dramatic) and undervalue fire prevention (invisible, undramatic). The brain is wired to detect what's present, not what's absent—making preventive excellence literally invisible to normal cognition.
- **How it helps:** Deliberately track and celebrate preventive actions: problems averted, risks mitigated, failures prevented. Make the invisible visible by creating counterfactual awareness: 'Without this investment in security, we would have experienced X breach with Y cost.' In performance evaluation: explicitly assess preventive contributions alongside reactive ones. In decision-making: when evaluating whether prevention investments are 'worth it,' compare against the counterfactual (what would have happened without the investment), not against the visible present (nothing happened, so why did we spend?).
- **When to use:** When preventive teams or functions feel undervalued; when the urge to cut 'overhead' threatens prevention capabilities; when performance evaluation rewards visible heroics over invisible prevention; when risk management investments are questioned because 'nothing has happened.'
- **Unique value:** Identifies the specific cognitive failure—inability to perceive non-events—that systematically undervalues prevention across all domains. This isn't a bias toward one type of evidence; it's a fundamental limitation of perception. Making this limitation explicit is the first step toward compensating for it through deliberate counterfactual thinking.
- **Limits:** Not all prevention is valuable—some preventive activities address risks that are genuinely negligible. Creating counterfactuals is speculative by nature. The model can justify any prevention expense by imagining catastrophic counterfactuals. Some 'prevention' is actually unnecessary overhead. Quantifying prevented events requires assumptions that may not be testable.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in perceptual psychology's salience research and Kahneman's availability heuristic. Draws on Nassim Taleb's silent evidence concept, absence data in epidemiology (Sherlock Holmes logic), and signal detection theory. Related to counterfactual thinking research, base rate neglect, and survivorship bias in decision-making.
- *Secondary roles:* RULE · *Collection:* cognitive-bias

### Availability Cascade · #714 · `high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** A self-reinforcing cycle where a belief becomes more plausible through repetition in public discourse—regardless of its truth. A claim is made → media covers it → public discusses it → the discussion makes it feel more real → more media coverage → the claim feels even more established. Availability cascades explain how minor risks become public panics, how false narratives become 'common knowledge,' and how market bubbles form. The mechanism: availability heuristic (frequently mentioned = important/true) + social proof (everyone's talking about it = it must be real) + media incentives (alarming stories get clicks) create a self-amplifying loop.
- **How it helps:** When evaluating any belief that 'everyone knows': trace it back to evidence rather than prevalence. How many INDEPENDENT sources support this claim versus how many sources are citing each other? Availability cascades are recognizable by their velocity—claims that spread faster than evidence can support them are likely cascading on repetition rather than truth. Defensively: wait for the cascade to settle before making decisions based on it. Offensively: understand that availability cascades are the mechanism behind viral marketing, trend creation, and narrative warfare.
- **When to use:** When public concern about a risk seems disproportionate to evidence; when market enthusiasm or panic accelerates faster than fundamentals justify; when 'common knowledge' claims lack identifiable original evidence; when designing communication strategies that might intentionally or accidentally trigger cascades.
- **Unique value:** Identifies the self-reinforcing mechanism that converts repetition into perceived truth—separate from whether the claim IS true. The availability cascade operates independently of truth: true claims AND false claims can cascade with equal force. This makes 'how many people believe it' an unreliable indicator of truth and demands evidence-based evaluation.
- **Limits:** Some cascades amplify genuine truths that deserve attention. Dismissing all cascading claims as false produces conspiracy thinking. The model is better at explaining cascades retroactively than predicting them prospectively. In a media-saturated world, virtually all information reaches you through cascade dynamics.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in Kuran and Sunstein's availability cascade theory and social epistemology. Draws on Noelle-Neumann's spiral of silence, social amplification of risk framework, and cascade dynamics in sociology (Bikhchandani). Related to informational cascades, media agenda-setting theory, and herding behavior in decision-making.

### Classical Conditioning · #724 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Pavlov's discovery that neutral stimuli, when paired repeatedly with stimuli that naturally produce responses, eventually produce those responses on their own. The bell that preceded food eventually triggered salivation without food. Applied broadly: brands paired with positive emotions eventually trigger those emotions without the product. Office environments paired with stress eventually produce stress upon entering. People paired with specific experiences eventually trigger the emotions of those experiences upon appearance. Classical conditioning operates below conscious awareness—you don't choose to salivate at the bell; the association forms automatically.
- **How it helps:** Recognize and design classical conditioning deliberately. In branding: consistently pair your brand with specific emotions through advertising, experience design, and sensory cues (Apple's distinctive sounds, Starbucks's consistent ambiance). In personal productivity: pair specific environments with specific types of work until the environment alone triggers the desired work state. In relationships: be aware of what experiences are being paired with your presence—if you consistently bring bad news, you'll become classically conditioned as a negative stimulus regardless of your intentions.
- **When to use:** When designing brand experiences that need to create emotional associations; when environmental design should trigger specific behaviors or states; when understanding why certain stimuli produce strong emotional reactions without obvious cause; when habits need to be built through environmental pairing.
- **Unique value:** Reveals the mechanism by which environments, people, and stimuli acquire emotional charge: automatic association through repeated pairing. This operates below conscious choice, making it both a powerful design tool (create associations you want) and a diagnostic tool (understand existing associations that are driving behavior).
- **Limits:** Classical conditioning can be overridden by conscious evaluation. The associations extinguish over time without reinforcement. Not all emotional responses are conditioned—some are innate. Creating desired associations requires consistent pairing, which takes time and control over the environment. Ethical concerns arise when conditioning is used manipulatively.
- **Foundations:** Founded by Ivan Pavlov's physiological research (1890s–1900s). Integrated into behavioral psychology by Watson. Connected to associative learning theory, neuroscience of synaptic plasticity, and evolutionary psychology's prepared learning. Basis for exposure therapy and behavioral modification.

### Commitment and Consistency Bias · #728 · `high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Cialdini's principle that once people make a commitment (especially publicly), they experience strong internal pressure to behave consistently with that commitment—even when new evidence suggests changing course. The mechanism: cognitive dissonance between 'I committed to X' and 'X may be wrong' is resolved by doubling down on X rather than acknowledging the error. A CEO who publicly commits to a strategy continues investing despite deteriorating evidence. A voter who commits to a candidate rationalizes increasingly problematic behavior. The bias is amplified by: public commitment (others saw you commit), effort (you've invested heavily), and identity ('I'm the kind of person who...').
- **How it helps:** Build in explicit decision review points BEFORE commitment escalates. The practices: (1) Separate the initial commitment from continued commitment—'we decided to do X' doesn't mean 'we must continue doing X.' (2) Pre-commit to reassessment criteria: 'if we don't see Y by date Z, we revisit.' (3) Normalize course-correction as strength, not weakness. (4) For personal decisions: notice when you're defending a position more because you've committed to it than because the evidence supports it. The diagnostic: 'would I make this same choice today, with today's information, if I hadn't already committed?'
- **When to use:** When sunk costs and public commitments are preventing course correction; when escalation of commitment is producing worse outcomes with each cycle; when organizational culture punishes changing course more than staying wrong; when personal identity attachment to positions prevents honest evidence evaluation.
- **Unique value:** Identifies the specific psychological mechanism behind escalation of commitment: the need for self-consistency creates an internal pressure to rationalize past decisions rather than evaluate them honestly. This makes commitment itself a bias-generating mechanism—the more you've committed, the less capable you are of objective evaluation.
- **Limits:** Consistency IS sometimes the right strategy—not every commitment should be revisited. Building trust requires some degree of commitment reliability. Over-frequent reassessment produces instability. Some of what looks like consistency bias IS rational (information from having tried). The model can justify flakiness.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in Cialdini's influence research (1984) and Festinger's cognitive dissonance theory. Connected to self-perception theory, foot-in-the-door compliance research (Freedman and Fraser), and escalation of commitment literature in organizational behavior (Staw).

### Domain Dependence (Dobelli) · #740 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Rolf Dobelli's term for the failure to transfer knowledge and skills from one domain where you possess them to another domain where they'd be equally applicable. A surgeon who understands risk perfectly in the operating room but makes irrational investment decisions. A statistician who applies rigorous thinking professionally but falls for marketing manipulation personally. Domain dependence reveals that human knowledge is stored contextually—bound to the domain where it was learned—and doesn't automatically transfer to analogous situations. Transfer requires deliberate, effortful recognition of cross-domain applicability.
- **How it helps:** Regularly ask: what do I know in one domain that I'm failing to apply in another? The practice: when facing a challenge in any domain, deliberately search your OTHER domains of expertise for analogous problems and solutions. Business problem? What would an evolutionary biologist say? Relationship challenge? What would a negotiation expert recommend? The most valuable insights often come from applying established knowledge from one domain to a novel domain where it hasn't been tried. Build the habit of cross-domain translation as a deliberate practice.
- **When to use:** When expertise in one area coexists with naive errors in another; when solutions to current problems already exist in different domains but haven't been recognized; when building a mental models approach that requires cross-domain transfer; when teams with diverse expertise aren't benefiting from each other's knowledge.
- **Unique value:** Explains WHY the 'latticework of mental models' approach works: most people have knowledge trapped in domain-specific silos, and deliberately transferring frameworks across domains produces insights that neither domain generates alone. Domain dependence is the failure that mental models thinking specifically corrects.
- **Limits:** Some domain-specific knowledge genuinely doesn't transfer—domains differ in ways that make analogies misleading. Forced cross-domain application can produce bad analogies. The effort required for transfer means it can't be applied to every decision. Some specialization is valuable precisely because it respects domain boundaries.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in Nassim Taleb's epistemology and Dobelli's rationality critique. Connected to transfer of learning research in cognitive psychology, situated cognition theory, and philosophy of mind debates about domain-general versus domain-specific rationality modules (Fodor, Cosmides/Tooby).

### Duration Neglect · #741 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The psychological finding that people's retrospective evaluation of an experience is largely insensitive to its duration—a 3-minute cold-water immersion and a 30-minute one are remembered similarly if the peak intensity and ending are the same. Duration neglect works with the peak-end rule: the remembered experience is dominated by the most intense moment and the final moment, with duration barely registering. Applied to customer experience, work satisfaction, and memory: a 2-hour meeting with one great insight and a good ending is remembered as positively as a 30-minute meeting with the same profile.
- **How it helps:** Design experiences for peak intensity and strong endings rather than duration optimization. A shorter, more intense workshop may produce the same or better remembered experience as a longer, diluted one. In customer service: a quick, empathetic resolution creates a better memory than a thorough but lengthy investigation. In presentations: a tight 15-minute talk with a powerful moment is remembered more favorably than a comprehensive 60-minute presentation. The practical implication: cutting duration rarely hurts the remembered experience and often improves it by increasing intensity.
- **When to use:** When designing experiences, presentations, meetings, or customer interactions; when deciding between shorter-more-intense and longer-more-comprehensive formats; when retrospective satisfaction matters more than real-time experience; when resource constraints require choosing between duration and intensity.
- **Unique value:** Challenges the intuitive assumption that longer experiences are remembered as more valuable. Duration neglect reveals that the brain's memory system essentially ignores duration, meaning you can often deliver the same subjective value in much less time by concentrating the peak and ending. This is a direct resource-saving insight.
- **Limits:** Duration does matter for real-time experience—suffering for 30 minutes is worse than suffering for 3, even if the memory is similar. Some experiences genuinely require duration (deep learning, relationship building). Duration neglect applies to retrospective evaluation, not to all aspects of experience. Some contexts (therapeutic, educational) need sustained engagement.
- **Foundations:** Discovered by Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber, and Redelmeier (1993) via the Peak-End rule. Grounded in hedonic psychology, utility theory, and cognitive psychology's memory evaluation research. Challenges utilitarian aggregation assumptions in welfare economics.

### Feature-Positive Effect · #751 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The cognitive bias where the presence of a feature is more noticeable and influential than its absence—we notice what IS there more readily than what ISN'T there. A product with an extra feature seems better than one without it (even if the feature is useless). A resume with more credentials seems stronger (even if the additional credentials are irrelevant). A warning that lists dangers is more compelling than one that lists safeties. The feature-positive effect means: adding things is easier to perceive and value than subtracting things, which produces systematic over-complexity in products, processes, and decision-making.
- **How it helps:** Actively look for what's MISSING, not just what's present. In product evaluation: what features does this product LACK that matter? In problem diagnosis: what's ABSENT that should be present? In strategic analysis: what's NOT happening in the market that's significant? The feature-positive bias means you'll naturally over-weight additions and under-weight subtractions. For product design: features that are REMOVED (simplification) are often more valuable than features that are ADDED—but harder to sell because the absence is invisible.
- **When to use:** When product complexity is growing without proportional value increase; when analysis focuses on present features while ignoring critical absences; when addition bias is producing bloated processes, products, or strategies; when the value of simplification needs to be recognized despite being invisible.
- **Unique value:** Explains the specific perceptual asymmetry behind complexity creep: additions are visible and valued while subtractions are invisible and undervalued. This makes simplification—one of the highest-leverage design moves—systematically undervalued because removing something doesn't activate the feature-positive detection system.
- **Limits:** Some additions genuinely ARE more valuable than subtractions. In some contexts, comprehensiveness is a legitimate virtue. The bias toward noticing presences may be adaptive in many environments. Extreme subtraction produces inadequacy. The effort to notice absences has diminishing returns.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in Wason and Johnson-Laird's research on hypothesis testing and presence-absence asymmetries in attention. Connected to signal detection theory, ecological rationality, and cognitive psychology's figure-ground distinctions in perception and reasoning.

### Forecast Illusion · #754 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Dobelli's term for the persistent belief that experts can predict the future—despite overwhelming evidence that expert predictions are barely better than chance for most complex domains. Philip Tetlock's research showed that the average expert's predictions were roughly as accurate as 'a dart-throwing chimpanzee.' Stock market forecasters, political analysts, and economic prognosticators consistently fail to outperform simple baseline models. Yet people continue to consume and act on forecasts because: the forecasters are confident, occasional correct predictions get remembered while failures are forgotten, and the alternative (accepting fundamental uncertainty) is psychologically intolerable.
- **How it helps:** Evaluate forecasts by track record, not by confidence. For any forecast: (1) Does this forecaster have a documented track record of accuracy? (2) Is this a domain where prediction is possible (weather short-term, physical systems) or fundamentally uncertain (markets, geopolitics, technology adoption)? (3) How would a simple baseline model (last year's numbers, trend extrapolation) perform? If the expert can't beat the baseline, the forecast has no value above free information. For your own planning: build strategies that are robust across multiple scenarios rather than optimized for one predicted future.
- **When to use:** When strategic decisions are based on expert forecasts without track record verification; when confidence in predictions is high despite prediction domains being fundamentally uncertain; when planning needs to shift from prediction to robustness; when evaluating whether forecasting services provide value above simple baselines.
- **Unique value:** Challenges the entire forecasting industry by showing that expert confidence doesn't correlate with expert accuracy in complex domains. The illusion is the BELIEF in predictability, not the predictions themselves. This redirects strategy from prediction (impossible) to robustness (achievable): build strategies that work across multiple scenarios.
- **Limits:** Some domains ARE predictable (short-term weather, demographic trends, physical processes). Tetlock's 'superforecasters' show that some people CAN outperform baselines. Scenario planning requires some forecasting, even if probabilistic. Complete rejection of forecasting can produce strategic paralysis. Some forecasts are useful even when imprecise.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in Kahneman and Lovallo's research on optimistic bias and the planning fallacy. Connected to Tetlock's forecasting research, Philip Brier scoring, calibration literature in decision science, and organizational behavior on overconfidence in strategic planning.

### Groupthink · #759 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Irving Janis's concept describing a mode of thinking where the desire for group harmony and conformity overrides realistic appraisal of alternatives—producing irrational or dysfunctional decisions. Groupthink symptoms: illusion of invulnerability, collective rationalization, belief in the group's moral superiority, stereotyping of out-groups, pressure on dissenters, self-censorship, illusion of unanimity, and self-appointed 'mind guards.' Bay of Pigs, Challenger disaster, and corporate scandals consistently trace back to groupthink dynamics where dissent was suppressed and the group converged on catastrophically wrong decisions.
- **How it helps:** Build structural protections against groupthink: assign a devil's advocate role (rotating), solicit anonymous input before group discussion, invite outside perspectives regularly, have leaders speak LAST (not first), and explicitly reward disagreement. The most important practice: create a norm where questioning the group consensus is treated as a contribution, not a threat. Groupthink is strongest in cohesive groups with a directive leader under pressure—exactly the conditions that describe most high-stakes business decisions.
- **When to use:** When team decisions feel unanimously obvious (unanimity is a groupthink warning sign); when dissenting voices have gone quiet; when the team is cohesive, under pressure, and led by someone with strong opinions; when past decisions produced by group consensus turned out to be catastrophically wrong.
- **Unique value:** Identifies the specific social dynamics that produce collective stupidity from individually smart people. Groupthink isn't about having bad members—it's about good members in bad dynamics. This means the intervention is structural (change the decision process) rather than personnel (change the people), which is more actionable.
- **Limits:** Not all group consensus is groupthink—sometimes groups correctly converge on the right answer. Over-correcting with forced dissent can slow decision-making and create artificial conflict. The original research methodology has been criticized. Some cohesive groups with strong leaders make excellent decisions. Distinguishing groupthink from legitimate agreement requires judgment.
- **Foundations:** Coined by Irving Janis (1972) from Bay of Pigs case studies. Rooted in social psychology's conformity research and Asch experiments. Connected to organizational behavior, social identity theory, group cohesion research, and team decision-making literature on devil's advocacy.

### Illusion of Attention · #768 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The false belief that we notice everything important in our visual and cognitive field—when in reality, attention is extremely selective and we miss enormous amounts of information that we're 'looking at.' Simons and Chabris's famous gorilla experiment: people counting basketball passes completely miss a person in a gorilla suit walking through the scene. Inattentional blindness isn't a failure of vision—it's a feature of attention: we see what we're looking for and miss what we're not. The 'illusion' is our belief that we would notice—most people are confidently wrong about their attentional capacity.
- **How it helps:** Design systems and processes that don't rely on attention alone for catching important information. In quality control: checklists and structured inspection protocols catch what attention misses. In decision-making: structured evaluation criteria prevent important factors from being missed because attention was focused elsewhere. In safety: alarms, automated monitoring, and redundant checks exist because human attention WILL miss critical signals. The personal practice: assume you're missing important information in every situation, and build habits that compensate (checklists, second opinions, structured reviews).
- **When to use:** When relying on human attention for quality control or safety monitoring; when important information might be missed because focus is elsewhere; when designing systems that need to catch anomalies that attention would miss; when overconfidence in attentional capacity is producing preventable oversights.
- **Unique value:** Reveals that the biggest attention problem isn't distraction but INVISIBILITY—we literally don't see things we're not looking for, and we're confidently unaware of what we've missed. This makes attention-dependent systems inherently unreliable and makes structured, attention-independent systems essential for high-stakes domains.
- **Limits:** Expertise increases relevant attention (radiologists see what untrained eyes miss). Not everything needs to be noticed. The gorilla experiment is a specific condition—real-world attention is often better than lab conditions suggest. Building systems for everything attention might miss is impractical.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in Simons and Chabris's inattentional blindness research (1999, gorilla experiment). Rooted in cognitive psychology's selective attention theory (Broadbent, Treisman) and Mack and Rock's inattentional blindness work. Connected to human factors and safety research.

### Illusion of Transparency · #771 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The false belief that your internal states—thoughts, feelings, intentions—are more visible to others than they actually are. You feel nervous during a presentation and assume everyone can see it. You feel guilty about a decision and assume it shows on your face. You think your hint was obvious and assume the other person got it. The illusion works in both directions: you also overestimate how well you can read others' internal states. The result: chronic miscommunication where people assume their intentions were communicated (they weren't) and assume they understood others' intentions (they didn't).
- **How it helps:** When communication matters: make your internal states EXPLICIT rather than assuming they're visible. Don't assume people can tell what you're thinking, feeling, or intending—tell them. 'I want you to know I'm excited about this project' communicates more reliably than assuming your excitement is obvious. 'I'm feeling uncertain about this direction' communicates more reliably than assuming your hesitation is readable. In leadership: explicit communication of intention, reasoning, and emotion prevents the miscommunication that the illusion of transparency guarantees.
- **When to use:** When important intentions or feelings need to be communicated reliably; when assuming others 'should know' how you feel is producing miscommunication; when leadership communication needs to be explicit rather than implied; when feedback conversations require stating the unsaid rather than assuming it's obvious.
- **Unique value:** Identifies the specific perceptual error that produces chronic miscommunication: we believe our internal states leak out more than they do. The practical implication: NEVER assume a thought, feeling, or intention has been communicated unless you've stated it explicitly. The illusion guarantees that implicit communication fails far more often than people realize.
- **Limits:** Some people ARE more transparent than they think (expressive faces, obvious body language). Some communication IS successfully implicit (close relationships, high-context cultures). Over-explicit communication can feel patronizing. Emotional intelligence does enable some accurate reading of others. Cultural context heavily influences how much implicit vs. explicit communication is expected.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in Gilovich, Savitsky, and Medvec's research (1998). Connected to the spotlight effect, perspective-taking failures in social psychology, theory-of-mind research, and egocentric anchoring in Kahneman's heuristics literature.

### Information Bias (Seeking Useless Info) · #775 · `high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The tendency to seek additional information even when it cannot affect the decision—gathering data for comfort rather than for decision quality. A manager who requests more reports before making a decision that the existing data already supports. A researcher who runs more studies on a question that's already been answered. The mechanism: information-seeking feels productive and reduces anxiety, but past a threshold, additional information adds noise without adding signal and delays action without improving outcomes. Information bias produces analysis paralysis disguised as diligence.
- **How it helps:** Before seeking more information, ask: 'would any possible result from this additional information change my decision?' If no realistic outcome would alter the course of action, the information-seeking is bias, not diligence. The practice: identify the decision-relevant information BEFORE gathering data, then stop when you have it. In organizational decision-making: set explicit decision criteria and data requirements upfront, then decide when those criteria are met—rather than continuously raising the evidence bar. - **When to use:** When decisions keep getting delayed pending 'more data'; when additional analysis hasn't changed the recommendation for several iterations; when information-gathering has become a substitute for decision-making; when the cost of delay exceeds the value of additional information.
- **Unique value:** Distinguishes between information that can change a decision (valuable) and information that can't (noise). This provides a specific stopping rule for analysis: if no possible result would change the action, stop analyzing. Most 'insufficient data' claims are actually information bias—the data is sufficient, the decision is uncomfortable.
- **Limits:** Sometimes additional information genuinely IS needed. The 'would it change my decision?' test requires knowing what decisions you face, which isn't always clear. In uncertain environments, gathering more information can be genuinely valuable even when the immediate use isn't obvious. Premature closure on information-gathering can miss important insights.

### Liking Bias (Tendency to Agree with People We Like) · #782 · `high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The systematic tendency to evaluate ideas, proposals, and arguments more favorably when they come from people we like—and more critically when they come from people we dislike. ### Normalcy Bias · #803 · `high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The psychological tendency to underestimate the probability and impact of disruptions—assuming that because things have been normal, they will continue to be normal. People in the path of a hurricane don't evacuate because 'it's never been that bad before.' Organizations don't prepare for disruption because 'our industry has always been stable.' In the 2004 tsunami, people stood on the beach watching the water recede—interpreting an unprecedented event through normal expectations rather than recognizing danger.

- **When to use:** When warning signs are being dismissed as 'probably nothing'; when risk assessment is anchored to historical normalcy; when crisis preparation feels unnecessary because 'it's never happened before'; when any planning process assumes the future will resemble the past.
- **Unique value:** Identifies the specific cognitive mechanism that causes delayed response to genuine threats: the brain's default assumption that conditions will remain normal until proven otherwise, combined with the tendency to interpret even abnormal evidence through a normal-conditions framework. This isn't optimism—it's a perceptual filter that literally prevents threat recognition.
- **Limits:** Most of the time, normalcy IS the correct expectation—rare events are, by definition, rare. Over-correcting for normalcy bias produces constant false alarms and crisis fatigue. The model can justify excessive pessimism or doomsday thinking. Distinguishing between genuine warning signs and noise requires judgment that the model doesn't provide.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in disaster psychology and risk communication research. Connected to cognitive psychology's status quo bias, prospect theory's loss aversion, evolutionary psychology on threat response calibration, and emergency management research on warning compliance and evacuation behavior.

### Omission Bias · #806 · `high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The tendency to judge harmful actions as worse than equally harmful inactions—preferring to let bad things happen through inaction rather than cause them through action, even when the consequences are identical or worse. People judge a doctor who administers a lethal injection as more culpable than one who withholds life-saving treatment—even though both result in death. In business: companies that fail to act on known problems (omission) face less scrutiny than those whose actions cause problems (commission)—creating a systematic bias toward inaction. Omission bias makes 'doing nothing' feel morally safer even when it's practically identical to 'doing harm.'
- **How it helps:** Recognize when you're choosing inaction because it feels morally safer rather than because it produces better outcomes. The test: would you judge this situation differently if someone actively CAUSED the same outcome that your inaction is allowing? If the outcome is the same, the moral distinction between action and inaction is largely illusory. In organizations: evaluate the consequences of NOT acting with the same rigor as the consequences of acting. Decisions NOT to launch, NOT to hire, NOT to invest are decisions with consequences—they just feel less weighty because they're omissions.
- **When to use:** When inaction is being chosen because it feels safer than action despite equal or worse consequences; when the status quo is being preserved not because it's good but because changing it would require active choice; when risk evaluation is asymmetric between action risks and inaction risks; when 'do nothing' is being treated as costless.
- **Unique value:** Identifies the specific mechanism by which organizations and individuals systematically under-invest in action: harmful inaction is judged more leniently than harmful action, creating a bias toward the status quo even when it's producing ongoing harm. The insight: 'do nothing' IS a decision with consequences that should be evaluated as rigorously as any active alternative.
- **Limits:** The action-inaction distinction IS morally relevant in some frameworks (though not in consequentialism). In some cases, inaction genuinely is the lower-risk option. The model can be used to justify reckless action ('we must do something!'). Legal and social accountability for actions vs. omissions differs, making the distinction practically relevant even if philosophically questionable.

### Operant Conditioning · #807 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Skinner's framework that behavior is shaped by its consequences: reinforcement (positive or negative) increases behavior frequency, while punishment decreases it. Positive reinforcement (adding reward) is the most effective shaper. Negative reinforcement (removing discomfort) also strengthens behavior. Punishment (adding pain) suppresses behavior temporarily but doesn't teach alternatives and generates resentment. Applied to organizations: every incentive system, feedback practice, and management style is an operant conditioning program—the question is whether it's conditioning the behaviors you want or the behaviors it accidentally reinforces.
- **How it helps:** Audit what your current environment is actually reinforcing—not what it's intended to reinforce but what consequences actually follow behavior. If staying late is praised (reinforcement) while efficient early completion is ignored, you're conditioning long hours, not productivity. If speaking up in meetings is followed by criticism (punishment), you're conditioning silence. The fix: reinforce the behavior you want, don't punish the behavior you don't want (punishment creates fear and avoidance, not change). Use variable-ratio reinforcement (occasional, unpredictable rewards) for the most persistent behavioral change.
- **When to use:** When desired behaviors aren't occurring despite stated expectations; when incentive systems produce unintended behaviors; when team dynamics seem to reward the wrong things; when designing feedback, recognition, or incentive programs.
- **Unique value:** Provides the most empirically validated framework for understanding why people do what they do in organizations: they do what gets reinforced. This shifts the question from 'why won't people do X?' to 'what is currently being reinforced instead of X?'—which immediately suggests the intervention (change the reinforcement schedule, not the people).
- **Limits:** Humans are more complex than Skinner's framework suggests—intrinsic motivation, values, and identity also drive behavior. Treating people as subjects of conditioning can be dehumanizing. The optimal reinforcement schedule depends on context and individual differences. Operant conditioning describes behavioral change but not cognitive or emotional change.
- **Foundations:** Founded by B.F. Skinner (1938) building on Thorndike's Law of Effect. Rooted in behaviorist psychology. Connected to reinforcement schedules, behavior modification, organizational behavior management, neuroscience of dopaminergic reward circuits, and behavioral economics on variable-ratio reinforcement.

### Overthinking (Analysis Paralysis) · #812 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The state where continued analysis reduces rather than improves decision quality—where gathering more information, considering more scenarios, and deliberating longer produces worse outcomes than deciding with the information already available. Overthinking occurs when the marginal cost of additional analysis (delayed action, missed opportunities, cognitive exhaustion, increased anxiety) exceeds its marginal benefit (improved accuracy). Research shows that beyond a moderate amount of deliberation, additional thinking frequently DECREASES decision quality by introducing noise, anxiety, and second-guessing.
- **How it helps:** Set decision deadlines proportional to reversibility and stakes. High-stakes, irreversible decisions deserve deliberation. Low-stakes, reversible decisions should be made quickly. The most common error: treating reversible decisions as irreversible and overthinking them. Jeff Bezos's framework: Type 1 decisions (irreversible, high-stakes) deserve deep analysis. Type 2 decisions (reversible, moderate-stakes) should be made quickly by individuals or small teams. Most decisions are Type 2 but get treated as Type 1, producing organizational paralysis.
- **When to use:** When decision-making is consistently too slow relative to the stakes involved; when additional analysis isn't changing the conclusion; when the cost of delayed action exceeds the benefit of additional information; when anxiety rather than genuine uncertainty is driving continued deliberation.
- **Unique value:** Provides the counterargument to 'think more carefully': beyond a threshold, thinking MORE produces WORSE decisions. This is counterintuitive in analytical cultures that assume more analysis = better outcomes. The model gives permission to decide with imperfect information by making explicit that over-analysis has costs.
- **Limits:** Some decisions genuinely require extensive analysis—not all deliberation is overthinking. The threshold between productive analysis and overthinking is context-dependent and hard to identify in the moment. The model can be used to justify reckless decisions. Speed isn't always better—some 'slow' decisions prevent catastrophic errors.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in Schwartz's paradox of choice research, satisficing theory (Simon), and dual-process theory. Connected to regret theory, decision fatigue research, and organizational behavior on committee decision failures. Also grounded in positive psychology on rumination (Nolen-Hoeksema).

### Peak-End Rule · #817 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Kahneman's finding that people judge experiences primarily by their most intense moment (the peak) and their final moment (the end)—not by the average or total of the experience. A colonoscopy that's less painful overall but ends with higher pain is remembered as worse than one that's more painful overall but ends gently. A vacation with one magical moment and a pleasant last day is remembered more fondly than a uniformly good trip. The implication: the experience people REMEMBER (and base future decisions on) is different from the experience they actually HAD.
- **How it helps:** Design experiences—customer journeys, employee experiences, presentations, events—to optimize the peak and the end. Ensure the most memorable positive moment is truly exceptional (the peak), and ensure the final interaction is positive (the end). Customer service: a resolved complaint that ends with unexpected generosity creates a better memory than a smooth, unremarkable experience. Presentations: build to a powerful climax and end strong rather than distributing quality evenly. Job exits: the last impression an employee has determines their lasting narrative about the company.
- **When to use:** When designing customer experiences, events, presentations, or any journey with a beginning and end; when optimizing limited resources across an experience (invest disproportionately in the peak and end); when understanding why experiences that were objectively good are remembered poorly (weak ending); when offboarding employees, concluding client engagements, or ending any relationship.
- **Unique value:** Reveals that the experience people evaluate is NOT the experience they have—it's a reconstruction dominated by two moments. This means experience design should allocate resources non-uniformly: invest disproportionately in creating one remarkable peak and a strong ending, rather than spreading quality evenly across the entire experience.
- **Limits:** The rule applies to retrospective evaluation, not real-time experience—moment-to-moment quality still matters for in-the-moment satisfaction. Very long or complex experiences may not follow the rule as cleanly. Negative peaks are remembered too—a terrible moment can define an otherwise positive experience. Some experiences don't have clear peaks or endings.
- **Foundations:** Discovered by Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber, and Redelmeier (1993). Rooted in hedonic psychology and memory evaluation research. Connected to duration neglect, utility theory critiques, and service design literature. Grounded in dual-process distinctions between experiencing and remembering selves.

### Pluralistic Ignorance · #823 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The social phenomenon where everyone privately disagrees with a norm but publicly complies because they assume everyone else genuinely supports it—creating a false consensus that nobody actually holds. The classic example: every student in a class thinks the material is confusing but nobody asks questions because they assume everyone else understands. In organizations: every team member thinks the strategy is flawed but nobody speaks up because they assume everyone else supports it. Pluralistic ignorance maintains dysfunctional norms, bad policies, and harmful practices long past their expiration date because the private dissent never becomes visible.
- **How it helps:** When you suspect pluralistic ignorance: break the silence. One person who says 'I don't understand this' or 'I'm not sure this strategy is right' often unleashes a cascade of relief and agreement. The most effective intervention: create anonymous feedback channels that bypass the social pressure to conform. Surveys that reveal private opinions publicly can shatter pluralistic ignorance instantly—showing everyone that the consensus they've been complying with doesn't actually exist. For leaders: explicitly invite dissent and model it yourself. The emperor has no clothes, but nobody will say so until someone goes first.
- **When to use:** When compliance exists without genuine buy-in; when team unanimity seems too perfect; when nobody questions decisions that seem questionable; when anonymous feedback reveals dramatically different views than public discussion; when cultural norms persist despite private dissatisfaction.
- **Unique value:** Reveals the mechanism by which groups maintain norms NOBODY actually supports: each person's public compliance serves as evidence for everyone else that the norm has genuine support. This means that the most dysfunctional norms can be the most stable—because the more dysfunctional the norm, the less likely anyone is to risk being the first to challenge it.
- **Limits:** Not all compliance is pluralistic ignorance—sometimes people genuinely support the norm. Being 'the first to speak' carries real social risk. Anonymous feedback can be gamed or unrepresentative. Some norms persist for good reasons that individuals don't fully understand. Breaking pluralistic ignorance can unleash chaos if the underlying disagreements are deep.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in Allport's social psychology (1920s) and Katz and Allport's empirical work. Connected to spiral of silence theory (Noelle-Neumann), conformity research, false consensus effect, and organizational behavior on unspoken norms and preference falsification (Kuran).

### Priming · #825 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The psychological phenomenon where exposure to a stimulus influences subsequent judgment and behavior—often without conscious awareness. Seeing the word 'old' makes people walk slower. Reading about achievement makes people try harder on subsequent tasks. Exposure to money-related concepts makes people more self-reliant and less helpful. Priming operates below consciousness: the primed person doesn't know they've been influenced and sincerely believes their subsequent behavior is freely chosen. Every environment, communication, and experience is priming future behavior.
- **How it helps:** Design your environments, communications, and rituals to prime the behaviors you want. Office design primes collaboration or isolation. Meeting openings prime creative or analytical thinking. Language choices prime competitive or cooperative behavior. The practical application: before important activities, expose yourself and your team to priming stimuli aligned with the desired mindset. Before creative work: expose to diverse stimuli, art, or nature. Before analytical work: expose to structure, data, and precision. - **When to use:** When designing environments, communications, or rituals that shape subsequent behavior; when subtle cues may be undermining desired outcomes; when preparing for important activities and want to set the right mindset; when analyzing why behavior patterns emerge in specific environments.
- **Unique value:** Reveals that behavior is shaped by environmental cues operating below conscious awareness—meaning you can influence behavior through environmental design rather than through instruction or willpower. This is both a powerful tool (design environments that prime desired behavior) and a critical warning (your current environment is already priming you in ways you're not aware of).

### Relative Satisfaction/Misery Tendencies · #835 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The finding that subjective satisfaction is determined more by RELATIVE comparisons than by ABSOLUTE conditions—people evaluate their situation by comparing to reference points (peers, past selves, expectations) rather than by objective standards. A $100K salary feels great among friends earning $70K and terrible among friends earning $200K—the same absolute condition produces opposite subjective experiences depending on the reference point. Relative comparison explains: why rising absolute prosperity doesn't increase average happiness (the reference point rises too), why social media decreases wellbeing (upward comparison), and why lottery winners return to baseline happiness (reference point adjusts).
- **How it helps:** Manage your reference points as deliberately as you manage your circumstances. The practices: (1) Be intentional about social comparison targets—compare to relevant benchmarks, not to the most visible successes. (2) Practice gratitude (shifts reference point to include what you have rather than only what you lack). (3) Recognize hedonic adaptation—the satisfaction from any improvement is temporary as the new state becomes the new reference point. (4) For organizations: relative pay and recognition matter as much as absolute levels. An employee who discovers a peer earns 10% more experiences a pay CUT in relative terms.
- **When to use:** When satisfaction seems disconnected from objective conditions; when social comparison is driving unhappiness despite favorable circumstances; when designing compensation systems that account for relative perception; when understanding why improvements produce temporary satisfaction that fades as reference points adjust.
- **Unique value:** Explains the paradox of progress: absolute conditions improve but satisfaction doesn't, because the reference point rises alongside conditions. This makes reference point management as important as condition management—and explains why 'more' often doesn't feel like 'better.'
- **Limits:** Absolute conditions DO matter at low levels (poverty, deprivation). Some comparisons are informative and useful. Attempts to manage reference points can feel manipulative. Individual variation in comparison tendency is significant. Cultural context shapes which comparisons are activated.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in social comparison theory (Festinger, 1954) and hedonic adaptation research in psychology. Draws on prospect theory's reference-dependence framework and Easterlin's paradox in welfare economics. Stoic philosophy and Buddhist psychology offer complementary traditions on managing comparative cognition.

### Scope Insensitivity · #841 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The failure of human emotion and valuation to scale proportionally with the magnitude of an issue. People will pay roughly the same to save 2,000 birds as to save 200,000 birds—the emotional response to 'birds dying' doesn't scale with the number. A charity appeal featuring one starving child raises more than one featuring millions (the 'identifiable victim effect'). In business: the emotional urgency assigned to a $100K problem and a $10M problem may be similar because both register as 'big problem.' Scope insensitivity means human judgment is poorly calibrated for quantity, scale, and magnitude.
- **How it helps:** When evaluating problems, opportunities, or risks of different magnitudes: force quantitative comparison rather than relying on emotional response. Your gut will assign similar urgency to a 10x range of magnitudes—so make the numbers explicit and visible. Create decision frameworks that weight by actual magnitude: a bug affecting 100,000 users isn't just 'worse' than one affecting 1,000—it's 100x worse and should receive 100x the resource allocation. The most common scope insensitivity error in business: allocating resources based on emotional salience rather than quantitative impact.
- **When to use:** When resource allocation doesn't match problem magnitude; when all problems feel equally urgent regardless of actual scale; when philanthropic or social impact decisions need to be calibrated to magnitude; when prioritization seems to ignore 10x or 100x differences in impact.
- **Unique value:** Reveals why 'bigger' problems don't automatically receive proportionally bigger responses: human emotion doesn't scale with magnitude. This explains chronic misallocation—from charitable giving (more money to photogenic causes than to larger-scale suffering) to business (equal urgency for 10x different problems). The fix: impose quantitative discipline on what emotion can't calibrate.
- **Limits:** Scope sensitivity ISN'T always appropriate—sometimes the 'small' problem has qualitative significance beyond its magnitude. Pure quantitative scaling can miss strategic importance. Some scope insensitivity may be rational given diminishing marginal utility. The model applies to emotional valuation; analytical valuation can be scope-sensitive.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in psychophysics and Weber-Fechner law showing non-linear perception of magnitude. Kahneman and colleagues documented scope insensitivity in willingness-to-pay studies. Connected to moral psychology's work on psychic numbing (Slovic) and utilitarian ethics, which requires consistent scaling of outcomes.

### Sleeper Effect · #847 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The counterintuitive finding that persuasive messages from low-credibility sources become MORE persuasive over time, not less. Initially, you discount the message because the source is unreliable. Over time, you remember the content but forget the source—and the discounting fades. A rumor from an unreliable source is dismissed initially but, weeks later, you vaguely remember the claim without remembering you'd dismissed it. In marketing: ads from distrusted sources may be ineffective immediately but slowly become effective as the source-content association weakens. The sleeper effect reverses the normal pattern where persuasion decays over time.
- **How it helps:** When managing information and persuasion: be aware that source credibility fades faster than message content. Defensively: when you dismiss a claim because of its source, note the dismissal alongside the claim—otherwise the sleeper effect may cause you to accept it later. Offensively: understand that persistent messaging from even low-credibility sources can be effective over time as source associations weaken. For brand management: even unfair negative claims can become 'things people vaguely know' over time. The implication: addressing misinformation quickly matters because once the content separates from the source, it becomes harder to counter.
- **When to use:** When old, dismissed information is resurfacing as vaguely remembered 'facts'; when misinformation from unreliable sources is gaining traction over time; when persuasion campaigns need to account for delayed effects; when designing communication strategies that anticipate source-content dissociation.
- **Unique value:** Reveals the counterintuitive mechanism by which discredited information gains influence over time: the source attribution decays faster than the message content, removing the reason you initially dismissed it. This makes timely response to misinformation critical—delay allows the sleeper effect to separate the claim from its discrediting context.
- **Limits:** The sleeper effect requires specific conditions (strong initial discounting cue, persuasive message content) and doesn't always occur. The effect is typically small and controversial in the research literature. High-impact, high-credibility refutations may prevent it. Individual variation in source-content memory association is significant.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in social psychology's persuasion and attitude change literature (Hovland). Draws on source credibility theory and memory research showing differential decay rates for message content vs. source cues. Connected to media effects research and misinformation studies examining how discounting cues erode over time.

### Substitution Heuristic · #856 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Kahneman's meta-heuristic describing how the brain handles difficult questions by substituting an easier question without noticing the swap. 'How happy am I with my life?' gets substituted with 'how do I feel right now?' 'Is this a good investment?' gets substituted with 'do I like this company?' 'How probable is this risk?' gets substituted with 'how easily can I imagine this happening?' The substitution is invisible to the thinker—they believe they've answered the hard question when they've actually answered the easy one. This meta-heuristic EXPLAINS many other biases: affect heuristic, availability heuristic, and representativeness are all specific instances of substitution.
- **How it helps:** When making important judgments: identify the ACTUAL question being answered versus the question being ASKED. The practice: after reaching a conclusion, ask 'did I actually answer the question that was asked, or did I substitute a different, easier question?' Common substitutions: 'is this strategy good?' → 'does this strategy feel right?' 'Is this person competent?' → 'is this person likeable?' 'How likely is this outcome?' → 'how easily can I imagine it?' Identifying the substitution is the first step to answering the actual question.
- **When to use:** When important questions are being answered too quickly (substitution may have occurred); when intuitive answers don't match analytical answers (a different question may have been substituted); when understanding why specific biases occur (they're often substitution of an easier question); when decision quality depends on answering the RIGHT question, not an easier proxy.
- **Unique value:** Provides the meta-explanation for dozens of specific biases: they're all instances of the brain substituting an easier question for a harder one. This makes the substitution heuristic the 'theory behind the theories'—understanding substitution gives you a single diagnostic that catches many specific biases simultaneously.
- **Limits:** Substitution is sometimes appropriate—heuristic substitutes are often good-enough answers. Identifying the substitution requires knowing what the 'real' question is, which isn't always clear. Fighting every substitution is cognitively exhausting. In some cases, the substitute question IS the more relevant question.
- **Foundations:** Central to Kahneman's System 1/System 2 framework and the attribute substitution research with Frederick. Draws on cognitive psychology's dual-process theory and heuristics research. Connected to decision analysis showing systematic gaps between target questions and surrogate questions people actually answer.

### Unit Bias · #871 · `high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The tendency to consume or complete one 'unit' of something regardless of how the unit is defined—eating a full plate regardless of plate size, completing a full project regardless of project scope, watching a full episode regardless of episode length. The mechanism: the brain uses the unit boundary as a natural stopping point, so larger units produce larger consumption and smaller units produce smaller consumption. Restaurants that increase portion sizes increase consumption without increasing hunger. Projects defined in large chunks get completed in large chunks; the same work broken into smaller units gets evaluated (and potentially abandoned) at each boundary.
- **How it helps:** Design units strategically to shape behavior. For consumption reduction: use smaller plates, smaller packages, smaller default serving sizes. For project management: break large projects into small milestones—each milestone creates a natural evaluation point where continuation is a deliberate choice rather than an automatic continuation of the current unit. For productivity: define 'done' in small increments (the Pomodoro Technique exploits unit bias by making 25 minutes the unit of work). The unit boundary is where behavior gets evaluated and choices get made—so place boundaries where you want evaluation to happen.
- **When to use:** When consumption patterns need to be reshaped (food, time, resources); when projects continue past the point of value because there's no natural stopping point; when designing products, portions, or processes where unit size affects behavior; when large commitments need to be made interruptible.
- **Unique value:** Identifies a simple, powerful lever for behavior change: redefine the unit. Unlike interventions that require willpower or motivation, unit bias works through environmental design—change the size of the container, portion, or project chunk and behavior follows automatically. The most effortless behavioral intervention available.
- **Limits:** Unit bias is overridable by conscious choice—it influences but doesn't determine. In some contexts, breaking things into smaller units creates overhead that exceeds the benefit. People can learn to compensate (eating two small plates). The optimal unit size depends on context and goals.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in psychophysics (Weber-Fechner law on unit perception), behavioral economics research on portion size (Wansink), and consumer behavior research. Draws on social norms theory and cognitive psychology's research on satisficing, where pre-packaged units function as default stopping rules for consumption.

### Deprival-Superreaction Tendency · #886 · `high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Munger's term for the disproportionately intense reaction people have to LOSING something they already possess—a reaction that exceeds the pleasure of acquiring the same thing. Losing $100 feels roughly twice as bad as gaining $100 feels good (Kahneman & Tversky's loss aversion quantified). But Munger's framing goes beyond loss aversion: the 'super-reaction' means people will take IRRATIONAL ACTIONS to prevent or reverse loss—fighting harder to keep a mediocre employee than they would to hire an equivalent one, holding losing investments far too long, and escalating conflicts to avoid conceding anything. The deprival super-reaction is especially intense when the loss is perceived as caused by someone else.
- **How it helps:** When experiencing the impulse to fight a loss: pause and evaluate the ABSOLUTE value of what's at stake rather than the LOSS FRAME. The practice: 'Would I choose to acquire this thing today at this cost if I didn't already have it?' If not, the deprival super-reaction is driving your behavior, not rational assessment. In negotiations: understand that the other party's resistance to concessions is partly deprival super-reaction, not just rational valuation. In investment: set sell rules BEFORE purchasing so the sell decision isn't contaminated by deprival super-reaction when losses materialize.
- **When to use:** When resistance to loss seems disproportionate to the value at stake; when sunk cost reasoning is preventing rational cut-loss decisions; when negotiations are stuck because both parties are in loss-prevention mode; when the emotional intensity of a potential loss exceeds what the situation warrants.
- **Unique value:** Goes beyond standard loss aversion by naming the SUPER-REACTION: people don't just dislike losses more than equivalent gains; they take irrational, disproportionate ACTION to prevent losses. This explains escalation of commitment, irrational litigation, and sunk cost behavior better than simple loss aversion.
- **Limits:** Some strong reactions to loss ARE proportionate (threats to survival, reputation). Not all loss aversion is irrational. The 2:1 loss/gain ratio is approximate and context-dependent. Complete immunity to deprival super-reaction might produce excessive passivity about genuine threats.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in Kahneman's loss aversion research and prospect theory. Closely related to the endowment effect (Thaler) and status quo bias. Munger's formulation connects to behavioral economics research on reference-point dependence and evolutionary psychology's asymmetric valuation of gains vs. losses.

### Social-Proof Tendency · #902 · `high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Munger's emphasis on the overwhelming power of social proof in shaping human behavior: people determine what is correct by observing what others do, especially in conditions of uncertainty. When uncertain about how to behave, we look to others—and their behavior becomes our evidence for correct action. The mechanism is often adaptive (others' behavior contains real information about the environment) but becomes dangerous when: (1) Everyone is looking at everyone else and no one has independent information (information cascade). Munger considers social proof among the most powerful and dangerous of psychological tendencies.

Once you've checked social proof, ask: 'does the crowd have independent information, or is everyone just following everyone else?' In organizations: prevent social proof cascades by collecting opinions independently before group discussion (pre-meeting written inputs, anonymous polling). For marketing: social proof is your most powerful persuasion tool—testimonials, user numbers, and endorsements work because they activate this tendency.
- **When to use:** When crowd behavior is influencing decisions that should be based on independent analysis; when information cascades might be producing false consensus; when marketing needs to leverage social proof effectively; when organizational decision-making needs to prevent opinion cascades.
- **Unique value:** Munger's framing emphasizes social proof as BOTH the most useful heuristic (crowds often have information) AND the most dangerous bias (crowds can be systematically wrong). The key diagnostic: does the social proof reflect independent information aggregation or is everyone just following everyone else?
- **Limits:** Social proof is often genuinely informative—crowds frequently outperform individuals. Complete independence from social proof is neither possible nor desirable. The wisdom-of-crowds effect requires independent judgment, which is real but hard to verify. Resisting social proof carries social costs.
- **Foundations:** Parallels row 849/850; grounded in Asch's conformity research, informational vs. normative social influence theory, and evolutionary herd behavior theory. Munger draws on crowd psychology and information cascade theory to explain why social proof is simultaneously a useful heuristic and a source of systematic error.

### Streisand Effect · #920 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Attempting to suppress or hide information often amplifies its spread. Named after Barbra Streisand's lawsuit to remove photos of her home, which only drew millions more views. The attempt to control information becomes the story, generating far more attention than the original information would have received. In the internet age, the Streisand Effect is one of the most reliable and counterintuitive dynamics in communication.
- **How it helps:** Critical for crisis communications, PR strategy, and reputation management. Helps leaders know when to address issues quietly vs. when any response will amplify the problem. The first question in any suppression strategy should be: 'Will this trigger the Streisand Effect?'

### Self-Deception and Denial · #1229 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The psychological mechanisms by which people systematically distort their perception of reality to maintain psychological comfort, protect self-image, or avoid painful truths. Self-deception differs from lying (which requires knowing the truth and deliberately stating otherwise): in self-deception, the person genuinely doesn't 'see' the truth they're avoiding. DENIAL is a specific form: the unconscious refusal to acknowledge a reality that's too threatening. Mechanisms include: selective attention (noticing only confirming evidence), rationalization (constructing plausible but false justifications), minimization (acknowledging but downplaying), and projection (attributing your own unacceptable traits to others). As a mental model: self-deception is the LARGEST obstacle to self-knowledge and effective decision-making because it operates BELOW conscious awareness.

This necessitates STRUCTURAL interventions (external data, accountability, behavioral tracking) that bypass the self-deceptive mechanism rather than trying to directly overcome it.

- **Foundations:** Rooted in psychoanalytic theory (Freud's defense mechanisms), cognitive dissonance theory (Festinger), and ego psychology. ### Sensemaking Bias · #1233 · `high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The cognitive tendency to construct coherent narratives and causal explanations from random or loosely connected events—seeing meaningful patterns where none exist. The brain is a SENSEMAKING MACHINE that abhors randomness and actively constructs explanations, even for noise. After-the-fact narratives make random outcomes feel inevitable ('of course the startup succeeded—they had X, Y, Z advantages'). Pattern-matching in random data produces superstitions, conspiracy theories, and overconfident causal claims. As a mental model: sensemaking bias reveals that our confidence in explanations is driven by the COHERENCE of the story rather than the VALIDITY of the evidence.
- **How it helps:** When an explanation feels compelling: check whether the compellingness comes from EVIDENCE or from NARRATIVE COHERENCE. The diagnostic: 'Would I find this explanation compelling if I only had the evidence and not the story?' For business: post-hoc explanations of success or failure almost always feel more coherent than the messy reality warranted. The practice: (1) Distrust explanations that are TOO coherent—reality is messier. (2) Check whether the same 'causal factors' also appear in failure cases (if so, they're not really causal). (3) Seek DISCONFIRMING evidence for compelling narratives. (4) Accept that some outcomes are primarily random, not primarily caused.
- **When to use:** When narrative coherence is being mistaken for causal validity; when post-hoc explanations need scrutiny for sensemaking bias; when pattern-matching in random data is producing false conclusions; when accepting randomness would be more accurate than constructing causal narratives.
- **Unique value:** Identifies that the brain's COMPULSION to make sense is itself a bias—one that makes us see causation in randomness, pattern in noise, and inevitability in chance. The confidence we feel in our explanations often reflects narrative coherence rather than evidential strength.
- **Limits:** Some patterns ARE real. Sensemaking is often adaptive and useful. Dismissing all narrative explanations as bias is overcorrective. The world isn't entirely random. Some causal explanations ARE accurate despite feeling too neat.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in cognitive anthropology and organizational sensemaking theory (Weick), cognitive psychology on schema-driven perception, and social constructivism (Berger & Luckmann). Draws on narrative cognition research, confirmation bias literature, and information processing models in judgment research.

### Emotional Arousal · #1238 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM

- **How it helps:** Explains why decisions made in anger, fear, or excitement are so often regretted. Provides the foundation for cooling-off periods, HALT checks, and the general principle of never making important decisions in heightened emotional states.

### Echo Chamber · #1242 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The information environment where beliefs are amplified and reinforced through repetition within a closed community that excludes dissenting views. Echo chambers form through: self-selection (people choose media and communities that confirm existing beliefs), algorithmic filtering (social media amplifies engaging content, which is often confirming content), and social reinforcement (expressing conforming views is rewarded; dissent is punished). As a mental model: echo chambers don't just FAIL to correct errors—they AMPLIFY them by making false beliefs seem universal and well-supported. The person inside the echo chamber experiences their beliefs as mainstream, moderate, and evidence-based—because every signal they receive confirms this.
- **How it helps:** Detect and escape echo chambers through deliberate information diversity. The practices: (1) AUDIT your information sources—if they all agree, you're probably in an echo chamber. (2) Seek STEELMAN versions of opposing views (the strongest version, not the straw man your echo chamber presents). (3) Follow thoughtful people who DISAGREE with you. (4) Apply the 'reasonable people disagree' test—if you can't imagine a reasonable person holding the opposing view, your echo chamber has distorted your perception of that view. For organizations: encourage cross-functional exposure and institutionalize Devil's Advocacy.
- **When to use:** When information environments may be amplifying rather than correcting beliefs; when perceived consensus actually reflects echo chamber dynamics; when belief diversity in one's information diet needs assessment; when organizational groupthink shows echo chamber characteristics.
- **Unique value:** Reveals that echo chambers don't just FILTER information—they TRANSFORM the epistemic experience: beliefs feel more certain, alternatives feel more extreme, and the community's consensus feels like universal truth. This experiential distortion is what makes echo chambers so difficult to recognize from the inside.
- **Limits:** Not all agreement indicates an echo chamber. Some communities share beliefs because the beliefs are well-supported. Deliberate exposure to opposing views can be unproductive. Echo chamber detection is easier to prescribe than practice. Information diversity has cognitive costs.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in social psychology (social influence, Cialdini), network theory on homophily (McPherson et al.), and media studies on filter bubbles (Pariser). Draws on information cocoon theory, selective exposure research, and sociology of knowledge. Related to group polarization (Sunstein) and epistemic bubbles.

- **What it is:** Physical pain and chemical substances (alcohol, drugs, medication, sleep deprivation-induced neurochemical changes) directly impair cognitive function, producing poor judgment that the person experiencing it often cannot recognize. Bevelin and Munger emphasize this as a non-obvious source of systematic decision error: people making decisions while in pain, medicated, hungover, or sleep-deprived are operating with degraded cognitive hardware—yet they feel like they're thinking normally.
- **How it helps:** Build structural protections against impaired decision-making: never make irreversible decisions when in pain, under the influence, or severely sleep-deprived. The insidious aspect is that impairment reduces your ability to recognize impairment—you feel clear-headed when you're not. The model can be used to dismiss decisions retroactively ('I was tired'). In some professional contexts (emergency medicine, military), you must make decisions under physical stress and can't always wait for optimal conditions.

### Asch Conformity Experiment · #1303 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Solomon Asch's 1951 study demonstrating that people will deny the evidence of their own eyes to conform with a group's incorrect answer. When confederates unanimously gave wrong answers about line lengths, 75% of participants conformed at least once—even when the correct answer was obvious. The study reveals that conformity pressure doesn't just affect opinions about ambiguous topics; it can override direct sensory perception. In organizations, this means teams can converge on wrong answers not because anyone is individually confused, but because no one wants to be the dissenter.
- **How it helps:** Design decision-making processes to protect against conformity effects. Concrete practices: have people write down their views before group discussion; assign a devil's advocate role; poll in reverse seniority order (junior before senior); use anonymous input methods for critical decisions. The Asch experiment shows that a single dissenter dramatically reduces conformity—so even one person willing to voice disagreement can break the spell.
- **When to use:** When group decisions feel unanimously obvious (unanimity is often a warning sign); when junior team members consistently agree with senior leaders; when designing decision processes for high-stakes choices; when a team has a track record of groupthink errors.
- **Unique value:** Provides experimental proof that conformity operates even for objective, easily verifiable facts—not just ambiguous opinions. If people will deny what their eyes tell them about line lengths, imagine how much conformity pressure affects genuinely ambiguous strategic decisions where there's no objectively right answer.
- **Limits:** Conformity varies significantly across cultures, personalities, and contexts. The laboratory setting may amplify conformity effects. Not all agreement is conformity—sometimes groups converge on the right answer. The desire to seem non-conformist can itself become a form of social pressure, leading to contrarianism for its own sake.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in social psychology (Asch, 1951–1956), conformity and social influence research, and social norms theory. Draws on normative versus informational influence distinction, evolutionary psychology on social learning, and organizational behavior on groupthink. Related to minority influence research (Moscovici).

### Stanford Prison Experiment · #1304 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Zimbardo's 1971 study demonstrating how rapidly normal people adopt abusive behaviors when placed in positions of authority within certain institutional structures. College students randomly assigned as 'guards' in a simulated prison began exhibiting cruel behavior within days, while 'prisoners' became passive and depressed. The study (despite methodological criticisms) illustrates a fundamental insight: situational forces and role assignments shape behavior far more powerfully than individual character, especially when institutional structures enable and reward certain behaviors.
- **How it helps:** When designing organizations, roles, and authority structures, assume that normal people will behave in ways that the structure rewards—not in ways that their 'character' would predict. If your organizational design gives people unchecked authority, expect some to abuse it, regardless of how good they seemed in interviews. The solution is structural: checks and balances, transparency, accountability mechanisms, and explicit norms—not relying on hiring 'good people' and hoping for the best.
- **When to use:** When designing authority structures, management hierarchies, or oversight systems; when 'bad behavior' appears in an organization and the instinct is to blame individuals; when evaluating whether organizational problems are 'people problems' or 'structure problems'; when building cultures that need to resist authority abuse.
- **Unique value:** Provides the most dramatic evidence for situation over character in explaining behavior. While the study has methodological limitations, its core insight is well-supported: people conform to the roles and structures they're placed in. This shifts intervention from 'fix the people' to 'fix the structure'—a more effective approach.
- **Limits:** The original study had serious methodological problems: small sample, experimenter involvement, selection effects. Replication attempts have shown mixed results. The situational explanation can become deterministic, denying individual agency and moral responsibility. Some individuals DID resist—character isn't irrelevant, just less predictive than structure.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in social psychology (Zimbardo, 1971), situationism versus dispositionism debate, and institutional sociology. Draws on role theory (Goffman), deindividuation research, and organizational behavior on how structural roles shape behavior. Related to Milgram's obedience research and Bandura's moral disengagement.

### Assumptive World Theory · #1464 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Ronnie Janoff-Bulman's Assumptive World Theory proposes that humans operate on three fundamental assumptions: the world is benevolent, the world is meaningful and predictable, and I am worthy. Traumatic loss shatters one or more of these assumptions, creating an existential crisis that goes beyond the specific loss itself. A random act of violence shatters 'the world is predictable.' Being betrayed by a trusted partner shatters 'the world is benevolent.' Being fired despite excellent performance shatters 'I am worthy.' The psychological work of recovery is not just grieving the specific loss but rebuilding these core assumptions—often in modified, more nuanced forms. People who held the strongest, most rigid assumptions before the loss experience the greatest shattering.
- **How it helps:** This model explains why some losses feel existential—why being laid off from a company you believed in can feel like the ground disappearing beneath you. It's not just the job; it's the assumption that 'good work gets rewarded' that shattered. For knowledge workers: (1) identify which core assumption a loss violated to understand the true scope of your distress, (2) rebuild assumptions in more resilient form—'the world is mostly benevolent, with exceptions' rather than 'the world is completely benevolent,' (3) recognize that others' extreme reactions to loss often reflect assumption-shattering, not overreaction, (4) deliberately stress-test your own assumptions before they're shattered.
- **When to use:** When a loss produces distress that seems disproportionate to the practical impact—the existential dimension may be the missing explanation. When rebuilding after trauma or betrayal and struggling with cynicism or lost trust. When you need to understand why some people recover from devastating losses while others are undone by seemingly minor ones.
- **Unique value:** Explains the existential dimension of loss that purely emotional or practical frameworks miss. Predicts which individuals are most vulnerable to specific types of loss based on their assumption strength. Provides a reconstruction target: not restoring old assumptions but building more resilient ones.
- **Limits:** The three core assumptions may not be universal across cultures. The theory better explains shattering than it prescribes reconstruction. Some critics argue it overemphasizes cognitive assumptions at the expense of embodied, somatic grief responses. Doesn't account for pre-existing trauma that may have already modified assumptions.
- **Foundations:** Founded on Parkes's assumptive world theory in attachment-informed bereavement research. Draws on cognitive schema theory (Piaget's assimilation/accommodation), constructivist psychology, and Janoff-Bulman's shattered assumptions theory on trauma's disruption of core beliefs.

### Imposter Syndrome Spiral · #2172 · `high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Beyond the basic concept of feeling like a fraud despite evidence of competence, the spiral model maps how imposter syndrome creates a self-reinforcing cycle that worsens over time: (1) initial success is attributed to luck, timing, or deception rather than ability, (2) to compensate, the person over-prepares and overworks, (3) success from over-preparation is attributed to the extra effort rather than underlying competence ('I only succeeded because I worked twice as hard'), (4) the person concludes they need to keep over-preparing to avoid exposure, (5) promotions and increased responsibility raise the stakes, increasing both the over-preparation and the fear. The spiral's cruelest feature: each success makes it worse, not better, because the person reasons 'Now there's more to lose and the gap between what people think I can do and what I actually can do is even larger.' Pauline Clance and Suzanne Imes identified five behavioral patterns: the perfectionist, the superwoman/man, the natural genius, the soloist, and the expert—each a different strategy for managing the same underlying fear.
- **How it helps:** Maps the specific self-reinforcing mechanism so interventions can target the right link in the chain. The critical intervention: break the attribution pattern. When you succeed, actively practice attributing it to ability rather than luck or effort. Keep a 'competence log'—documented evidence of situations where your skills, knowledge, or judgment produced the result. When the spiral voice says 'you just got lucky,' consult the log. Additional interventions: (1) normalize imposter feelings by discussing them openly (research shows 70% of people experience them), (2) seek feedback from trusted sources and practice believing it, (3) mentor others—teaching what you know makes your competence concrete and visible to yourself.
- **When to use:** When success increases anxiety rather than confidence. When you attribute your achievements to anything other than your abilities. When you're chronically over-preparing to avoid 'being found out.' When promotions feel more threatening than rewarding.
- **Unique value:** Goes beyond 'imposter syndrome exists' to model the spiral dynamics that make it worsen over time and specifically with more success. The 'competence log' intervention targets the root attribution error rather than offering reassurance.
- **Limits:** Sometimes people are genuinely in over their heads and the feeling of being underqualified is accurate, not imposter syndrome. The model can discourage honest self-assessment. Cultural factors (gender, race, class) create real barriers that imposter feelings may partially reflect.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in Clance & Imes (1978) original imposter phenomenon research, attribution theory (Weiner), and self-efficacy theory (Bandura). Draws on stereotype threat (Steele), cognitive distortion research in CBT, and socialization research on gender and competence attribution.

### Decision Fatigue Cascade · #2194 · `high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Roy Baumeister's research showing that decision quality deteriorates predictably as the number of decisions increases, with each decision depleting a finite cognitive resource. The cascade model extends this: (1) early decisions deplete executive function, (2) subsequent decisions default to either impulsive choices (choosing the easiest option) or decision avoidance (choosing the status quo or deferring), (3) the poor decisions create problems that require more decisions, (4) the additional decisions further deplete resources, (5) a cascade of increasingly poor decisions follows. The famous study: Israeli judges granted parole at 65% after meals but near 0% before meals—not because they became harsher but because the default (deny parole) required less cognitive effort. The cascade is especially dangerous in environments that require many sequential decisions: emergency rooms, trading floors, executive calendars, and anyone managing multiple projects simultaneously. The implication: your most important decisions should never be your last decisions of the day.
- **How it helps:** Provides specific scheduling and structural interventions. (1) Schedule your most consequential decisions for your highest-energy periods (usually morning). (2) Reduce trivial decisions through routines and automation (the Steve Jobs wardrobe rationale). (3) Never make important decisions when you're already depleted—build in recovery breaks. (4) Implement default options for routine decisions so they don't consume executive function. (5) Recognize the warning signs of fatigue: choosing the easiest option rather than the best, deferring decisions that should be made now, and increasing irritability. The structural fix: design decision architectures that put fewer decisions on any individual.
- **When to use:** When decision quality seems to decline throughout the day. When you notice defaulting to easy options late in the day. When designing workflows that require many sequential decisions. When planning important meetings or negotiations.
- **Unique value:** The cascade model extends simple decision fatigue to show how it compounds—bad decisions create problems that require more decisions. This self-reinforcing dynamic explains why some days spiral into chaos. The scheduling intervention is immediately actionable.
- **Limits:** The ego depletion model underlying decision fatigue has faced replication challenges in recent research. Individual variation in fatigue susceptibility is large. Some people make better decisions under time pressure. Not all sequential decision-making produces fatigue.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in Baumeister's ego depletion research, decision fatigue experiments (Danziger's parole judge study), and glucose-cognition research. Draws on dual-process theory (Kahneman), resource depletion models, and time-of-day effects on cognitive performance research.

### Motivated Reasoning Spiral · #2202 · `high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The self-reinforcing process by which desired conclusions shape the reasoning that's supposed to evaluate them. Dan Kahan's research shows that higher intelligence and scientific literacy actually increase motivated reasoning—smarter people are better at constructing sophisticated justifications for what they already believe. The spiral: (1) you have a desired conclusion (usually emotionally or identity-motivated), (2) you search for evidence that supports it, (3) you apply rigorous scrutiny to disconfirming evidence but accept confirming evidence uncritically, (4) the asymmetric scrutiny produces a biased evidence base, (5) the biased evidence base strengthens your conviction, (6) stronger conviction increases the asymmetry of scrutiny. The spiral is terminal because each iteration makes the person feel more justified—they have 'done the research' and 'considered the evidence.' The deeper problem: motivated reasoning is invisible from the inside because the reasoning process feels effortful and genuine. You're not lying to yourself; you're doing bad science with total sincerity.
- **How it helps:** Provides interventions targeted at the asymmetric scrutiny mechanism. (1) Apply 'ideological Turing test' (Bryan Caplan): can you argue the opposing position so convincingly that supporters would mistake you for one of them? If not, you haven't understood it. (2) 'Consider the opposite': for every important conclusion, spend equal time building the strongest case against it. (3) Identify the emotional or identity stake in your conclusion—what do you want to be true? That's where motivated reasoning lives. (4) Seek out your 'intellectual adversaries'—people who are smart, informed, and disagree with you. (5) Ask: 'If the evidence pointed the other way, would I change my mind?' If honestly no, you're in the spiral.
- **When to use:** When you feel very certain about a conclusion that also happens to be convenient or identity-confirming. When smart people disagree with you and you can't understand why. When evaluating evidence for decisions where you have emotional stakes. During any analysis where objectivity matters.
- **Unique value:** Kahan's finding that intelligence amplifies motivated reasoning rather than correcting it is crucial—it means the standard remedy ('think harder') makes the problem worse. The asymmetric scrutiny mechanism provides a specific target for intervention.
- **Limits:** Not all reasoning toward preferred conclusions is 'motivated'—sometimes the preferred conclusion is also the correct one. The model can create excessive self-doubt about all strongly held beliefs. Identifying motivated reasoning in others is much easier than in yourself.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in Kunda's motivated reasoning research (1990), confirmation bias literature (Nickerson), and dual-process theory (Evans). Draws on identity-protective cognition (Kahan), emotional reasoning in CBT, and directional vs. accuracy goals in cognitive processing research.

- **What it is:** Marshall McLuhan's insight that the medium through which content is delivered shapes the message more fundamentally than the content itself. ### Double Bind Communication (Bateson) · #2227 · `llm-high` · communication · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Gregory Bateson's concept from schizophrenia research: a communication pattern where someone receives two contradictory messages simultaneously, cannot comment on the contradiction, and cannot escape the situation. The classic form: a parent tells a child 'come give me a hug' with stiff, rejecting body language—approaching is punished (by the body language) and not approaching is punished (by the verbal instruction). The recipient cannot win, cannot point out the contradiction, and cannot leave. In organizational contexts: 'Take risks and innovate! (But don't fail.)' 'Be honest with me! (But don't say anything I don't want to hear.)' 'Work-life balance is important! (But everyone who gets promoted works weekends.)' The double bind produces anxiety, confusion, and eventual learned helplessness because every response is wrong. The pattern is especially destructive when there's a power differential—the person in the lower-power position can't call out the contradiction without risk.
- **How it helps:** Provides a framework for identifying and naming impossible communication situations, which is itself the primary intervention—double binds survive by being invisible. (1) When you feel confused, anxious, and unable to respond correctly no matter what, check for contradictory messages. (2) Name the bind explicitly: 'I'm hearing X but seeing Y—which should I respond to?' This breaks the meta-communicative taboo that maintains the bind. (3) When you're the one sending double binds, check for alignment between your verbal and non-verbal messages, and between your stated values and your actual rewards. ### Adverse Selection · #171 · `high` · economics · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The market failure that occurs when information asymmetry causes the WORSE options to dominate the market—because the party with more information self-selects in ways that disadvantage the less-informed party. (3) Mandatory participation: prevent high-quality options from self-selecting out (universal insurance mandates). In hiring: adverse selection means the applicants most eager for your job may not be the best ones—the best may already have options.
- **When to use:** When markets are deteriorating in quality (the good options are leaving); when insurance or risk pools are becoming adversely selected; when hiring processes are attracting the wrong candidates; when any exchange involves information asymmetry between buyer and seller.

- **Limits:** Not all markets with information asymmetry fail through adverse selection. Signaling and screening mechanisms can mitigate the problem. ### Perverse Incentive · #212 · `llm-high` · economics · CLAIM
- **What it is:** An incentive structure that produces the opposite of its intended effect—rewarding the behavior the incentive was designed to discourage, or discouraging the behavior it was meant to encourage. The Cobra Effect: British colonial India offered bounties for dead cobras; entrepreneurs bred cobras for the bounty; when the program ended, they released the cobras—increasing the cobra population. Rewarding programmers for lines of code produces bloated code. Perverse incentives are inevitable when incentive design fails to anticipate strategic behavior.
- **How it helps:** For any incentive system: simulate how a SELF-INTERESTED OPTIMIZER would exploit it—because that's what will happen. Before implementing: ask 'if someone wanted to maximize their reward while minimizing genuine contribution, how would they game this?' Then redesign to prevent the gaming. The key practices: (1) Measure outcomes, not inputs (results, not activity). (2) Use multiple metrics to prevent optimization of one at the expense of others. (3) Include qualitative assessment alongside quantitative metrics. (4) Review and revise incentive structures regularly as gaming strategies evolve. - **When to use:** When incentive programs produce unexpected or counterproductive behavior; when people seem to be gaming the system rather than pursuing its intended goals; when designing compensation, bonus, or reward structures; when Goodhart's Law is turning metrics into gamed targets.
- **Unique value:** Provides the specific mechanism by which well-intentioned incentive design backfires: strategic optimization by self-interested agents who find ways to maximize the reward while minimizing the intended behavior. - **Limits:** Not all incentive gaming is perverse—some optimization IS the intended behavior. Designing gaming-proof incentives is extremely difficult. Excessive anti-gaming measures can make systems too complex. Some perverse incentives are tolerable costs of otherwise useful systems. People are not purely self-interested—intrinsic motivation can override perverse incentives.

### Ponzi Dynamic Recognition · #2241 · `llm-high` · economics · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The structural pattern underlying all Ponzi schemes: returns to existing participants are funded by capital from new participants rather than by actual value creation, making the scheme mathematically certain to collapse once new participant growth slows. The recognition pattern goes beyond 'too good to be true' returns to identify specific structural markers: (1) returns are unusually consistent (real investments have variance; fabricated ones don't), (2) the investment strategy is vague or described as proprietary, (3) difficulty withdrawing funds (because the funds have already been used to pay others), (4) affinity-based recruiting (targeting community trust networks), (5) growing urgency to recruit new participants. Madoff's scheme exhibited all five markers for decades before collapse. The pattern extends beyond explicit fraud: many legitimate businesses have Ponzi-like dynamics when their business model depends on continuous new customer acquisition to fund returns to existing customers (early Uber subsidized rides from investor capital; some SaaS metrics disguise churn by offsetting with new sign-ups). The mathematical inevitability: any system that pays returns from new capital rather than value creation requires exponential growth, which is impossible to sustain.
- **How it helps:** Provides a five-marker checklist for evaluating whether any investment, business, or system has Ponzi characteristics. (1) Consistency test: are returns suspiciously stable? Real assets produce variable returns. (2) Opacity test: can the source of returns be independently verified? (3) Liquidity test: can you get your money out on normal terms? (4) Growth dependency test: does the system require continuous new participants to function? (5) Exponential test: does the math require impossible growth rates to sustain? Apply these five tests to any investment opportunity, especially those marketed through trust networks. The broader application: any business metric that's funded by growth rather than value creation has Ponzi dynamics.
- **When to use:** When evaluating investment opportunities, especially those with unusually attractive returns. When assessing business models that depend heavily on new customer acquisition. When investigating whether financial results are generated by value creation or capital cycling. During due diligence on any financial entity.
- **Unique value:** The five-marker checklist is more practical than 'be skeptical of high returns' because it identifies specific structural features. The extension to legitimate-but-Ponzi-like business dynamics (growth-funded metrics) applies the pattern to everyday business evaluation.
- **Limits:** Not all consistent returns are Ponzi schemes (some strategies genuinely produce stable returns). Not all growth-dependent businesses are fraudulent. The model can create excessive paranoia about legitimate investments. Early-stage businesses often look Ponzi-like by these metrics even when legitimate.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in Minsky's financial instability hypothesis, Ponzi finance as category in credit cycle research, and fraud psychology (Cressey's fraud triangle). Draws on behavioral economics of momentum investing, greater fool theory, and contagion dynamics in financial markets.

### Chain Reaction · #642 · `high` · physics · CLAIM
- **What it is:** A process where the products of one reaction trigger additional reactions in a self-sustaining or accelerating cascade—each event causes one or more subsequent events, producing exponential growth until a limiting factor intervenes. The critical question: what's the reproduction rate? If each event triggers more than one subsequent event (R>1), you have exponential dynamics.
- **When to use:** When growth or decline seems to accelerate rather than stay linear; when one failure is triggering additional failures; when designing systems that need protection against cascading failure; when building products or movements that need viral dynamics.

- **Foundations:** Rooted in nuclear physics (fission chain reactions) and nonlinear dynamics. ### Spacing Effect · #693 · `llm-high` · productivity · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The robust cognitive finding that distributing learning over time (spaced practice) produces dramatically better long-term retention than concentrating it in a single session (massed practice/cramming). Studying a topic for 30 minutes across 6 days produces far more lasting knowledge than studying for 3 hours in one day—despite identical total time. The spacing effect works because each retrieval after a gap strengthens the memory trace and forces deeper processing than re-reading in the same session. It's one of the most replicated findings in cognitive science.
- **How it helps:** Restructure all learning and skill development around spaced repetition. Use tools like Anki for factual knowledge, schedule skill practice in distributed sessions rather than marathon blocks, and revisit important concepts at increasing intervals (1 day, 3 days, 1 week, 2 weeks, 1 month). For organizations: replace day-long training events with distributed learning programs. For yourself: 20 minutes of daily practice beats a 3-hour weekend session for any skill. The spacing effect is the highest-leverage learning technique available—it multiplies retention per hour of study invested.
- **When to use:** When learning new material that needs to be retained long-term; when training programs aren't producing lasting behavior change; when skill development has plateaued despite significant practice time; when designing learning systems, onboarding programs, or educational content.
- **Unique value:** The single highest-ROI learning technique: simply distributing the same amount of study time over more sessions dramatically increases retention without increasing effort. Most learning systems are designed around massed practice (classes, workshops, bootcamps) because it's logistically convenient, not because it works—the spacing effect reveals this as a massive, systematic waste of learning time.
- **Limits:** Spaced practice requires more planning and discipline than massed practice. It's harder to schedule organizationally. For some types of learning (complex skills requiring sustained immersion), massed practice has advantages. The optimal spacing interval depends on the material and the desired retention period. Spaced repetition tools work best for factual knowledge; complex understanding may require different approaches.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in Ebbinghaus's forgetting curve research (1885) and spaced repetition theory. Draws on distributed practice research, Bahrick's permastore concept, and Bjork's new theory of disuse. Related to desirable difficulties, long-term memory consolidation neuroscience, and testing effect amplification.

### Turkey Problem (Thanksgiving Turkey) · #940 · `llm-high` · risk · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Taleb's parable illustrating the danger of inferring safety from a track record of safety. A turkey is fed every day for 1,000 days, each day confirming its model that 'the farmer is a friend.' On day 1,001—Thanksgiving—the model catastrophically fails. The turkey's confidence was highest at the moment of maximum danger. Every day of feeding was 'evidence' that made the turkey more confident in a model that was about to be violently disproven. In business and investing, long periods of stability can mask building fragility.
- **How it helps:** When a strategy, investment, or system has a long track record of success, ask: am I the turkey? Is this track record evidence of genuine safety, or evidence that I haven't yet encountered the event that will destroy me? The turkey problem is especially dangerous because traditional risk metrics (volatility, variance) look BEST right before catastrophe—the system appears most stable when it's most fragile. Look for hidden fragility beneath surface stability: leverage building up, concentration increasing, or single points of failure accumulating.
- **When to use:** When a long track record of success is being used to justify continued confidence; when risk metrics show low volatility (which may reflect hidden fragility, not true safety); when evaluating any system that has never been tested by extreme conditions; when 'this has always worked' is the primary justification for a strategy.
- **Unique value:** The most visceral illustration of why the past doesn't predict the future in fat-tailed domains. Unlike abstract statistical arguments, the turkey parable makes the danger of inductive reasoning emotionally immediate. It permanently changes how you evaluate track records.
- **Limits:** Not every long track record is a turkey situation—some strategies genuinely are robust. The parable can create excessive paranoia about any period of stability. The model identifies the problem but doesn't specify how to distinguish genuine safety from turkey-style fragility. Some amount of inductive reasoning is unavoidable and useful.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in Hume's problem of induction and philosophy of science, Taleb's synthesis with complex systems research, and behavioral economics research on overconfidence in extrapolation. Draws on statistical theory (out-of-sample prediction failure), organizational learning research, and evolutionary epistemology.

### Comparison Trap (Social Comparison Spiral) · #2159 · `high` · social · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Leon Festinger's social comparison theory extended to its failure mode: the systematic destruction of satisfaction through upward comparison, amplified by social media's highlight-reel effect. The trap's mechanism: (1) you compare on a single dimension (wealth, attractiveness, career success), (2) you compare your behind-the-scenes to their highlight reel, (3) you feel inadequate, (4) you strive harder, (5) upon achieving more, your reference group shifts upward, (6) the gap feels identical. The cruelest feature: comparison is automatic and pre-conscious—you can't stop doing it, only notice when you're doing it. Social media turbo-charges the trap by making upward comparison constant and multi-dimensional—simultaneously comparing your body, career, relationship, parenting, travel, home, and creativity against the best exemplar in each category. Nobody can win against a composite of everyone's best dimension. The paradox: the more you achieve, the more rarefied your comparison group, making the trap tighter, not looser.
- **How it helps:** Provides specific interventions beyond 'stop comparing yourself to others' (which is like saying 'stop breathing'). (1) Curate your information environment—unfollow accounts that trigger upward comparison without providing genuine value. (2) Practice 'temporal comparison'—compare yourself to your past self rather than current others. (3) Build 'comparison awareness'—when you feel suddenly inadequate, ask 'who did I just compare myself to?' The awareness itself reduces the comparison's emotional power. (4) Deliberately seek the full picture of people you envy—understanding their struggles and tradeoffs breaks the highlight-reel illusion. (5) Choose comparison dimensions that align with your values, not society's defaults.
- **When to use:** When scrolling social media leaves you feeling inadequate. When achievement doesn't produce satisfaction because someone else achieved more. When you can't enjoy your own life because of awareness of others' lives. During any period of self-evaluation.
- **Unique value:** Goes beyond 'comparison is the thief of joy' to explain the specific mechanism (highlight reel vs. behind-the-scenes, upward reference group shift) and provides environment-design interventions rather than willpower-based solutions.
- **Limits:** Some comparison is healthy and motivating—it inspires improvement and calibrates expectations. Eliminating all comparison can lead to complacency. The model can encourage isolation from others' achievements. Downward comparison can produce problematic smugness.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in Festinger's social comparison theory (1954), relative deprivation theory (Runciman), and social identity theory (Tajfel & Turner). Draws on Veblen's conspicuous consumption, envy research in behavioral economics, and social media effects literature.

### Contempt Spiral (Gottman's Terminal Pattern) · #2173 · `high` · social · CLAIM
- **What it is:** John Gottman's research identifies contempt—the expression of superiority and disgust toward a partner—as the single most reliable predictor of relationship dissolution, with 93% accuracy. The spiral mechanism: (1) unresolved complaints harden into criticism (attacking character rather than behavior), (2) criticism triggers defensiveness, (3) persistent criticism without repair transforms into contempt (eye-rolling, name-calling, mockery, dismissive body language), (4) contempt triggers emotional withdrawal (stonewalling), (5) each cycle intensifies the contempt because the perceived failures accumulate without repair. The spiral is terminal because contempt corrodes the foundation of respect that makes repair possible—you can't resolve conflict with someone you fundamentally look down on. The physiological signature: Gottman measures 'diffuse physiological arousal' (heart rate >100 BPM) during conflict, which triggers fight-or-flight and makes constructive dialogue neurologically impossible. Contempt isn't just an emotion—it's a relationship neurotoxin.
- **How it helps:** Provides the earliest possible warning system for relationship failure. Prevention: (1) monitor for contempt expressions in yourself—sarcasm, eye-rolling, and 'you always/never' language are early signs, (2) maintain a 5:1 ratio of positive to negative interactions (Gottman's 'magic ratio'), (3) practice 'gentle startup' in complaints (describe behavior + your feeling + your need, not character attack), (4) build a 'culture of appreciation' through daily expressed gratitude, (5) when contempt surfaces, treat it as a code red requiring immediate attention—couples therapy, extended conversation, or structured repair. The key: contempt responds to intervention early in the spiral but becomes treatment-resistant late.
- **When to use:** When relationship conflicts feel increasingly bitter and personal. When you notice sarcasm replacing genuine communication. When respect for your partner is eroding. When you or your partner have started using dismissive language or gestures.
- **Unique value:** Gottman's research provides empirical precision that most relationship advice lacks. The 93% prediction accuracy and the specific behavioral markers (eye-rolling, dismissive tone) make this scientifically grounded rather than folk wisdom. The 5:1 ratio is a measurable target.
- **Limits:** The model was developed primarily from heterosexual couples research, though later studies extended to same-sex couples. Cultural expression of contempt varies. Some contempt is a response to genuinely contemptible behavior (abuse, betrayal). Not all criticism spirals into contempt.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in Gottman's longitudinal marriage research (Four Horsemen model), social psychology of contempt (Ekman's microexpressions), and escalation research. Draws on intergroup hostility literature, dehumanization research, and affect regulation theory in close relationships.

### Stonewalling Dynamics (Gottman's Fourth Horseman) · #2175 · `llm-high` · social · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Emotional and communicative shutdown during conflict—going blank, refusing to engage, leaving the room, or giving monosyllabic non-responses. Gottman identifies stonewalling as the fourth horseman of relationship apocalypse, typically emerging after prolonged exposure to criticism and contempt. The physiology is key: stonewalling is usually not a deliberate strategy but a flooding response—the nervous system becomes overwhelmed (heart rate exceeds 100 BPM, cortisol spikes), and the brain's rational processing literally shuts down. The person physically cannot engage constructively. The damage: the stonewalled partner experiences abandonment and invisibility ('I don't matter enough for you to even engage'), which triggers more intense pursuing, which triggers more flooding and shutdown. Over time, stonewalling becomes a habitual avoidance strategy that extends beyond conflict—the person learns to preemptively shut down before difficult topics even arise, creating emotional deserts in the relationship.
- **How it helps:** Understanding the physiological basis transforms stonewalling from 'you don't care' to 'your nervous system is overwhelmed,' which changes the intervention entirely. The key: stonewalling requires a systems-level intervention—both partners must change behavior simultaneously.
- **When to use:** When one partner goes blank or leaves during arguments. When emotional shutdown has become the default response to conflict. When important topics keep getting avoided because they trigger shutdown. When one partner feels invisible during disagreements.
- **Unique value:** The physiological explanation (flooding response) destigmatizes stonewalling and makes it treatable. The structured time-out protocol with a return commitment addresses both partners' needs simultaneously—space for the flooder, reassurance for the abandoned.
- **Limits:** Some stonewalling is strategic manipulation, not physiological flooding. The model can excuse avoidance as 'I was flooded' when engagement was possible. In abusive relationships, leaving may be self-protection, not stonewalling. The 20-minute time-out research is based on averages.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in Gottman's physiological flooding research, polyvagal theory (Porges), and affect regulation literature. Draws on fight-flight-freeze response research, attachment theory's avoidant strategies, and clinical research on emotional shutdown in couples therapy.

### Swiss Cheese Model (Error Chain) · #1074 · `llm-high` · strategy · CLAIM
- **What it is:** James Reason's model of accident causation: catastrophic failures don't result from a single error but from MULTIPLE failures aligning—like holes in sequential slices of Swiss cheese lining up to allow a hazard to pass through every defense layer. Each layer has weaknesses (holes), but normally the other layers catch what one layer misses. Catastrophe occurs only when holes in EVERY layer align simultaneously. The layers include: organizational culture, supervision, preconditions, and individual acts. As a mental model: the Swiss Cheese Model reveals that safety depends on REDUNDANT, INDEPENDENT defense layers—and that catastrophic failure requires the simultaneous breakdown of multiple defenses, not just one error.
- **How it helps:** Design multi-layered defenses and ensure layer independence. The practices: (1) Identify your defense layers: what systems, checks, and safeguards prevent failure? (2) Ensure layers are INDEPENDENT: if one failure can compromise multiple layers simultaneously, they're not truly redundant. (3) Regularly assess each layer's 'holes': what conditions cause this defense to fail? (4) Monitor for alignment: when multiple layers are weakened simultaneously, risk escalates dramatically. (5) After near-misses: analyze which layers held and which had holes—near-misses are evidence of hole alignment that stopped one layer short of catastrophe.
- **When to use:** When safety or quality depends on redundant defense layers; when understanding why catastrophes require multiple simultaneous failures; when near-miss analysis would reveal defense layer weaknesses; when defense layer independence needs verification.
- **Unique value:** Reveals that CATASTROPHIC FAILURE requires not one error but MANY aligned errors—making redundancy and layer independence the primary defenses. This transforms safety analysis from 'prevent all errors' (impossible) to 'ensure independent layers prevent error alignment' (achievable).
- **Limits:** The model can produce complacency ('we have many layers'). Layer independence is hard to verify. Some failures are genuinely single-point. The model applies better to engineered systems than to organic ones. Defense layers have costs.
- **Foundations:** Developed by psychologist James Reason in human error and organizational accident theory. Rooted in systems safety engineering and sociotechnical systems theory. Draws on cognitive psychology of error, redundancy theory in reliability engineering, and organizational learning literature.

### Tyranny of Small Decisions · #1213 · `llm-high` · strategy · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Alfred Kahn's concept that a series of individually rational, small decisions can aggregate into a collectively irrational, large outcome that NO individual decision-maker would have chosen if presented with the total result in advance. Each homeowner paves their yard for convenience → the neighborhood loses all green space. Each airline cuts costs slightly → the flying experience becomes intolerable. Each workday extends by 15 minutes → a 70-hour work week emerges. The tyranny: no single decision seems consequential, so each passes without deliberation—but the CUMULATIVE effect is a dramatic, unwanted transformation that no one chose.
- **How it helps:** Protect against the tyranny of small decisions through: (1) AGGREGATE MONITORING: regularly assess the cumulative effect of many small decisions ('what has the sum of our small cost-cutting decisions done to product quality?'). (2) Bright lines: establish non-negotiable thresholds that can't be eroded incrementally ('we will never work more than X hours/week, regardless of individual requests'). (3) Periodic full-view assessment: step back and ask 'would we choose this outcome if we were deciding from scratch?' If not: the tyranny of small decisions has produced an outcome that needs deliberate correction.
- **When to use:** When cumulative small decisions have produced large unwanted outcomes; when incremental erosion is degrading quality, culture, or standards; when bright lines need establishment to prevent small-decision tyranny; when aggregate monitoring would reveal what individual decision-making has obscured.
- **Unique value:** Identifies the specific mechanism by which UNWANTED macro-outcomes emerge from RATIONAL micro-decisions: each small decision is individually reasonable, but the aggregate is a destination no one would have chosen. This makes cumulative-effect monitoring as important as individual-decision quality.
- **Limits:** Not all cumulative effects are negative. Monitoring every small decision is impractical. Bright lines can be too rigid. The tyranny can be difficult to reverse once established. Some gradual changes are genuinely desirable.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in social choice theory, public choice economics, and Kahn's (1966) original formulation on the tyranny of small decisions. Draws on systems thinking, behavioral economics on present bias and salience, and institutional economics on path dependence and cumulative causation (Myrdal).
- *Secondary roles:* PATTERN · *Collection:* anti-pattern

### Hydra Effect · #1240 · `llm-high` · systems · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The systemic phenomenon where attempting to suppress or destroy a problem MULTIPLIES it—named after the mythological Hydra that grew two heads for every one cut off. Real-world examples: cracking down on a drug network scatters dealers to new territories, expanding the market. Censoring information draws attention to it (Streisand Effect). Antibiotics that don't kill all bacteria breed resistant strains. As a mental model: some problems have structures that CONVERT suppression into proliferation—and applying force to such problems makes them worse. The key diagnostic: does the problem's structure allow it to replicate or scatter when attacked? If yes: direct suppression will produce Hydra dynamics.
- **How it helps:** Before attacking any problem directly: assess whether the problem structure would convert suppression into proliferation. The diagnostic: (1) Can the problem REPLICATE when pressured? (2) Can the problem SCATTER to new locations when concentrated effort is applied? (3) Does attacking the problem generate ATTENTION or SYMPATHY that helps it grow? If yes to any: use indirect strategies instead—address root causes, change the environment that sustains the problem, or starve it of resources rather than attacking it directly. For organizations: some cultural problems get worse when directly addressed because the attention amplifies them.
- **When to use:** When direct suppression of a problem risks making it worse; when problems have structures that convert attack into proliferation; when indirect strategies would be more effective than direct confrontation; when the Streisand Effect or similar amplification dynamics need anticipation.
- **Unique value:** Identifies the specific STRUCTURAL CONDITION that makes problems grow when attacked: the ability to replicate, scatter, or gain attention from suppression efforts. This diagnostic prevents the most counterproductive intervention type: applying force to problems that feed on force.
- **Limits:** Not all problems exhibit Hydra dynamics. Some problems DO respond to direct suppression. Identifying Hydra structure in advance is difficult. Indirect strategies are slower. The concept can justify inaction when direct action is needed.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in systems thinking and feedback loop theory, Greek mythology (Lernaean Hydra), and policy analysis on perverse intervention effects. Draws on complexity economics, Whack-a-Mole dynamics in organizational theory, and Merton's unintended consequences. Related to compensatory behavior in behavioral economics.

### Common Cause Failure · #2215 · `high` · systems · CLAIM
- **What it is:** When redundant or independent safety systems fail simultaneously because they share a vulnerability that was assumed not to exist. Unlike cascade failures (where one failure causes the next), common cause failures involve multiple components failing independently for the same root reason. The Fukushima disaster is the defining example: backup generators were designed to be redundant, but they all shared the same vulnerability—placement at flood level—so the tsunami disabled all of them simultaneously. The pattern: (1) safety is designed with redundancy (multiple independent barriers), (2) the independence assumption is wrong—components share a common vulnerability (location, manufacturer, software version, maintenance crew, power source), (3) an event that exploits the shared vulnerability defeats all redundant layers simultaneously, (4) the system experiences the exact total failure that redundancy was supposed to prevent. The insidious quality: common cause failures turn redundancy from a strength into an illusion. You think you have four layers of defense; you actually have one, duplicated four times.
- **How it helps:** Transforms redundancy design from 'add more backup' to 'ensure backups are genuinely independent.' The intervention: (1) for every redundant system, map shared dependencies: same power source? same vendor? same physical location? same maintenance team? same software? (2) apply 'diversity' principle: use different technologies, different vendors, different locations for redundant systems, (3) stress-test the independence assumption: 'What single event could take out both the primary and backup systems?' (4) include 'common cause' as a specific category in failure mode analysis. The rule: if your backup shares any single vulnerability with your primary, it's not a true backup.
- **When to use:** When designing redundant safety systems. When evaluating whether backup systems are genuinely independent. When investigating failures that defeated supposedly redundant defenses. During risk assessment of critical infrastructure.
- **Unique value:** Reveals that adding redundancy can create a dangerous illusion of safety if the redundant systems share hidden dependencies. The shared dependency audit is a specific, practical technique that's absent from most redundancy discussions.
- **Limits:** Achieving true independence between redundant systems is expensive and sometimes technically impossible. Some shared vulnerabilities are acceptable given their probability. Over-engineering for common cause can be costlier than the risk it prevents.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in reliability engineering's common cause failure analysis, fault tree analysis methodology, and systems safety. Draws on interdependency research, diversity-redundancy tradeoff in system design, and correlated failure research in infrastructure engineering and finance.

### System Migration to the Edge (Rasmussen) · #2217 · `llm-high` · systems · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Jens Rasmussen's model of how systems migrate toward the boundary of acceptable performance under three simultaneous pressures: (1) pressure toward least effort (workers optimize for efficiency), (2) pressure toward financial targets (management optimizes for cost), (3) pressure from the performance gradient (the boundary of unacceptable performance creates a wall that systems bounce against). The system settles into a dynamic equilibrium near the edge of the safety envelope, where normal operational variation can push it over the boundary into failure. The model explains why accidents cluster at the edge of acceptable performance rather than occurring randomly: the system is systematically pushed toward the edge by the same forces that make it efficient. NASA's operations before both Challenger and Columbia drifted to the edge of thermal protection margins. BP's operations before Deepwater Horizon drifted to the edge of well control margins. The drift is not malicious; it's rational response to persistent pressures.
- **How it helps:** Provides a systems-level explanation for why safety interventions that don't address the underlying pressures will fail—the system will migrate back to the edge because the pressures remain. Interventions: (1) make the safety boundary visible and measurable, not just procedural, (2) create counter-pressures that push back from the edge (safety incentives, margin monitoring), (3) build 'hard stops' that prevent migration past critical thresholds, (4) monitor the trajectory, not just the current position—are you closer to the edge than last year? (5) address the three pressures directly rather than trying to override them with rules. The key: you can't prevent edge migration through procedures alone; you must change the pressure landscape.
- **When to use:** When investigating why a system that was previously safe became unsafe. When safety improvements don't stick. When production pressure consistently overrides safety culture. When designing resilient systems in competitive or resource-constrained environments.
- **Unique value:** Rasmussen's three-pressure model explains why edge migration is a law of organizational physics, not a management failure. This reframing from 'bad management' to 'structural inevitability' leads to structural solutions rather than blame-based interventions.
- **Limits:** Some organizations maintain safety margins effectively through culture and leadership. The model can encourage fatalism about safety. Not all three pressures are equally strong in every context. The 'edge' isn't always well-defined.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in Rasmussen's AcciMap and risk homeostasis research, systems safety theory, and migration to boundary research. Draws on normalization of deviance (Vaughan), drift in complex systems, and safety science's shift from component to systemic accident causation models.

### Complexity Catastrophe (Kauffman) · #2218 · `llm-high` · systems · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Stuart Kauffman's concept from evolutionary biology applied to organizational and technical systems: beyond a critical threshold of interconnection and interdependency, adding more components or connections decreases rather than increases system fitness. In Kauffman's NK models, when each component depends on too many other components (high K), the fitness landscape becomes so rugged that the system gets trapped in local optima and cannot improve through incremental change. The organizational translation: when everything depends on everything else, changing anything requires changing everything, so nothing changes. The pattern: (1) a system grows by adding components and connections, (2) each addition makes sense locally (new feature, new integration, new dependency), (3) beyond the complexity threshold, the system's ability to adapt drops precipitously, (4) attempted improvements create as many problems as they solve, (5) the system enters a state where no incremental change improves it but radical simplification is too risky. Legacy enterprise software, government bureaucracies, and highly matrixed organizations often exhibit complexity catastrophe.
- **How it helps:** Provides a specific diagnostic: when attempted improvements consistently create as many new problems as they solve, you may have crossed the complexity threshold. Interventions: (1) modularize—reduce the number of dependencies between components so changes can be localized, (2) periodically prune connections: 'Which integrations and dependencies can we remove?' (3) set complexity budgets: 'How many dependencies can this component have before it needs to be split?' (4) track the 'problem creation rate' of changes—if improvements are generating more issues than they resolve, the system is too complex. The radical intervention: sometimes the only path forward is to build a new, simpler system alongside the complex one and gradually migrate.
- **When to use:** When system improvements consistently create new problems. When no incremental change seems to help. When the system feels 'stuck' despite talented people working on it. When evaluating legacy system modernization.
- **Unique value:** Kauffman's NK model provides a mathematically grounded explanation for why complex systems reach a point where they can't be improved incrementally. The 'problem creation rate' metric gives a measurable indicator of complexity catastrophe.
- **Limits:** The NK model is a simplified abstraction that doesn't capture all aspects of real-world systems. Some highly interconnected systems work well (the internet, ecosystems). The threshold between productive complexity and catastrophic complexity is hard to determine in advance.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in Kauffman's NK complexity model, complexity theory, and fitness landscape research in evolutionary biology applied to organizations. Draws on rugged landscape metaphors, adaptive management research, and edge-of-chaos dynamics in complex adaptive systems theory.

### Maintenance Neglect Spiral · #2221 · `high` · systems · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The self-reinforcing pattern where deferred maintenance creates escalating costs that further pressure maintenance budgets, accelerating deterioration. The mechanism: (1) maintenance is deferred to save costs or allocate resources to other priorities, (2) the system continues functioning (confirming the 'wisdom' of deferral), (3) deferred maintenance accumulates as hidden liability, (4) components begin failing, requiring emergency repairs that cost 5-10x scheduled maintenance, (5) emergency costs crowd out remaining preventive maintenance budget, (6) more maintenance is deferred, accelerating the spiral. U.S. infrastructure provides a national-scale example: the American Society of Civil Engineers estimates $2.59 trillion in deferred infrastructure maintenance. Every year of further deferral increases the total cost because deterioration is nonlinear—a pothole costs $50 to fix; the collapsed road underneath costs $500,000. The pattern applies to physical infrastructure, software systems (technical debt), organizations (organizational debt), and personal health (deferred medical and dental care).
- **How it helps:** Makes the exponential cost of deferred maintenance visible, converting abstract 'we should maintain things' into concrete economic arguments. (1) Calculate your 'maintenance debt': list all deferred maintenance items and estimate both their current repair cost and their projected cost if deferred another year. (2) Compare preventive maintenance cost to emergency repair cost for each system—the ratio (typically 1:5 to 1:10) makes the economic case for prevention. (3) Make maintenance budgets 'sacred'—not available for reallocation during budget pressure. (4) Track the age and condition of all critical assets and plot deterioration curves. (5) The rule: maintenance deferred is interest accumulating, and the interest rate is high.
- **When to use:** When maintenance budgets are being cut under financial pressure. When 'we'll fix it next quarter' is a repeated pattern. When emergency repairs are consuming the maintenance budget. When evaluating the true cost of infrastructure, technology, or organizational maintenance deferral.
- **Unique value:** The compound interest metaphor makes the economics of deferred maintenance visceral and intuitive. The 1:5 to 1:10 ratio of preventive to emergency costs provides a concrete number for budget conversations. Applies identically across physical, digital, organizational, and personal domains.
- **Limits:** Not all maintenance is equally valuable—some can be safely deferred. The model can justify gold-plating maintenance budgets. Priority must be given to critical systems. Some assets are better replaced than maintained. The cost ratios are averages that vary by system.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in organizational neglect research, deferred maintenance economics, and systems degradation theory. Draws on broken windows theory (Wilson & Kelling) applied to systems, availability bias in risk perception, and temporal discounting (hyperbolic discounting, Ainslie).

### Automation Complacency (Ironies of Automation) · #2224 · `llm-high` · systems · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Lisanne Bainbridge's foundational insight: automation creates three paradoxes that can make systems less safe rather than more. (1) The irony of skill degradation: automation removes the need for manual skill, but that skill is exactly what's needed when automation fails—so the situations where humans must take over are precisely the situations they're least prepared for. (2) The irony of monitoring: automation makes the task boring enough that humans can't maintain vigilance, but monitoring automation requires sustained attention—the very thing boredom prevents. (3) The irony of complexity: automation designed to help the operator often makes the system more complex, creating new failure modes that require even more skill to manage. Tesla Autopilot crashes exemplify all three: drivers lose the skill of attentive driving, can't sustain monitoring attention, and face a system with complex edge-case behaviors. The 737 MAX crashes demonstrate the complexity irony: MCAS, designed to make the plane easier to fly, created a failure mode that required advanced troubleshooting skill the pilots didn't have.
- **How it helps:** Provides specific design principles that address each irony. For skill degradation: (1) build regular manual practice into automated operations, (2) design for smooth human takeover, not just smooth automation. For monitoring: (1) give humans meaningful tasks alongside monitoring, (2) implement 'attention checks' that detect disengagement. For complexity: (1) make automation transparent—operators must understand what the automation is doing and why, (2) design failure modes that are simple and recognizable, not complex and surprising. The overarching principle: design automation to support human capability, not replace it. The moment you assume humans aren't needed is the moment you've created the most dangerous system.
- **When to use:** When implementing automation in safety-critical systems. When evaluating the human role in automated processes. When investigating failures where automation 'should have helped.' When designing the human-automation interface.
- **Unique value:** Bainbridge's three ironies are the most important insights in human-automation interaction. They explain why more automation doesn't always mean more safety, which is counterintuitive to most engineers and managers. The specific design principles for each irony make the insights actionable.
- **Limits:** Automation does genuinely improve safety in many domains (aviation overall is much safer with automation). The ironies describe edge cases, not normal operation. Full automation (removing the human entirely) avoids some ironies. The model can justify resistance to beneficial automation.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in Bainbridge's 'ironies of automation' (1983), human factors research, and skill degradation literature. Draws on situation awareness research (Endsley), out-of-the-loop performance problems, and complacency research in aviation and nuclear operations contexts.

### Thrashing (Over-Scheduling) · #1191 · `llm-high` · technology · CLAIM
- **What it is:** A computing term for when a system spends more time switching between tasks than doing actual work—the processor is 'busy' but producing nothing because context-switching overhead consumes all available capacity. In organizations: when people have so many concurrent priorities that they spend more time in meetings about work than doing work, more time switching contexts than thinking deeply, and more time managing their task list than completing tasks. Thrashing is the systemic result of exceeding human channel capacity with simultaneous demands.
- **How it helps:** Diagnose thrashing by tracking the ratio of productive work time to coordination/switching time. If meetings, email, context-switching, and task management consume more than 50% of the day, you're thrashing. The fix isn't better time management—it's reducing concurrent commitments. Work-in-progress limits (from kanban), focus days (no-meeting blocks), and ruthless prioritization (doing fewer things) address thrashing at the systemic level. The counterintuitive solution: doing fewer things simultaneously produces more total output because it eliminates switching overhead.
- **When to use:** When teams are busy but nothing is getting finished; when adding more tasks is slowing overall throughput; when context-switching costs are consuming productive capacity; when calendar density has increased but output quality has declined.
- **Unique value:** Provides the precise technical diagnosis for the common organizational complaint 'we're busy but nothing gets done.' Thrashing isn't a motivation problem or a skill problem—it's a capacity problem caused by too many concurrent demands. The diagnosis points to the correct intervention: reduce work-in-progress, don't try to work harder.
- **Limits:** Some roles genuinely require frequent context-switching (executive, support). Zero context-switching is neither possible nor desirable. The computing analogy is imperfect—humans can sometimes benefit from interleaving tasks. Reducing work-in-progress requires organizational authority that individuals often don't have.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in operating systems theory and computer science (Denning, 1968), thrashing describes CPU inefficiency from excessive paging. Extended via cognitive load theory (Sweller) and attention economics. Organizational parallels draw from queuing theory, lean manufacturing's WIP limits, and flow theory (Csikszentmihalyi).
