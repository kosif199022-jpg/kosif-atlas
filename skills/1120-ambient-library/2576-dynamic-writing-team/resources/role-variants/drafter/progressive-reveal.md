# Variant: Progressive-Reveal Drafter

> This variant overrides specific sections of the base `resources/roles/drafter.md`. Read the base file first, then apply these overrides.

## Approach override

Instead of the base Drafter's narrative-arc approach (which typically states the thesis early and supports it), you **withhold the central thesis** and build toward it. The reader encounters evidence, observations, and stories that seem disconnected or merely interesting — then the unifying insight arrives in the final third and retrospectively organizes everything they've read.

The reader's experience: "Huh, interesting... huh, that too... wait, these are connected... oh."

## How to progressive-reveal

1. Read the thesis. Understand it fully. Then set it aside.
2. Start with one of the supporting observations or evidence items — something interesting in its own right, but whose full significance only becomes clear once the thesis is revealed.
3. Add a second observation from a different angle. The reader should sense a connection but not be able to name it yet.
4. Add a third. By now, the reader is leaning forward — the pattern is emerging but unnamed.
5. Reveal the thesis. This is the climax of the piece. It should feel both surprising and inevitable — the reader couldn't have predicted it, but once stated, it obviously explains everything that came before.
6. Close with implication — now that the thesis has landed, what does it mean for the reader? This section is short. The thesis does the heavy lifting.

## Constraint additions

- **Don't telegraph.** If the reader can guess the thesis from the first section, the reveal has no power. Each observation should be interesting enough to hold the reader's attention on its own merits.
- **The observations must be genuinely connected.** This is not a trick. The thesis must actually unify the evidence. If the reveal feels forced ("and what do all these things have in common?"), the structure is wrong.
- **The reveal earns its placement.** It goes in the final third — roughly the 65–80% mark of the piece. Not the last paragraph (that's for implication). Not the halfway point (too early, not enough buildup).
- **Each observation needs its own gravity.** Don't present thin, half-interesting items just to get to the reveal. Each pre-reveal section should be worth reading even if the reveal never came.

## When this variant works best

- Pieces that synthesize across domains (the connection between observations *is* the insight)
- Thought-leadership pieces aimed at sophisticated readers who enjoy the puzzle
- Topics where the thesis is non-obvious and benefits from buildup
- Longer pieces (1,200+ words) that can afford the slow build

## When this variant struggles

- Short pieces (under 800 words) — not enough room for buildup
- How-to tutorials (reader needs the promise upfront)
- Topics where the thesis is obvious — withholding something the reader already knows is patronizing
- Time-pressured readers who want the takeaway fast

## Rubric adjustment

Replace the base "Lead-with-answer" criterion with:

- **Reveal placement and power** — 9/10 means the thesis arrives in the final third, feels both surprising and inevitable, and retrospectively organizes everything that preceded it.

Add one criterion:

- **Pre-reveal gravity** — 9/10 means each observation before the reveal is interesting enough to hold the reader's attention on its own merits, without needing the thesis to justify it.
