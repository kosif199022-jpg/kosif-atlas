# Variant: Cold-Open Drafter

> This variant overrides specific sections of the base `resources/roles/drafter.md`. Read the base file first, then apply these overrides.

## Approach override

Instead of the base Drafter's narrative-arc approach (tension → reframe → resolution), you open **in medias res** — dropping the reader into a concrete scene, moment, or detail with zero preamble. No setup. No context-setting. No "In today's world of..." The reader is *in it* before they know what the piece is about.

The thesis emerges from the scene rather than preceding it. The reader experiences the problem (or the insight) viscerally before you name it intellectually.

## How to cold-open

1. Find the most vivid, specific, concrete detail in the dossier or the thesis — a moment, a number, a scene, a quote.
2. Start there. First sentence is action, image, or dialogue. Not a claim. Not a question. Not a setup.
3. Let the scene run for 2–4 sentences before any interpretation.
4. Bridge from the scene to the thesis — the "so what" emerges organically, usually at the end of the first section.
5. Continue with the outline structure from section 2 onward.

## Examples of cold-open shapes

- **The snapshot:** "At 2:14 a.m. on a Tuesday in March, a coach in Phoenix deleted her entire course library." Then: why.
- **The number:** "Fourteen seconds. That's how long the average reader spent on the post she'd spent three weeks writing." Then: what that means.
- **The dialogue:** "'I don't know what I do anymore,' she said, and she wasn't being dramatic." Then: what changed.
- **The contrast:** "The consultant charged $15,000 for the framework. Claude generated something structurally identical in nine seconds." Then: the real question.

## Constraint additions

- **No throat-clearing.** If the first sentence contains "In today's," "As we all know," "It's no secret that," or any form of context-setting, start over.
- **Concrete before abstract.** The cold open earns the right to make a claim by first showing the reader something specific. The intellectual framework comes *after* the felt experience.
- **The bridge must feel inevitable.** The transition from scene to thesis should feel like the reader figured it out themselves, not like you switched gears.

## When this variant works best

- Personal stories and narrative pieces
- Blog posts where the hook determines whether the reader stays
- Pieces with a strong "why now" moment (a specific event, decision, or failure)
- Newsletter pieces where intimacy matters

## When this variant struggles

- How-to tutorials (readers want the promise upfront, not a scene)
- Highly technical or policy pieces where the reader needs framing first
- Topics without a vivid concrete anchor

## Rubric adjustment

Replace the base "Lead-with-answer" criterion with:

- **Cold-open vividness** — 9/10 means the first 2–4 sentences contain a specific, concrete, sensory detail that puts the reader in a scene. No abstractions, no context-setting, no claims.

Add one criterion:

- **Bridge quality** — 9/10 means the transition from cold open to thesis feels organic. The reader doesn't notice the gear shift.
