## 1. Strategic-job library

### ATTRACT — earn attention

- **Reader shift:** “This is irrelevant” -> “This matters to me.”
- **Useful mechanisms:** surprise, tension, specificity, novelty, pattern interruption, timely relevance, stakes.
- **Good evidence:** recognizable symptoms, vivid observations, consequential changes.
- **Failure mode:** empty sensationalism that wins attention but breaks the promise.

### EXPLAIN — create understanding

- **Reader shift:** “I do not get it” -> “Now I understand.”
- **Useful mechanisms:** definition, decomposition, mechanism, example, comparison, sequence, causal model.
- **Good evidence:** accurate explanations, demonstrations, worked examples.
- **Failure mode:** information without a clear learner outcome.

### REFRAME — disrupt the existing model

- **Reader shift:** “This is how it works” -> “My lens may be wrong.”
- **Useful mechanisms:** contradiction, hidden premise, inversion, diagnosis, paradox, counterexample.
- **Good evidence:** anomalies the old model cannot explain and a sharper replacement lens.
- **Failure mode:** contrarianism without a more useful model.

### PERSUADE — establish the replacement belief

- **Reader shift:** “I am skeptical” -> “This conclusion is credible.”
- **Useful mechanisms:** proof, demonstration, case study, mechanism, evidence chain, belief bridge, objection reversal, risk comparison, inevitable conclusion.
- **Good evidence:** claim -> evidence -> implication, with uncertainty preserved.
- **Failure mode:** asserting harder instead of closing the reasoning gap.

### CONNECT — build interpersonal trust

- **Reader shift:** “I do not know this person” -> “This person understands people like me.”
- **Useful mechanisms:** relevant personal story, values, honest uncertainty, shared experience, behind-the-scenes context, reader recognition.
- **Good evidence:** authentic supplied experience and consistent behavior.
- **Failure mode:** synthetic vulnerability, oversharing, or personality without relevance.

### POSITION — build epistemic trust

- **Reader shift:** “Why listen to you?” -> “This is a credible and distinctive guide.”
- **Useful mechanisms:** original thesis, named mechanism, synthesis, diagnosis, teardown, standards, first-principles reasoning, demonstrated judgment.
- **Good evidence:** precise distinctions, useful predictions, credible experience, frameworks that explain why.
- **Failure mode:** status claims, jargon, or teaching that never demonstrates judgment.

### ENVISION — create desire

- **Reader shift:** “Interesting” -> “I want that outcome or identity.”
- **Useful mechanisms:** future pacing, transformation, possibility, aspirational identity, second-order benefits, freedom gained, problems removed.
- **Good evidence:** plausible, specific future states connected to the mechanism.
- **Failure mode:** fantasy, inflated promises, or desire detached from reality.

### ACTIVATE — cause noncommercial action

- **Reader shift:** “I agree” -> “I will try, decide, measure, or change something.”
- **Useful mechanisms:** audit, experiment, prompt, checklist, challenge, decision rule, five-minute win, implementation step, report-back invitation.
- **Good evidence:** clear action, low ambiguity, relevant feedback signal.
- **Failure mode:** motivational language without an executable next step.

### SELL — cause commercial action

- **Reader shift:** “I may want help” -> “I will subscribe, apply, book, join, or buy.”
- **Useful mechanisms:** problem specificity, desired outcome, fit, differentiation, use case, customer evidence, objection handling, legitimate urgency, risk reversal, direct invitation.
- **Good evidence:** accurate offer details, qualified outcomes, real proof.
- **Failure mode:** asking for commitment before belief, trust, or desire exists.

## 2. Canonical 63-angle library

Preserve these names and this order in canonical modes.

### 1–21: EXPLAIN — “Help me understand”

1. **Tips** — Provide practical, actionable advice; use a numbered list when it improves execution.
2. **Stats** — Use a surprising or counterintuitive verified statistic about the problem or solution.
3. **Steps** — Present a short process leading to a specific, nameable outcome.
4. **Lessons** — Share something genuinely learned the hard way, with supplied specifics.
5. **Benefits** — Show what becomes possible once the problem is solved.
6. **Reasons** — Explain why the problem is harder than expected or why the solution works.
7. **Mistakes** — Identify a specific mistake and its exact or credibly described cost.
8. **Examples** — Use a concrete example or verified case study.
9. **Questions** — Ask a question that makes the reader reconsider an assumption.
10. **Ways** — Present multiple approaches to the same problem or outcome.
11. **Ideas** — Offer a fresh take, emerging approach, or underused tactic.
12. **Myths** — Correct a widely held belief and provide the replacement belief.
13. **Tools** — Explain a specific tool, resource, or asset that changes the outcome.
14. **Frameworks** — Provide a mental model or structure for thinking about the problem.
15. **Comparisons** — Contrast before and after, old and new, or approach A and B.
16. **Objections** — Address a common reason people resist the solution and give the real answer.
17. **Predictions** — Make a qualified, reasoned forecast about where the topic is heading.
18. **Challenges** — Describe the honest difficulty of implementation and how to handle it.
19. **Results** — Show specific measurable outcomes only when supported by real evidence.
20. **Trends** — Explain a verified current change in the space and its implications.
21. **Signs** — Show how to recognize that the problem is worsening or the solution is working.

### 22–42: REFRAME — “Help me see it differently”

22. **Stories** — Use a short true-to-life narrative that dramatizes the problem instead of explaining it abstractly.
23. **Confessions** — Share something the creator genuinely used to believe, do, avoid, or get wrong and what changed.
24. **Contrarian Takes** — Challenge familiar advice and explain precisely where it breaks down.
25. **Uncomfortable Truths** — State a defensible reality people in the space may know privately but rarely say publicly.
26. **Observations** — Reveal a pattern genuinely noticed among clients, customers, peers, or the market.
27. **Diagnoses** — Take a familiar symptom and reveal the less-obvious underlying cause.
28. **Warnings** — Identify something that appears harmless but creates a predictable later problem.
29. **Reframes** — Change the lens through which the reader interprets the problem.
30. **Paradoxes** — Show how two apparently contradictory propositions can both be true.
31. **Tradeoffs** — Reveal what the reader gives up when choosing one approach over another.
32. **Experiments** — Propose a small test readers can run to discover what works for themselves.
33. **Rules** — Give a strong operating principle in the form “Whenever X, do Y.”
34. **Anti-Rules** — Identify a conventional rule worth deliberately breaking and specify when.
35. **Behind the Scenes** — Show the real decisions, process, and messiness involved in solving the problem.
36. **Turning Points** — Identify the moment that separates continued struggle from meaningful progress.
37. **Identity Shifts** — Show how progress requires thinking or behaving as a different kind of person.
38. **If/Then** — Give a diagnostic decision rule: if X, avoid Y and do Z.
39. **What Nobody Tells You** — Surface a second-order consequence people discover only after going deeper.
40. **Counterfactuals** — Ask what follows if the opposite assumption is true.
41. **Provocations** — Make a sharp, defensible statement designed to create productive disagreement.
42. **Reader Challenges** — Ask the audience to do, notice, stop, measure, or change one specific thing.

### 43–63: ENGAGE — “Pull me into the idea”

43. **Polls** — Offer 2–4 meaningful choices that reveal where readers stand.
44. **A or B** — Force a choice between two competing approaches, priorities, beliefs, or outcomes.
45. **Finish the Sentence** — Provide an incomplete statement readers can complete from experience.
46. **What Would You Do?** — Present a realistic difficult decision and ask how readers would handle it.
47. **Agree or Disagree?** — Make a specific debatable claim and invite a position.
48. **Hot Takes** — Offer a deliberately strong position and invite challenge, defense, or refinement.
49. **Rank These** — Ask readers to order several factors, strategies, problems, or priorities.
50. **Choose One** — Present several desirable options but permit only one choice.
51. **Rate Yourself** — Provide a simple scale or scorecard for self-assessment.
52. **Spot the Problem** — Present a situation, example, workflow, or advice and ask readers to identify what is wrong.
53. **Critique This** — Share an approach, claim, strategy, or example and invite improvement.
54. **Complete the List** — Start a useful list and ask what is missing.
55. **Your Experience** — Ask whether readers have encountered a specific situation and what happened.
56. **Confession Prompt** — Create a safe opening for readers to admit a struggle, avoidance, private belief, or hard lesson.
57. **Unpopular Opinion** — State or solicit an opinion people may hold privately but hesitate to express.
58. **Prediction Game** — Ask what readers think will happen next; optionally state a reasoned prediction first.
59. **One Question** — Ask the single most valuable question of people experiencing the problem.
60. **Help Someone** — Present another person's situation and ask readers what advice they would give.
61. **Show Your Work** — Invite readers to share their process, setup, attempt, example, result, or solution.
62. **Mini Challenge** — Give one small action readers can complete today and invite a report-back.
63. **Debate** — Frame strong cases for two opposing positions and ask which wins.

## 3. Teaching-strategy library

Select the minimum strategy needed. Combine strategies only when the learner outcome requires it.

| Strategy | Use when | Required move |
|---|---|---|
| Outcome-first design | Scope is unclear | Define what readers can explain, decide, or do afterward. |
| Prior-knowledge activation | New material needs an anchor | Connect it to an accurate familiar concept. |
| Chunking | Complexity overloads a Note | Divide material into meaningful units. |
| Scaffolding | Later steps depend on earlier ones | Sequence from prerequisite to independent application. |
| Worked example | Reasoning or procedure is invisible | Show the full process with a concrete case. |
| Model -> Guide -> Try | The goal is performance | Demonstrate, support an attempt, then give independent practice. |
| Misconception repair | An intuitive model blocks learning | Surface the model, create contradiction, replace it, apply it. |
| Comparison and contrast | Readers confuse adjacent concepts | Hold dimensions constant and expose the decisive difference. |
| Analogy with boundaries | Abstraction blocks understanding | Map the analogy and state where it stops applying. |
| Retrieval prompt | Retention matters | Ask readers to recall or reconstruct without looking. |
| Application exercise | Readers understand but cannot use it | Apply the concept to a realistic situation. |
| Transfer | Readers overfit one example | Apply the principle in a meaningfully different context. |
| Progressive disclosure | The full model would overwhelm | Reveal only the complexity needed at each stage. |
| Troubleshooting tree | Multiple failure causes look alike | Diagnose by symptom and branch to corrective action. |
| Verification and feedback | Correct execution is not obvious | Define an observable success signal and correction path. |

### Teaching structures

- **Why -> What -> How -> Try -> Check**: Default micro-tutorial.
- **Problem -> Principle -> Procedure -> Proof**: Teach a mechanism and demonstrate it.
- **Show -> Explain -> Practice -> Reflect**: Skill-building sequence.
- **Misconception -> Contradiction -> Better model -> Application**: Belief repair.
- **Example -> Pattern -> Principle -> New example**: Inductive concept formation.
- **Symptom -> Diagnosis -> Fix -> Verification**: Troubleshooting Note.
- **Before -> Decision -> Action -> Result -> Lesson**: Evidence-backed case teaching.

### Tutorial integrity checklist

- State one observable outcome.
- Name prerequisites and required tools.
- Put actions in executable order.
- Explain decision points, not only clicks or steps.
- Use examples consistent with the instructions.
- Name common failure modes where consequential.
- Give the reader a way to verify success.
- Split the tutorial when one Note cannot hold the prerequisite chain.

## 4. Hook and expression library

### Hook types

Select one that honestly fits the substance:

- Specific problem recognition
- Surprising consequence
- Contradiction
- Direct promise
- Diagnostic question
- In-progress scene
- Crisp observation
- Credible result
- Unexpected comparison
- Timely change
- Identity tension
- Open loop with prompt payoff

Avoid generic suspense, inflated certainty, bait-and-switch, and “You won't believe…” language.

### Expression formats

- One-line insight
- Short argument
- Micro-story
- Numbered list
- Checklist
- Before/after
- Myth/reality
- Question-led reflection
- Worked example
- Demonstration
- Mini case study
- Dialogue or objection/answer
- Decision tree
- Scorecard
- Poll
- Fill-in-the-blank
- Challenge
- Teardown
- Field note or behind-the-scenes observation
- Step-by-step micro-tutorial
- Serialized sequence

Change one layer at a time for controlled variants. For example, hold job and angle constant while testing two hooks; do not call entirely different arguments an A/B test.

## 5. Destination and artifact guide

### Destinations

| Destination | Default behavior |
|---|---|
| Substack Notes | One dominant idea; fast opening; conversational; self-contained payoff; optional light interaction. |
| Newsletter email | Relational opening; sustained arc; context from the sender-reader relationship; one primary CTA. |
| Blog | Descriptive headings; comprehensive coverage; explicit navigation; search intent only when requested. |
| LinkedIn | Early relevance; scannable paragraphs; professional context; avoid engagement bait. |
| Generic social | Respect stated character and formatting limits; optimize for native reading. |

### Artifact types

| Artifact | Structural center |
|---|---|
| Note | One idea or interaction with a complete payoff. |
| Article | Structured coverage of a defined question. |
| Essay | Thesis, exploration, voice, and earned conclusion. |
| Tutorial | Observable outcome, prerequisites, ordered actions, examples, and verification. |
| Series | Dependency-aware sequence in which each installment has its own payoff. |
| Thread | Ordered short units that require sequence but remain individually legible. |

### Roles relative to a larger work

- **Standalone:** Deliver the complete promised value in the piece.
- **Lead-in:** Establish a problem, tension, or question the larger work resolves.
- **Excerpt:** Adapt a coherent passage; do not paste a context-dependent fragment.
- **Companion:** Add a distinct example, implication, or application.
- **Extension:** Develop a branch the source could not cover.
- **Follow-up:** Address response, objection, result, or unresolved question.
- **Promotion:** Give real value before inviting the click; do not disguise an ad as teaching.

## 6. Portfolio patterns

Treat these as starting hypotheses, not fixed formulas.

### Mix 21

- 7 EXPLAIN-family angles
- 7 REFRAME-family angles
- 7 ENGAGE-family angles

Use for creative breadth when no reader-state diagnosis is available.

### Educational authority portfolio

- Lead with EXPLAIN and ACTIVATE.
- Add REFRAME where misconceptions block understanding.
- Add POSITION through demonstrated judgment, not self-praise.
- Use ENGAGE angles for retrieval, diagnosis, practice, and feedback.

### Belief-change portfolio

- Use REFRAME to loosen the old model.
- Use PERSUADE to establish the replacement belief.
- Use EXPLAIN for mechanisms the belief depends on.
- Add CONNECT or POSITION only when the trust gap is real.

### Relationship portfolio

- Weight CONNECT while preserving topic relevance.
- Add EXPLAIN or REFRAME so personality carries useful substance.
- Use ENGAGE angles to invite genuine experience, not vanity responses.

### Launch or offer portfolio

- Diagnose belief, trust, desire, and objection gaps before SELL.
- Use ENVISION to make the future specific and plausible.
- Use PERSUADE for mechanism and proof.
- Use SELL for fit, offer, objection handling, and invitation.
- Keep commercial facts exact.

### Learning series

- Sequence prerequisites before dependent skills.
- Alternate explanation, demonstration, application, and feedback.
- Use short ACTIVATE pieces between denser EXPLAIN pieces.
- End with transfer or independent performance, not recap alone.

### Repurposing ecosystem

From one source, consider:

- Thesis Note
- Contrarian implication
- Worked example
- Misconception repair
- Behind-the-scenes decision
- One-step tutorial
- Troubleshooting Note
- Reader diagnostic
- Discussion prompt
- Companion or extension
- Relevant invitation to the full source

Do not repeat the source's headline and summary 21 times.

## 8. Quality rubric

Score each dimension from 0–2. Revise any piece scoring 0 or any portfolio averaging below 1.5.

| Dimension | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strategic fit | Job is unclear or mismatched | Plausible but diffuse | Clear state change and mechanism |
| Angle integrity | Label only | Partial use | Angle drives the substance |
| Distinctness | Duplicate | Some new framing | Materially new argument or payoff |
| Specificity | Generic | Some concrete detail | Concrete mechanism, example, or decision |
| Teaching | Confusing or non-executable | Understandable | Learnable, applicable, and verifiable |
| Truth | Unsupported or fabricated | Qualified but thin | Supported and appropriately bounded |
| Destination fit | Wrong container behavior | Serviceable | Native and polished |
| Voice | Templated | Neutral | Human, credible, and consistent |
| Payoff | Promise not delivered | Partial reward | Opening promise fully earned |
| Restraint | Bloated or coercive | Minor excess | One idea, clean ending, proportionate CTA |

### Portfolio-level checks

- No two adjacent pieces make the same central argument.
- Job allocation reflects the diagnosed bottleneck.
- Teaching sequences respect prerequisites.
- ENGAGE prompts are answerable and meaningful.
- CONNECT pieces use authentic supplied experience.
- POSITION pieces demonstrate judgment rather than claim status.
- ENVISION pieces remain plausible.
- SELL pieces match reader readiness and accurate offer details.
- Every factual proof point has a source or is explicitly qualified.
- Every piece can be published without hidden notes or placeholders.
