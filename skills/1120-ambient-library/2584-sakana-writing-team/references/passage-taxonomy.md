# Passage Taxonomy — Paragraph-Level Classification & Craft Targeting

## Purpose

The skill's existing architecture classifies articles into structural regions (Opening, Escalation, Pivot, Resolution) for merge scoring. This taxonomy operates one level deeper: it classifies individual paragraphs by *function* so that craft constraints, voice modulation, and pacing targets can be applied conditionally rather than uniformly.

A paragraph that advances an argument needs different sentence architecture than a paragraph that asks the reader to confront something uncomfortable about themselves. Applying the same craft constraints to both produces writing that's uniformly sharp but emotionally flat — or uniformly warm but intellectually soft. The taxonomy makes it possible to be sharp where sharpness serves the reader and spacious where spaciousness does.

## The Six Passage Types

### 1. Argument

**What it does:** Advances the thesis through logic, claim, or assertion. This paragraph is making a point the reader needs to accept before the article can move forward.

**How to identify:** The paragraph contains a claim that could be disagreed with, and the surrounding paragraphs either build toward it (setup) or follow from it (implication). If you removed this paragraph, the article's logical chain would break.

**Examples from practice:**
- "For someone whose entire practice is built on expertise, role compression isn't a job adjustment. It's an identity dissolution."
- "Solo adoption optimizes what already exists. It makes existing processes faster. More consistent. But optimization by definition works only with what's already there."

**Pacing targets:**
- Entry: Fast. The opening sentence should arrive mid-thought, already committed to a position. This is where the "invisible filler" technique works best — the sentence after the deleted filler is almost always a stronger argument opener than a topic sentence.
- Rhythm: Short-to-medium sentences. Declaratives for claims, slightly longer for the reasoning underneath.
- Exit: Don't summarize the claim. Let it sit. The next paragraph will either build on it or challenge it.

### 2. Evidence

**What it does:** Presents research, data, named examples, or documented cases that ground the article's claims in verifiable reality. This paragraph is proving something.

**How to identify:** Contains at least two of: a name, a number, a date, a place, a specific action taken. References a study, a company, a person, or an event that exists outside the article.

**Examples from practice:**
- "In 2025, researcher Michael Gerlich at SBS Swiss Business School surveyed 666 knowledge workers, measuring AI tool usage against critical thinking scores. The correlation was negative: r = -0.68."
- "Suzanne Simard spent years in Douglas fir stands in British Columbia studying something most forest science had stopped looking at."

**Pacing targets:**
- Entry: Grounded in a specific detail — a name, a date, a place. Not "Research shows that..." but the research itself, arriving with its specifics intact.
- Rhythm: Varied. Dense passages (multiple data points) benefit from shorter sentences that let each fact register. Narrative evidence (a person's story) can stretch into longer, more textured sentences.
- Integration: Every evidence paragraph must do rhetorical work, not just citation work. The evidence should advance the argument, not pause it. If removing the paragraph doesn't weaken the argument's logic, the evidence is decorative.
- Exit: Connect forward. The last sentence should create momentum toward the next paragraph's claim or the implication of the evidence, not simply close the citation.

### 3. Confrontation

**What it does:** Asks the reader to see something uncomfortable about themselves — their habits, blind spots, or assumptions. This paragraph holds up a mirror.

**How to identify:** Uses second person ("you") in a way that describes the reader's specific behavior or situation. The reader's likely first reaction is defensiveness, recognition, or both. The paragraph is diagnostic — it names what the reader is doing or experiencing.

**Examples from practice:**
- "You're experienced. You know AI's actual limits. You edit the output. You add your perspective. You shape the final product. You're not like those people in the studies."
- "If that was more than a month ago, you have your data."

**Pacing targets:**
- Entry: Slower than argument passages. The reader needs to be met, not ambushed. Scenic "you" (placing the reader in a universally true moment) is safer than diagnostic "you" (telling the reader what they're doing wrong). When diagnostic "you" is appropriate (body of article, after rapport is built), approach it conditionally: "If you find yourself..." rather than "You are..."
- Rhythm: Longer sentences in the middle of the paragraph give the reader room to sit with the recognition. One-sentence paragraphs can work here, but only if the single sentence earns its isolation through specificity, not cleverness.
- Emotional room: This is the most important differentiator. Between naming the uncomfortable truth and moving to the next point, leave at least one beat — a sentence that acknowledges the difficulty without resolving it. Rushing past a confrontation passage teaches the reader that the article doesn't take their experience seriously.
- Exit: Do not resolve the discomfort. Let it carry into the next paragraph. The confrontation's power comes from lingering, not from being tidied up.

### 4. Advice

**What it does:** Offers practical guidance the reader can act on. This paragraph is helping someone do something.

**How to identify:** Contains directives (implicit or explicit), process steps, or actionable frameworks. The reader could, after reading this paragraph, take a specific action they couldn't have taken before.

**Examples from practice:**
- "What works: list every task you've delegated to AI in the past month. Circle the ones where you actually used to learn something about your own expertise by doing them manually."
- "Design your AI workflow so the first move comes from you. You generate the diagnosis. You name the framework. You set the direction."

**Pacing targets:**
- Entry: Concrete and immediate. Not "Here's how to approach this" but the first actionable step itself, already in motion.
- Rhythm: Medium sentences. Clear enough to follow, textured enough to feel like a person talking — not a manual.
- Tone: Companionship over instruction. The reader should feel accompanied through the action, not ordered to perform it. The difference between "Do X" and "What works is X" is small in word count and large in felt experience. Coaching-intimate register permits more directive language; professional-conversational register benefits from the softer frame.
- Exit: Connect the advice to the article's thesis. Why does this specific action matter beyond its immediate utility? The connection should be implicit or brief — not a paragraph-length justification. Trust the reader to make the link.

### 5. Transition

**What it does:** Moves the reader from one idea, section, or register to another. This paragraph is a bridge.

**How to identify:** Sits between two sections or ideas that differ in content, tone, or altitude (zooming in/out). Often the shortest paragraphs in the article. May be a single sentence.

**Examples from practice:**
- "Then AI arrived."
- "This has a direct parallel in something happening right now across independent practices everywhere."
- "All three needs. All three threatened. At the same time. By the same person who's supposed to be championing the tools."

**Pacing targets:**
- Entry: The transition's opening sentence should feel like the natural next thought — not an announcement that the article is about to change direction. "Speaking of..." and "Now let's turn to..." are AI tells. The best transitions don't declare themselves.
- Length: Short. One to three sentences. A transition that runs longer than three sentences is probably an argument or confrontation paragraph that's been misidentified.
- Invisibility: The reader should feel momentum, not machinery. If the transition calls attention to itself as a structural device, it's too visible. Rewrite.

### 6. Landing

**What it does:** Closes a section or the article. This paragraph is the last thing the reader sits with before moving on or leaving.

**How to identify:** Sits at the end of a section break (---) or the article itself. Contains the passage's most resonant image, most precise claim, or most generative question.

**Examples from practice:**
- "Lose that sense and the limbs remain. Just can't feel them anymore."
- "That's not resistance. That's sovereignty."
- "The trees already figured this out."

**Pacing targets:**
- Entry: Earned specificity. The landing's opening sentence should feel like the culmination of everything the section built, not a summary of it. If the sentence could have appeared at the beginning of the section without the intervening paragraphs, it hasn't been earned.
- Rhythm: Variable — can be a single short sentence (verdict) or a longer passage (earned reflection). What matters is that the rhythm differs from the section's dominant pattern. If the section was dense and fast, the landing might slow down. If the section was exploratory, the landing might tighten.
- Residue: This is where the Overnight Test lives at the paragraph level. The landing is the passage the reader will remember 48 hours later. It should contain at least one of: a specific image, a reusable phrase, or a reframe that changes how the reader sees the topic. If the landing could close any article on the same topic, it's too generic.
- Exit: Do not summarize the section. The reader remembers what you just wrote. End with forward energy (implying what comes next) or with weight (letting the idea settle). Never both.

## Parameterization by Delivery Format and Register

The six passage types are universal — every article contains them. But the pacing targets shift based on the audience fitness profile. The taxonomy's skeleton is fixed; the muscle flexes.

### Delivery Format Adjustments

**Thought piece** (WHY/WHAT heavy):
- Argument passages dominate. Evidence serves argument, not the reverse.
- Confrontation passages carry more weight — the article's value is in the reframe, which requires the reader to feel the gap between their current thinking and the article's thesis.
- Advice passages are compressed or absent. Landing passages carry the heaviest residue burden.

**Practitioner guide** (HOW heavy):
- Advice passages dominate and need the most craft attention. They must feel like companionship, not manual.
- Evidence passages serve as proof that the advice works, not as intellectual argument.
- Confrontation passages appear early (establishing why the reader needs this) and then recede. Landing passages should point toward action, not reflection.

**Newsletter/signal** (WHY dominant, shorter overall):
- Argument passages are tighter — fewer sentences per claim.
- Evidence is compressed: one strong example per point, not three.
- Transition passages must be nearly invisible — the piece is too short for visible machinery.
- Landing optimizes for shareability: the reader needs a sentence they can quote.

**Tutorial** (HOW dominant):
- Advice passages are the article. They should be maximally clear, minimally stylized.
- Confrontation is minimal or absent. The reader already knows they need this.
- Evidence is functional: "this works because..." not "research shows that..."

### Register Adjustments

**Coaching-intimate:**
- Confrontation passages can be more direct. Diagnostic "you" is appropriate earlier (still not in the opening).
- Advice passages use directive language: "Do this." "Try this." Shared context assumed.
- Landing passages can be warmer — the reader-writer relationship permits intimacy.

**Professional-conversational:**
- Confrontation passages use conditional framing: "If you find yourself..." Not "You are..."
- Advice passages frame recommendations, not directives: "What works is..." Not "Do this."
- Landing passages balance warmth with precision.

**Technical-peer:**
- Confrontation passages assume the reader has already noticed the problem. Skip orientation.
- Evidence passages can reference research without extensive explanation — the reader knows the literature.
- Argument passages can move faster. Jargon is efficient, not exclusionary.

**Formal-academic:**
- Confrontation passages are reframed as observations about the field, not direct address.
- Evidence passages are more prominent and more rigorously sourced.
- Landing passages privilege precision over resonance.

## Classification Protocol

During DRAFT (or REFINE), classify each paragraph before applying craft constraints:

1. Read the paragraph.
2. Ask: What is this paragraph's primary function? (It may do more than one thing, but one function dominates.)
3. Assign the type.
4. Apply the type's pacing targets, adjusted for delivery format and register.

If a paragraph genuinely serves two functions equally (e.g., an evidence paragraph that also confronts), assign the type that demands more pacing care. Confrontation > Evidence > Argument > Advice > Transition > Landing. The hierarchy prioritizes the types where getting the pacing wrong costs the most reader goodwill.
