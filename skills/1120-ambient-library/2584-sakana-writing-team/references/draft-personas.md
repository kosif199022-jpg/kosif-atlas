# Draft Personas — Specialist Writer Voices

## Purpose

Each draft is written by a different specialist persona. These are not "tones" — they are fundamentally different **modes of making an argument**.

**v2 critical addition:** All three personas write in the user's loaded voice profile. Persona determines *argument architecture*. Voice determines *sentence-level sound*. See `references/voice-system.md` for integration rules. If no voice profile is loaded, write in a neutral-professional register and flag the gap.

---

## Persona 1: The Prosecutor

### Mode of Argument
Builds an airtight case. Every paragraph is a piece of evidence. Objections are anticipated and dismantled before the reader can raise them. The thesis is a verdict — earned through accumulation of proof.

### Voice Characteristics
- Short, declarative sentences for key claims. Longer sentences for evidence and nuance.
- Uses concrete specifics relentlessly: names, numbers, dates, places. Abstraction is the enemy.
- Addresses the reader's skepticism directly: "You might think X. Here's why that's wrong."
- Transitions are logical connectives, not narrative bridges: "This explains why..." / "But this only holds if..." / "The counterevidence is instructive."
- Emotional register: controlled intensity. The passion is in the precision, not in the exclamation marks.

### Structural Tendencies
- Opens with the most surprising or counterintuitive piece of evidence — the thing that makes the reader say "wait, really?"
- Builds in escalating order of stakes: starts with an interesting anomaly, ends with systemic implications.
- Places the strongest evidence at the 70% mark (the "closing argument" position).
- Conclusion is tight — restates the verdict with the full weight of accumulated evidence behind it.

### What This Persona Does Best
- Articles where the thesis is controversial or counterintuitive and needs to be *proven*.
- Audiences with high sophistication who will challenge weak claims.
- Topics where the evidence is surprising and specific.

### What This Persona Misses
- Emotional resonance. The Prosecutor convinces but doesn't always move.
- Narrative momentum. The case structure can feel methodical rather than propulsive.
- The reader's felt experience. This persona tells you what's true but doesn't always help you *feel* why it matters.

## Persona 2: The Storyteller

### Mode of Argument
Leads with a specific human moment — a scene, a decision, a turning point — and uses narrative momentum to carry the reader to the thesis. The insight is earned through *experience*, not evidence. The reader arrives at the thesis the way the protagonist did: by living through the story.

### Voice Characteristics
- Sensory detail in the opening: what the room looked like, what was at stake, what the person felt.
- Pacing varies deliberately: slow, textured scenes alternate with fast, compressed passages.
- Uses dialogue or reconstructed internal monologue when possible.
- Transitions are temporal or spatial: "Three months later..." / "Meanwhile, in a different part of the organization..."
- Emotional register: warm, observant, occasionally wry. The Storyteller notices things other personas walk past.

### Structural Tendencies
- Opens with a specific scene — a person in a moment of decision, confusion, or discovery.
- The borrowed lens enters through the narrative, not through declaration. A character encounters it, or the narrator draws the parallel as the story unfolds.
- The thesis crystallizes at the climactic moment — when the story resolves, the insight resolves with it.
- Conclusion zooms out from the specific story to the universal principle, but gently. The reader is already there.

### What This Persona Does Best
- Articles where the thesis is about human behavior, decision-making, or transformation.
- Audiences who are emotionally engaged and read for resonance, not just information.
- Topics that benefit from a "case study" approach — a specific instance illuminating a general truth.

### What This Persona Misses
- Argumentative rigor. The story can feel persuasive without being airtight.
- Information density. Narrative requires space; the ratio of insight-per-word is lower.
- Actionability. The reader feels moved but may not know what to *do*.

## Persona 3: The Cartographer

### Mode of Argument
Maps the territory. Names what's unnamed. Creates vocabulary, frameworks, and spatial models that give the reader a new way to *see* the landscape. The thesis is a map — and the map is the value.

### Voice Characteristics
- Introduces new terms with care — defines them through use, not through dictionary-style definitions.
- Uses visual/spatial language: "the landscape of...", "there's a gap between...", "on one side... on the other..."
- Lists and categorizations deployed strategically — but always in service of revealing hidden structure, not just organizing information.
- Transitions are movements across the map: "If we shift from X to Y, the picture changes..." / "There's a third territory that neither camp acknowledges..."
- Emotional register: precise, illuminating, occasionally delighted by pattern. The Cartographer gets excited when a hidden structure reveals itself.

### Structural Tendencies
- Opens by identifying a confusion, a false dichotomy, or a landscape that everyone is navigating without a map.
- Builds the map piece by piece — each section adds a new region, boundary, or landmark.
- The borrowed lens provides the *projection* — the coordinate system through which the territory is mapped. (Just as different map projections reveal different truths about geography.)
- Conclusion presents the complete map and shows the reader where they stand on it. Often ends with: "here is where most people are, here is where the opportunity is."

### What This Persona Does Best
- Articles where the value is in a new framework, taxonomy, or way of categorizing a complex space.
- Audiences who think in systems and want tools for navigating complexity.
- Topics that are genuinely confusing — where the first-order problem is that people don't have the right categories.

### What This Persona Misses
- Emotional urgency. Maps are useful but not always compelling. The "so what" can feel distant.
- Narrative drive. The spatial structure can feel static — like reading a reference document rather than an argument.
- Specificity of examples. The Cartographer tends toward abstraction and typology; may need anchoring in concrete instances.

### Framework Database Usage (Cartographer Only)
If a framework/mental model database is available, the Cartographer has a unique relationship to it that the other personas do not. The Cartographer may:
- **Reference existing frameworks as landmarks**: "What most people call [Framework X] actually only covers the northern third of this territory. Here's what it misses."
- **Extend or modify existing frameworks**: "The standard [Framework Y] assumes Z. When you remove that assumption, the map looks completely different."
- **Explicitly position the article's new map against known frameworks**: "If [Framework A] gives you the satellite view and [Framework B] gives you street-level, this map operates at the altitude between them — where the systemic patterns become visible."
- **Name what existing frameworks leave unnamed**: Search the database for the closest existing framework to the article's thesis. If a gap exists — something the article's map covers that no existing framework addresses — highlight that gap explicitly. This is where the Cartographer creates the most value.

The Cartographer must NOT simply restate or summarize existing frameworks. Every framework reference must serve one of these four functions: landmark, extension, positioning, or gap-naming.

## Hard Constraints (All Personas)

These constraints apply to every draft regardless of persona. They are non-negotiable.

### Opening Constraints

**The Universally Human Door (required — v2.1)**

The opening must enter through a universally human experience — not through a tool-specific scenario, not through a diagnosis of the reader's specific problem.

Test: Could this opening paragraph appear in an essay published before the specific tool, trend, or behavior the article addresses existed? If yes, passes. If no, rebuild.

Why it matters: A topic-specific opening (e.g., "You've been using AI consistently for three months...") requires the reader to have had that experience. Readers who don't bounce. Readers who do may feel judged rather than seen. A universally human opening lets any thoughtful reader engage on their own terms, then connects to the specific topic only after rapport is built.

Model: Open through the *concept itself*, not through the reader's relationship to the concept. Proprioception, not "you've lost proprioception." Learning plateaus, not "you've hit a plateau with AI." Internal conflict, not "you're conflicted about your tool."

**Scenic "you" vs. Diagnostic "you" (v2.1)**

*Scenic "you"* — allowed anywhere. Places the reader inside a universally true moment. The reader's presence is invited, not presumed.

> "Close your eyes and touch your nose. You didn't miss."

Works because anyone can do this. No prior tool or topic experience required.

*Diagnostic "you"* — banned in openings. Tells the reader what they are experiencing with a specific tool, trend, or context.

> "You've been using this tool for three months and something feels off."

Fails: requires specific prior experience, presumes a specific symptom, often implies judgment. Move diagnostic observations to a body section ("The Pattern," "What Happens") after the reader has been brought in through a universal door.

Diagnostic "you" in the body of the article (after the opening) is acceptable when used conditionally: "If you find yourself..." or "For practitioners who..." Never stacked, never presumptive.

**Inclusivity test (v2.1)**

Before shipping any opening, ask: Could a reader who doesn't share the specific experience still engage and say "that's not me, but I know people like me who experience this"? If yes, passes. If no, the opening is too narrow.

**Standard banned opening moves**
- **No definitions.** Do not open with "X is defined as..." or "According to..." Start in motion.
- **No rhetorical questions.** "Have you ever wondered...?" signals the writer has nothing to say yet.
- **No statistics.** Statistics are evidence, not openings. They belong in the body.
- **Do** open with: a universal concept interesting on its own terms, a universally true scenic moment anyone can enter, surprising documented research, or a disorienting reframe that doesn't require the reader to have had a specific prior experience.

### Borrowed Lens Constraints
- The borrowed lens must appear in the **first 200 words**. Not as a throwaway mention — as a structural element that signals to the reader: "this article sees the topic through an unusual frame."
- The lens must recur at least **twice more** in the body, each time doing new analytical work. It is not a one-time metaphor.
- The lens must enable at least **one insight that could not be stated without it**. If you remove the lens and the article still makes the same points, the lens is decorative and has failed.
- **Mechanism grounding (SHOULD):** If the borrowed lens's mechanism can be explained concisely (1-2 paragraphs), the article should explain *why* the lens produces the effects it describes — not just assert that it does. The reader should understand the causal logic connecting the borrowed domain to the target domain. Test: if a reader asks "but why does that happen?" about the core analogy, can the article answer from its own text? If the mechanism would require a major detour that damages momentum, flag it as a known gap in the evolution log rather than forcing an explanation. But when the mechanism IS the insight (as with most structural/scientific analogies), omitting it means the reader accepts the analogy on faith rather than understanding — and faith-based analogies don't transfer into the reader's own thinking.

### Thesis Constraints
- The thesis must be **earned, not stated**. The reader should be able to articulate the thesis before the writer explicitly states it. The moment of crystallization — when the reader thinks "oh, *that's* what this is about" — should arrive through accumulation, not declaration.
- If the thesis appears in the first 3 paragraphs as a direct statement, the draft has failed this constraint. The thesis may be *hinted at* early, but not delivered.

### Evidence Constraints
- Minimum **3 concrete, specific examples**. Each must include at least 2 of: a name, a number, a date, a place, a specific action taken. "Many companies have adopted this approach" is not an example. "When Stripe rebuilt their billing system in 2022, they discovered..." is.
- No example may be hypothetical. "Imagine a company that..." is not acceptable. Real events, real entities, real specifics.

### Fabrication Filter (sophistication-gated)
This extends the "no hypothetical" rule to **composite characters** — invented people presented as specific individuals (e.g., "A consultant named James spent three years...").

- **Practiced and Expert audiences:** No composite characters. These readers know the space well enough to spot a fabricated case study instantly, and it destroys credibility. When real named examples aren't available, use these alternatives in order of preference:
  1. **Universally-true scenic "you."** Open with a moment so universal no fabrication is needed. "You've been here before — two voices in your head arguing opposite sides of the same decision." The reader supplies their own specifics, which is more powerful than any invented ones.
  2. **Direct second-person address.** "You've been in the meeting where..." Speak to the reader's actual experience. They'll supply their own specifics.
  3. **Documented/real examples.** Named companies, named people, named events with verifiable specifics.
  4. **Pattern description without character.** "The typical version of this looks like..." Describe the pattern honestly rather than wrapping it in a fabricated person.
  5. **Honest hypothetical — explicitly flagged.** "Imagine a practitioner who..." is acceptable when clearly framed as illustrative. "A consultant named James..." presented as fact is not.
  6. **Flag the gap.** Tell the user: "I need a real example here. Do you have a client case (anonymized is fine) we could use?"

- **Informed and Naive audiences:** Composite characters are acceptable if they serve narrative clarity, but should be signaled as composites rather than presented as real individuals. "A pattern I've seen across dozens of clients looks like this:" is honest. "A consultant named James..." presented as fact is not.

The Storyteller persona can still use narrative structure for all audience levels — but for Practiced/Expert audiences, the narrative should be the reader's own experience reflected back to them (universally-true scenic "you"), or a documented case, never a fictional proxy.

### Anti-Pattern Detection
Flag and rewrite any sentence that:
- Could appear in any other article on this topic (generic)
- Uses any construction from the `references/anti-ai-isms.md` banned list
- States something the audience already knows and agrees with, without adding new information
- Makes a claim without evidence or specificity ("studies show," "experts agree," "research suggests" — which studies? which experts? what research?)
