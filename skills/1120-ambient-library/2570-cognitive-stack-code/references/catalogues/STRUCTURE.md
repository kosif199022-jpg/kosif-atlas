### Erotic-Domestic Tension (The Mating in Captivity Paradox) · #363 · `high` · behavioral · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Esther Perel's central insight from Mating in Captivity: the qualities that sustain desire (mystery, novelty, risk, distance, autonomy) are fundamentally in tension with the qualities that sustain domestic partnership (familiarity, security, closeness, predictability, togetherness). The paradox: the more successful you are at creating domestic security, the more you suppress the conditions that generate erotic desire. Couples who achieve deep intimacy and security often lose desire—not because something is wrong but because security and desire have opposing prerequisites. The tension is STRUCTURAL, not personal: you can't fully maximize both simultaneously.
- **How it helps:** Instead of trying to resolve the tension (which is irresolvable), MANAGE it by deliberately creating space for both dynamics. For desire: maintain separateness within togetherness (independent interests, time apart, mystery). For security: maintain reliable presence, shared projects, and emotional availability. Perel's practices: (1) See your partner in contexts where they're autonomous and competent (not just domestic). (2) Maintain erotic space that is separate from domestic space. (3) Don't confuse familiarity with knowledge—stay curious about your partner's inner world. (4) Accept that desire fluctuates and doesn't indicate relationship health.
- **When to use:** When long-term relationships have lost desire despite strong emotional connection; when understanding that the security-desire paradox is structural rather than personal would relieve shame; when relationship design needs to accommodate opposing needs; when the assumption that love should automatically produce desire needs examination.
- **Unique value:** Identifies the STRUCTURAL reason why desire fades in long-term relationships: it's not because something is wrong with the couple—it's because desire and security have opposing prerequisites. This depathologizes desire loss and provides specific design principles for maintaining both dynamics within the same relationship.
- **Limits:** Not all desire loss is the security-desire paradox—medical, psychological, and relational factors also matter. The framework is primarily from Western contexts. Some couples DO maintain both security and desire without deliberate management. The tension can't always be 'designed around.' Individual variation in the security-desire dynamic is significant.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in Perel's synthesis of existential psychology, cultural anthropology, and psychoanalytic theory. Draws on Bowen's differentiation theory, evolutionary psychology on mate selection, and sociological research on how domesticity and erotic imagination operate in opposing registers.

### Authoritative vs. Authoritarian vs. Permissive Parenting (Baumrind) · #541 · `high` · behavioral · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Diana Baumrind's foundational parenting taxonomy identifying three styles based on two dimensions—WARMTH (responsiveness, emotional connection) and CONTROL (expectations, structure, boundaries). Authoritative: HIGH warmth + HIGH structure (firm limits with emotional support and explanation). Authoritarian: LOW warmth + HIGH control (strict obedience without emotional attunement). Permissive: HIGH warmth + LOW control (emotional closeness without structure). A fourth style, neglectful (LOW warmth + LOW control), was added later. Research consistently shows authoritative parenting produces the best outcomes across virtually all measures: academic achievement, emotional regulation, social competence, and self-esteem.
- **How it helps:** Practice authoritative parenting by maintaining BOTH dimensions simultaneously. Set clear expectations and enforce consequences consistently (the control dimension). Explain the reasoning behind rules and invite discussion (the warmth dimension). Maintain emotional connection even during discipline. Adjust expectations to the child's developmental level while maintaining the structure-warmth combination. Common errors: oscillating between authoritarian (when frustrated) and permissive (when guilty)—the consistency of the authoritative style IS the intervention.
- **When to use:** When parenting approach needs calibration toward the evidence-based authoritative style; when the warmth-control matrix would clarify which dimension is underweighted; when understanding why strict-without-warmth and warm-without-structure both produce suboptimal outcomes; when leadership style benefits from the parenting analogy.
- **Unique value:** Demonstrates that warmth and control are INDEPENDENT dimensions, not a trade-off: you don't sacrifice one for the other. The authoritative combination (high both) outperforms all alternatives. This transfers directly to leadership: leaders who combine high expectations with high support outperform both harsh taskmasters and permissive managers.
- **Limits:** Cultural context affects which parenting style is adaptive. Some children's temperament requires modified approaches. The taxonomy simplifies complex parenting behavior. Authoritative parenting is harder to execute than either alternative. Parenting style interacts with socioeconomic context.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in Baumrind's empirical parenting research (1966), developmental psychology, and socialization theory. Draws on attachment theory (Bowlby), self-determination theory (Deci, Ryan), and cross-cultural developmental research questioning universality. Maccoby and Martin extended the original typology to four styles.

### Drama Triangle (Karpman) Dynamics · #1409 · `llm-high` · behavioral · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Stephen Karpman's Drama Triangle identifies three dysfunctional roles people unconsciously rotate through during conflict: the Persecutor (blames, criticizes, dominates — 'This is all your fault'), the Victim (feels helpless, oppressed, powerless — 'There's nothing I can do, it's hopeless'), and the Rescuer (enables, fixes, martyrs — 'Let me handle this for you, you can't do it yourself'). The key insight is that these are roles, not people — individuals shift between all three positions within a single conflict. A Rescuer who feels unappreciated becomes a Persecutor; a Persecutor who faces consequences becomes a Victim; a Victim who gains power becomes a Persecutor. The triangle is self-sustaining: each role enables the others. David Emerald's 'The Empowerment Dynamic' offers the antidote triangle: Creator (replaces Victim — focuses on desired outcomes), Challenger (replaces Persecutor — confronts constructively), and Coach (replaces Rescuer — empowers rather than enables).
- **How it helps:** The Drama Triangle is one of the most immediately useful models for knowledge workers navigating workplace conflict. When you notice a conflict cycling without resolution, map the participants to triangle roles. Which role are you playing? Often, the breakthrough comes from switching roles: if you're rescuing, stop and coach instead; if you're persecuting, challenge constructively; if you're victimizing, identify what you can create. In team settings, naming the triangle ('I think we might be in a drama triangle here') can break the pattern by making the unconscious dynamic visible.
- **When to use:** When a conflict keeps cycling without resolution. When you notice yourself feeling self-righteous (Persecutor), helpless (Victim), or burdened by fixing everyone's problems (Rescuer). When a team dynamic has a scapegoat, a savior, and a critic. When coaching someone stuck in a Victim narrative.
- **Unique value:** Maps the three most common dysfunctional roles in conflict with crystal clarity and provides specific exit routes from each. The insight that roles rotate — that today's Victim is tomorrow's Persecutor — prevents the oversimplification of casting permanent villains and heroes.
- **Limits:** Can oversimplify complex dynamics into three roles. Real power imbalances mean some people genuinely are victimized, not just 'playing Victim.' The model can be weaponized ('You're just being a Victim') to dismiss legitimate grievances. Not all conflict fits the triangle pattern.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in Karpman's transactional analysis extension (1968) building on Berne's TA framework. Draws on psychoanalytic object relations theory, family systems therapy, and the social psychology of interpersonal roles. The Drama Triangle maps pathological relational dynamics among Persecutor, Rescuer, and Victim positions.

Most people have a primary blueprint and a shadow (the type they're least comfortable with).

Use it to: (1) identify your primary blueprint and your partner's—are you speaking the same erotic language? - **Foundations:** Founded on Jaiya's somatic sexology and clinical sex therapy practice. Draws on sensate focus research (Masters & Johnson), attachment theory, neurodiversity research, and somatic psychology traditions mapping diverse arousal pathways as biologically grounded individual variation.

### Recovery Capital Framework · #1724 · `llm-high` · behavioral · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Recovery capital, conceptualized by William White, encompasses total resources for sustaining recovery: social capital (supportive relationships, sober networks), physical capital (finances, housing, health insurance), human capital (education, skills, health, self-efficacy), and cultural/community capital (shared values, group membership, spiritual community). The key insight: recovery isn't merely cessation of substance use but development of sufficient capital across all domains to sustain a life not requiring the substance. This explains why detox alone has ~85% relapse rates—removing the substance without building capital leaves underlying deficits. It also explains socioeconomic recovery disparities: affluent individuals have more capital across all domains.
- **How it helps:** For those supporting colleagues or family in recovery, this framework identifies actionable supports: rebuild social connections, support human capital development (job training), connect to community resources. For organizational leaders, recovery-friendly workplaces mean providing capital across domains—EAP programs, flexible scheduling, health insurance covering treatment. For entrepreneurs, recovery capital reveals massive unmet market needs for the 40+ million Americans in recovery.
- **When to use:** When supporting someone in addiction recovery beyond initial treatment. When designing organizational policies around substance use. When evaluating why some recovery attempts succeed and others fail. When assessing community-level recovery support resources.
- **Unique value:** Shifts the addiction recovery paradigm from 'stop using' to 'build a life worth living without the substance,' providing a multi-dimensional assessment of recovery resources that predicts long-term success better than treatment completion alone.
- **Limits:** Difficult to quantify recovery capital objectively. The framework doesn't address acute medical aspects of addiction treatment. Capital accumulation takes years while craving operates in moments. Cultural capital dimensions may not apply equally across all populations.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in social capital theory (Bourdieu, Coleman, Putnam), addiction recovery research, and community psychology. Draws from White's recovery management philosophy, strengths-based social work, human capital economics, and positive psychology's flourishing framework. Influenced by ecological systems theory (Bronfenbrenner).

### Behavior Change Wheel (Michie) · #1730 · `llm-high` · behavioral · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Susan Michie's Behavior Change Wheel (BCW) synthesizes 19 frameworks into one unified model. At its center: COM-B (Behavior requires Capability, Opportunity, and Motivation). Surrounding it are nine intervention functions: education, persuasion, incentivization, coercion, training, restriction, environmental restructuring, modeling, and enablement. The BCW's diagnostic power forces identification of which COM-B component is the barrier before selecting an intervention—most behavior change failures result from misdiagnosing barriers (providing education when the problem is opportunity, or incentivizing when the problem is capability).
- **How it helps:** When any desired behavior isn't happening, use COM-B diagnostically: Is it Capability (does the person know how?)? Opportunity (does the environment support it?)? Or Motivation (do they want to?)? Most organizations default to education and incentives, when the barrier is often environmental opportunity. If employees aren't using a new tool, the issue might not be training but that the tool requires five clicks where the old one required one. The BCW prevents misdiagnosis by forcing systematic barrier identification before intervention selection.
- **When to use:** When designing any behavior change intervention—personal, organizational, or product-level. When previous change attempts have failed and you need to diagnose why. When choosing between education, incentives, environmental design, or other approaches. When building health, wellness, or productivity products.
- **Unique value:** Provides the field's most comprehensive diagnostic framework, preventing the universal error of jumping to intervention before correctly diagnosing whether the barrier is capability, opportunity, or motivation—and which specific sub-component within each.
- **Limits:** The framework's comprehensiveness can make it overwhelming for simple behavior changes. Classifying barriers into COM-B components requires judgment and can be subjective. The model is better at diagnosis than at predicting which intervention will work in a specific context. Individual psychological complexity may resist categorization.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in health psychology, behavior change science, and implementation science. Michie's COM-B model synthesizes multiple theoretical traditions including self-determination theory, social cognitive theory (Bandura), behavioral economics, and the Theory of Planned Behavior (Ajzen) into a unified diagnostic framework.

### Ecosystem · #21 · `llm-high` · biology · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The community of interacting organisms and their physical environment functioning as an INTERCONNECTED SYSTEM—where each component affects and is affected by the others. Key ecosystem properties: INTERDEPENDENCE (removing one species affects many others through cascading effects), ENERGY FLOW (energy enters through producers and flows through food chains), NUTRIENT CYCLING (matter is recycled, not consumed), and EMERGENT PROPERTIES (the ecosystem has characteristics that no individual species possesses). Keystone species have disproportionate impact relative to their abundance—their removal fundamentally changes the ecosystem. As a mental model: ecosystem thinking reveals that individual elements can only be understood in CONTEXT—and that interventions on one element produce cascading effects throughout the system.
- **How it helps:** Map your system as an ecosystem to identify interdependencies, keystone elements, and cascading effects. The practices: (1) Identify KEYSTONE elements: which components have disproportionate impact? Their failure would cascade. (2) Map INTERDEPENDENCIES: which elements depend on which others? (3) Before ANY intervention: trace cascading effects through the ecosystem. (4) Build REDUNDANCY: ecosystems with more species are more resilient. (5) Protect ecosystem HEALTH: optimize for the whole system, not individual components. For business: your company exists in a business ecosystem—partners, suppliers, customers, regulators, and competitors all interact and co-evolve.
- **When to use:** When understanding system behavior requires seeing interconnections rather than individual components; when keystone elements need identification for protection or leverage; when cascading effects of interventions need tracing; when system resilience depends on diversity and redundancy.
- **Unique value:** Reveals that CONTEXT determines function: every element in an ecosystem has a role that's defined by its relationships to other elements, not by its intrinsic properties alone. This makes RELATIONSHIP MAPPING the primary analytical tool rather than component analysis.
- **Limits:** Ecosystem analogies can oversimplify human-designed systems. Not all systems have ecosystem-like properties. Ecosystem mapping is complex and incomplete. Biological ecosystems and business ecosystems differ significantly. Over-application of the metaphor can be misleading.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in Tansley's foundational ecosystem concept (1935) and systems ecology. Draws on energy flow and nutrient cycling research, Odum's ecosystem ecology, and cybernetics. Informed by Lovelock's Gaia hypothesis and complexity theory. Applied broadly as a systems metaphor for interdependent organizational or market environments.

### Fight-Flight-Freeze-Fawn Response · #1413 · `llm-high` · biology · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The classic fight-or-flight response (Walter Cannon, 1932) has been expanded to include freeze (immobilization, dissociation, playing dead) and fawn (people-pleasing, appeasing, over-accommodating). These four responses represent the autonomic nervous system's survival strategies when threat is perceived: Fight (aggression, boundary assertion, confrontation), Flight (avoidance, withdrawal, escape), Freeze (shutdown, dissociation, paralysis), and Fawn (compliance, people-pleasing, self-abandonment to maintain safety). Pete Walker identified the fawn response as particularly relevant to complex trauma survivors who learned that appeasement was the safest response to threatening caregivers. In the workplace, these four responses map onto conflict styles: the colleague who attacks back (fight), the one who avoids all conflict (flight), the one who goes blank in high-pressure meetings (freeze), and the one who agrees to everything regardless of their true position (fawn). Recognizing your default response is the first step to developing flexible, situation-appropriate responses.
- **How it helps:** Map your default stress response and notice how it shows up in professional conflict. If you default to fight, you may escalate conflicts unnecessarily. If flight, you may avoid necessary confrontations until they become crises. If freeze, you may go blank in exactly the moments that require your clearest thinking. If fawn, you may agree to unreasonable demands and build resentment. Once you know your default, you can prepare compensatory strategies: fight-defaults practice pausing before responding; flight-defaults practice staying in uncomfortable conversations; freeze-defaults practice grounding techniques; fawn-defaults practice articulating boundaries.
- **When to use:** When your conflict response feels automatic rather than chosen. When you notice a consistent pattern across different conflicts (always fighting, always avoiding). When a colleague's behavior in conflict seems disproportionate to the situation. When building self-awareness as a leader who needs flexible conflict responses.
- **Unique value:** Expands the classic two-response model to four, capturing freeze and fawn responses that are extremely common in workplace settings but rarely named. The fawn response in particular explains chronic over-accommodation that many knowledge workers experience.
- **Limits:** Oversimplifies a complex autonomic response into four categories. Most people show mixed responses rather than pure types. Can pathologize adaptive responses — sometimes fighting, fleeing, freezing, or fawning is genuinely the best option. The fawn response concept is clinically newer and less researched.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in the polyvagal theory (Porges, 1994) and the neuroscience of stress responses. Draws on Cannon's fight-or-flight research (1915), trauma theory (van der Kolk, Herman), and Walker's addition of the fawn response from trauma-informed care. Integrates evolutionary biology, neuroscience, and clinical trauma psychology.

### Confidence determines Speed vs. Quality · #41 · `high` · business · CLAIM
- **What it is:** A decision-making heuristic: when you have high confidence in the right direction, optimize for speed of execution; when confidence is low, optimize for quality of information-gathering. This resolves the false dichotomy between 'move fast' and 'be careful'—the answer depends on your current epistemic state. Teams that always move fast make avoidable mistakes; teams that always seek more information suffer analysis paralysis. The meta-skill is accurately calibrating your own confidence level.
- **How it helps:** Directly applicable to building, launching, and scaling products and businesses. Helps prioritize actions, identify market opportunities, and build sustainable competitive advantages.
- **When to use:** When building or iterating on products; when evaluating market opportunities; when deciding resource allocation; when scaling operations.
- **Unique value:** Directly actionable for product and business decisions. 'Confidence determines Speed vs. Quality' provides a specific framework that has been validated in real-world startup and enterprise contexts.
- **Limits:** Business models are context-dependent—what worked in one market may fail in another. Survivorship bias colors our understanding. Execution matters as much as strategy.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in signal detection theory, Bayesian decision theory, and satisficing (Simon). Draws on confidence calibration research in cognitive psychology and Type I/II error trade-offs in statistics. Informed by lean manufacturing's quality control philosophy and agile development's iterative validation approach.

### T-Shaped Professional · #1774 · `llm-high` · career · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The T-shaped professional has deep expertise in one domain (vertical stroke) combined with broad knowledge across disciplines (horizontal stroke). Popularized by Tim Brown at IDEO, it describes the ideal innovation team member: someone contributing deep technical skill while collaborating across disciplines. The vertical bar represents mastery—the skill making you indispensable. The horizontal bar represents empathy and versatility—understanding other functions well enough to collaborate and connect ideas. Organizations of T-shaped people outperform those with pure specialists (I-shaped) or pure generalists (dash-shaped) because they combine depth with cross-pollination.
- **How it helps:** Use this as a professional development roadmap. Early career: focus on building the vertical bar (deep expertise). Mid-career: deliberately broaden the horizontal bar through cross-functional projects and interdisciplinary learning. For hiring managers, evaluate candidates for both depth and breadth. For team composition, ensure T-shaped people whose verticals cover different domains but whose horizontals overlap for communication. Audit your own profile: is your vertical deep enough to be distinctive? Is your horizontal broad enough to collaborate?
- **When to use:** When designing your professional development plan, evaluating team composition, making hiring decisions, or deciding whether to go deeper in your specialty versus broadening into adjacent areas. Particularly useful at the 3-5 year mark when initial depth is established.
- **Unique value:** Provides a visual metaphor making the depth-vs-breadth tradeoff concrete and plannable. Unlike pure generalist or specialist models, it describes how to combine both and explains why organizations need this combination for innovation.
- **Limits:** The model is static—doesn't capture how T-shapes evolve over time. Underspecifies how broad the horizontal needs to be. In rapidly changing fields, yesterday's deep vertical may become obsolete. Some roles genuinely reward I-shaped depth over T-shaped breadth.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in organizational behavior, innovation management, and human capital theory. Draws from the IDEO innovation research tradition (Tim Brown), T-shaped skills concept from technology management, cross-functional team research, and Nonaka and Takeuchi's knowledge creation theory requiring both tacit depth and explicit breadth.

### Career Lattice vs. Ladder · #1777 · `high` · career · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The traditional career 'ladder' assumes vertical progression: analyst to manager to director to VP. The career 'lattice,' developed by Cathy Benko at Deloitte, recognizes that modern careers move laterally, diagonally, and sometimes temporarily downward. A lattice career might involve moving from marketing to product management, then to a startup, then back to a larger company. Each move builds different skills that compound over time. The lattice reflects modern realities: flatter hierarchies, project-based work, and longer working lives requiring reinvention. 'Down' moves can be strategically brilliant when they open access to faster-growing domains.
- **How it helps:** This liberates career planning from the anxiety of 'falling behind' on a linear ladder. When evaluating opportunities, ask 'does this add a new dimension to my lattice?' rather than 'is this a promotion?' Practically useful for: (1) justifying lateral moves, (2) designing career paths that build versatility, (3) evaluating non-obvious opportunities. For managers, it helps retain talent by offering lateral development when vertical promotions aren't available. For mid-career professionals, it normalizes the zigzag path most successful careers follow.
- **When to use:** When evaluating a career move that isn't a clear promotion, when feeling stuck with no vertical room, when advising someone considering a lateral or downward move, or when designing development programs in flat organizations.
- **Unique value:** Replaces the single-axis (up/down) career evaluation with a multi-axis framework, legitimizing moves that look like 'steps backward' but are actually strategic positioning. Reflects how careers actually work in the 21st century.
- **Limits:** Some industries and cultures still rigidly value vertical progression—lattice moves can genuinely hurt in hierarchical environments. The model can rationalize aimless job-hopping. Without strategic intent, a lattice career becomes a random walk.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in organizational sociology, career theory, and human capital economics. Draws from Arthur and Rousseau's boundaryless career concept, Schein's career anchors model, Super's career development theory, and Hall's protean career theory. Influenced by post-industrial labor market research and knowledge economy dynamics.

### Portfolio Career Model · #1790 · `llm-high` · career · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Charles Handy predicted that single-employer careers would give way to a 'portfolio' of concurrent income streams, projects, and roles. A portfolio career combines part-time roles, freelancing, board positions, consulting, and passion projects. The model identifies four work types: (1) wage work—paid by hour or month, (2) fee work—paid per project, (3) gift work—volunteer and creative work done for purpose, (4) study work—deliberate learning and skill development. A well-designed portfolio balances these while maintaining income. The advantage is resilience: when one stream declines, others compensate. The challenge is managing complexity and lack of benefits.
- **How it helps:** For knowledge workers considering alternatives to traditional employment: (1) start building portfolio elements while employed—freelance projects, advisory roles, content creation, (2) balance income stability (wage/fee work) with purpose (gift work) and growth (study work), (3) calculate minimum viable income before transitioning. - **When to use:** When productivity is low despite adequate time and talent; when work feels either boring or overwhelming (both indicate challenge-skill mismatch); when designing work environments, assignments, or learning progressions; when building personal practices for peak performance.

Organizational conditions (interruptions, meeting culture) can make flow structurally impossible regardless of individual effort.

Connected to self-determination theory (Deci and Ryan), attention research in cognitive psychology, and performance science on challenge-skill balance.

### Attachment Theory (Bowlby/Ainsworth) · #876 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The developmental psychology framework showing that early relationships with caregivers create internal 'working models' of relationships that persist into adulthood—shaping how people approach intimacy, trust, conflict, and dependency. Secure attachment (caregiver was consistently responsive) produces adults comfortable with intimacy and independence. Anxious attachment (caregiver was inconsistently responsive) produces adults who crave closeness but fear abandonment. Avoidant attachment (caregiver was emotionally unavailable) produces adults who value independence and suppress emotional needs. These patterns are remarkably stable and predict relationship behavior decades later.
- **How it helps:** Understand your own attachment style and those of key people in your life and work. In relationships: attachment style predicts conflict patterns better than the content of disagreements. Anxious partners escalate to get reassurance; avoidant partners withdraw to manage overwhelm—creating the classic pursue-withdraw cycle. In leadership: attachment style affects how you handle dependency (team members depending on you) and how you respond to criticism. Self-awareness is the intervention: knowing your pattern doesn't eliminate it but creates space between trigger and response.
- **When to use:** When relationship patterns repeat despite different partners or contexts; when leadership style creates predictable but dysfunctional team dynamics; when conflict escalation follows the same script regardless of content; when designing team environments that need to account for different relationship orientations.
- **Unique value:** Provides the most empirically validated framework for understanding why people relate the way they do—tracing adult behavior to specific early relationship patterns. Unlike personality models that describe WHAT people do, attachment theory explains WHY (the internal working model) and predicts WHEN (the triggering conditions).
- **Limits:** Attachment styles aren't deterministic—people can develop 'earned security' through later relationships and therapy. The categories are simplifications of continuous variation. Cultural context shapes attachment norms. Over-pathologizing insecure attachment can create identity limitations. The theory focuses on early relationships and may underweight adult experiences.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in Bowlby's ethological attachment theory drawing on Lorenz and Harlow, and Ainsworth's Strange Situation empirical work. Draws on object relations psychoanalytic theory (Winnicott, Klein), developmental neuroscience, and evolutionary psychology. Main and colleagues extended it to adult attachment representations.

### Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs · #879 · `high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Abraham Maslow's motivational theory that human needs are arranged in a hierarchy: physiological needs (food, water, shelter) → safety needs (security, stability) → belonging needs (love, community) → esteem needs (respect, achievement) → self-actualization (reaching full potential). Lower-level needs must be sufficiently satisfied before higher-level needs become motivating. A hungry person doesn't care about self-actualization; a person with unmet safety needs doesn't prioritize esteem. Applied to management: compensation satisfies lower needs; once adequate, further compensation has diminishing motivational returns, and higher needs (belonging, purpose, growth) become the primary motivators.
- **How it helps:** Diagnose which level of the hierarchy is currently unmet for yourself, your team, or your customers—then address THAT level. If basic security needs (job stability, financial adequacy) are unmet, purpose-driven motivation will fail. If belonging needs are unmet (isolated, excluded), achievement goals will feel hollow. The most common leadership error: trying to motivate with higher-level rewards (purpose, growth) while lower-level needs (fair pay, job security, belonging) are unmet. Meet lower needs first. Then: once lower needs are met, don't keep investing there—move up the hierarchy to belonging, purpose, and growth.
- **When to use:** When motivation strategies aren't working (you may be targeting the wrong level); when compensation increases aren't increasing motivation (lower needs are already met—shift to higher needs); when new employees need different motivational approaches than veteran employees; when designing organizational culture and need to understand the motivational hierarchy.
- **Unique value:** Provides the diagnostic hierarchy for motivation: always start by identifying which level is currently unmet. The model's greatest practical value isn't the specific five levels—it's the principle that unmet lower needs block the motivational power of higher needs. Address needs in order, not in whichever order seems most appealing.
- **Limits:** The strict hierarchy has been challenged—people can pursue self-actualization while lower needs are partially unmet. The five levels are somewhat arbitrary. Cultural variation affects which needs are primary. The theory lacks strong empirical support for the rigid hierarchy. Individual variation is significant—some people are strongly motivated by esteem even when safety is threatened.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in humanistic psychology (Rogers, Goldstein) and Maslow's synthesis of clinical observation and motivational theory. Draws on evolutionary psychology's threat-detection priority systems and existentialist philosophy. Critiqued and refined through cross-cultural psychology and self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan).
- **Recognition cue:** "Which need-level is this person operating at right now?"
- **Worked example:** Crisis response (safety first), marketing positioning, employee motivation design

### Apology Languages Framework · #1418 · `llm-high` · communication · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Gary Chapman and Jennifer Thomas identified five distinct 'apology languages' — different ways people give and expect to receive apologies — explaining why some apologies land and others feel hollow even when sincere. Most people have a primary apology language they need to hear for an apology to feel authentic. Mismatches create frustration: if you need to hear 'I was wrong' (Accepting Responsibility) but receive 'What can I do to fix it?' (Making Restitution), the apology feels evasive even if sincerely meant.
- **How it helps:** This framework prevents the common professional situation where a genuine apology fails to repair because it's in the wrong language. Before apologizing, consider what the other person needs to hear: Does this person need to know you understand the impact (Expressing Regret), that you own the mistake (Accepting Responsibility), that you'll make concrete amends (Restitution), that it won't happen again (Repentance), or that the relationship is restored (Forgiveness)? When in doubt, include all five — a comprehensive apology covers all languages. For managers, knowing your team members' apology languages transforms your ability to repair after mistakes.
- **When to use:** When an apology you gave wasn't received well despite being sincere. When someone has apologized to you but it doesn't feel like enough (your language may not have been spoken). When crafting an apology for a significant professional or personal mistake. When coaching others on repair skills.
- **Unique value:** Explains the specific mechanism behind 'hollow apologies' — the mismatch between the apologizer's language and the recipient's need. This transforms vague advice to 'apologize sincerely' into a diagnostic framework with five specific interventions.
- **Limits:** The five languages are not independently validated to the same degree as the underlying concept. Some situations require external accountability, not just apology. Cultural variation in apology norms is significant. Can reduce genuine moral reckoning to a communication technique.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in Chapman's love languages framework (1992) and clinical psychology of forgiveness. Draws on Tavris & Aronson's social psychology of excuse-making, Worthington's forgiveness pyramid model, and the sociology of apology (Lazare). Related to Schneider's moral philosophy of apology and restorative justice principles.

- **What it is:** The distinction between two classes of equity ownership with different rights: common stock provides voting rights and residual claim on earnings (dividends and appreciation) but is LAST in line during liquidation. ### Perfect Competition · #1325 · `llm-high` · economics · CLAIM
- **What it is:** A theoretical market structure with many buyers and sellers, identical products, perfect information, and zero entry barriers—where no individual firm can influence the market price. In perfect competition, economic profit is zero in the long run because any positive profit attracts new entrants who compete it away. Competitive strategy is essentially the art of avoiding perfect competition.
- **How it helps:** If your business has no meaningful differentiation, low switching costs, easy entry for competitors, and transparent pricing, you're approaching perfect competition—and your margins will reflect it. - **When to use:** When evaluating whether a market offers sustainable profit potential; when margins are compressing and you're wondering why; when designing strategy—every strategic action is an attempt to move away from perfect competition; when assessing competitive moats—each moat violates one or more assumptions of perfect competition.
- **Unique value:** Provides the theoretical benchmark against which all strategic advantage is defined. If you can't articulate which assumption of perfect competition your business violates, you don't have a competitive advantage. This makes the abstract concept immediately actionable for strategy.
- **Limits:** No real market is perfectly competitive, so the model is a theoretical reference point rather than a description of reality. The model assumes rational actors and ignores transaction costs, which are significant in every real market. Some businesses (commodities) operate close to perfect competition and survive through operational efficiency rather than strategic differentiation.

### Oligopoly · #1326 · `llm-high` · economics · CLAIM
- **What it is:** A market structure dominated by a small number of large firms whose strategic decisions are interdependent—each firm must consider rivals' likely responses when making pricing, investment, or marketing decisions. - **How it helps:** If you're in an oligopoly (most mature industries), your strategy must account for competitive response. Price cuts that make sense in isolation can trigger price wars that destroy industry profitability. Product innovations get copied quickly. Understanding which game you're playing determines which moves make sense.
- **When to use:** When operating in any market with 2-6 dominant players; when considering pricing changes that rivals will see and respond to; when evaluating whether to compete or differentiate; when assessing industry profitability dynamics and why margins stabilize or collapse.
- **Unique value:** Reveals why competitive strategy in concentrated markets differs fundamentally from strategy in fragmented markets. In oligopoly, your optimal move depends on your competitor's optimal move, which depends on your optimal move—creating strategic interdependence that requires game-theoretic thinking rather than simple optimization.
- **Limits:** Tacit coordination can become illegal collusion. Oligopoly analysis assumes rational actors, but real firms make emotional and political decisions. Industry structure changes—oligopolies can be disrupted by new entrants with different business models. The number of firms alone doesn't determine market dynamics; differentiation, capacity, and entry barriers matter too.

### Therapeutic Window (Too Little vs Too Much) · #529 · `high` · health · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The pharmacological concept that every treatment has a range where it's effective—below the window, the dose is too low to help; above it, the dose becomes toxic. The therapeutic window captures the critical insight that MORE is not always better and that the same substance that heals at one dose kills at another. Applied broadly: every intervention—management attention, regulation, investment, communication—has a therapeutic window. Too little management is neglect; optimal management is productive; too much management is micromanagement. The dose defines whether it's medicine or poison.
- **How it helps:** For any input you're considering increasing, ask: am I in the therapeutic window, approaching its upper bound, or already past it? The symptoms of being above the therapeutic window are paradoxical—the same input that helped when you started now seems to be making things worse. More meetings don't produce more alignment; they produce meeting fatigue. More features don't produce more satisfaction; they produce complexity. - **When to use:** When more effort or resources are producing worse outcomes (past the upper bound); when interventions seem to have no effect (below the lower bound); when calibrating the right amount of any organizational input—oversight, communication, process, support; when designing dosing strategies for any intervention.
- **Unique value:** Applies pharmacological precision to the vague advice 'find the right balance.' Instead of 'don't micromanage but don't neglect,' the therapeutic window says: there's a specific range where management attention is helpful, and the boundaries are identifiable through outcome monitoring. This makes 'balance' measurable.

In complex systems, multiple inputs interact, making it hard to isolate any single input's window. The metaphor from pharmacology to management is imperfect—organizational 'doses' are harder to measure than drug doses.
- **Foundations:** Founded in pharmacology (therapeutic index), toxicology, and clinical pharmacokinetics. Connects to systems biology (homeostatic set points), hormesis research, and optimal control theory in engineering. Therapeutic windows exist across biological, psychological, and organizational systems wherever dose-response curves are nonlinear.

### Evidence Hierarchy (EBM Pyramid) · #2012 · `high` · health · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Evidence-based medicine organizes evidence into a hierarchy from weakest to strongest: expert opinion and case reports at the bottom, case-control studies above them, cohort studies higher, randomized controlled trials (RCTs) near the top, and systematic reviews and meta-analyses of RCTs at the apex. Each level up provides more protection against bias: case reports can't control for confounders, observational studies are vulnerable to selection bias, RCTs control for both but may lack generalizability, and systematic reviews synthesize multiple RCTs to increase power and reduce the influence of any single flawed study. The practitioner's nuance: the hierarchy is a guideline, not a straitjacket. A well-designed cohort study may provide better evidence than a poorly designed RCT. Some questions (rare diseases, surgical techniques, long-term outcomes) can never be studied with RCTs.
- **How it helps:** This hierarchy provides a universal framework for evaluating the quality of evidence in any domain. When someone cites 'research shows...', you can ask: what kind of research? ### Decision Tree · #392 · `llm-high` · mathematical · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Scott Page's formal model of sequential decision-making under uncertainty: a branching structure where each node represents a decision point or chance event, each branch represents a possible action or outcome, and each terminal node represents a final outcome with its associated payoff and probability. Decision trees make complex sequential decisions tractable by: decomposing them into a series of simpler choices, making probabilities and payoffs explicit at each branch, and enabling backward induction (working from outcomes back to optimal current decisions). The expected value at each node equals the probability-weighted sum of downstream values—revealing which current choice maximizes expected long-term value.
- **How it helps:** For complex sequential decisions: draw the tree. The practice: (1) Map decision points (squares—you choose), chance events (circles—nature or others choose), and outcomes (triangles—terminal values). (2) Assign probabilities to chance branches and values to outcomes. (3) Work backward: at each node, calculate expected value by multiplying probability × value for each branch. (4) At decision nodes, choose the branch with highest expected value. The power of the tree: it forces explicit consideration of ALL possible paths, not just the one your intuition favors.
- **When to use:** When sequential decisions with uncertain outcomes need systematic analysis; when making the full range of possible outcomes explicit would improve decision quality; when backward induction from desired outcomes would clarify current choices; when the structure of a complex decision needs to be decomposed into manageable steps.
- **Unique value:** Forces the decision-maker to make EXPLICIT what is usually IMPLICIT: the probabilities, the payoffs, and the full range of possible paths. Most decision errors come from considering only a subset of possibilities—the tree structure requires comprehensive mapping. This alone improves decision quality significantly.
- **Limits:** Probability estimates are often unreliable. Trees become unwieldy for complex decisions with many branches. The model assumes rational evaluation that may not reflect psychological reality. Some decisions have outcomes that resist quantification. Decision trees can produce false precision.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in Bayesian probability theory, decision theory (von Neumann & Morgenstern), and operations research. Decision trees formalize sequential choices under uncertainty. Draws on expected utility theory, information theory (value of information), and normative models of rational choice in management science.

The practice: both images are tools—use the one that's most useful for your current purpose, and recognize that neither is the complete truth.
- **When to use:** When apparent conflicts between everyday experience and scientific description need resolution; when choosing the appropriate level of description for a given purpose; when understanding that 'common sense' and 'science' describe the same reality at different levels; when the manifest/scientific distinction would prevent category errors.
- **Unique value:** Resolves the apparent conflict between common sense and science by showing they're different IMAGES of the same reality rather than competing claims about different realities. This prevents both naive scientism (only atoms are real) and naive realism (only experience is real).

- **Foundations:** Founded by Wilfrid Sellars in 'Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind' (1956). Manifest image is the human life-world of persons and purposes; scientific image is the framework of particles and laws—reconciling them is a central philosophical project.

- **Foundations:** Grounded in decision theory and time management research. Draws on Eisenhower's urgent-important distinction, opportunity cost theory in economics, and attention economics (Simon). The optimal is both.

### Signaling Game (Costly Signals) · #1006 · `llm-high` · strategy · CLAIM
- **What it is:** A game theory model where one party has private information (their type, quality, or intention) and must communicate it credibly to another party who can't directly observe it. The solution: costly signals that only the genuine article can afford to send. A warranty signals product confidence because only a quality manufacturer can afford to honor it. An MBA signals ability because only capable students can complete the program. Advertising spending signals product quality because only profitable products can afford sustained campaigns. The informational content isn't in the signal itself—it's in the COST, which filters honest from dishonest signalers.
- **How it helps:** Design your signaling strategy around cost structures that only genuine quality can sustain. In hiring: what signals can genuinely skilled candidates produce that unskilled candidates can't fake? (Money-back guarantees, free trials, transparent pricing, public performance data.) When evaluating others' signals: calibrate trust to signal cost. Cheap signals (verbal promises, stated intentions) deserve less weight than costly signals (contractual commitments, resource investments).
- **When to use:** When information asymmetry exists and you need to communicate or evaluate hidden qualities; when designing hiring, partnership evaluation, or due diligence processes; when building credibility with skeptical audiences; when analyzing why some credentials or actions are more persuasive than others.
- **Unique value:** Provides the formal economic framework for 'actions speak louder than words.' The model specifies exactly WHY actions are more credible—their cost filters honest signalers from dishonest ones. This converts trust evaluation from gut feeling to structural analysis: how costly is this signal to fake?
- **Limits:** Signal costs can be discriminatory—expensive signals exclude capable but resource-poor signalers. Not all costly actions are informative signals. Signal inflation (credential creep) erodes the information content of once-reliable signals. The model assumes rational signal interpretation, but people are often poor at evaluating signal costs.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in signaling theory economics (Spence's job market signaling, 1973; Zahavi's handicap principle in biology). Draws on information economics, game theory of asymmetric information (Akerlof's lemon problem), and evolutionary biology. Connects to marketing theory, credibility research, and mechanism design.

### Stag Hunt (Cooperation vs Safety) · #1007 · `high` · strategy · CLAIM
- **What it is:** A game theory model where two hunters must independently choose: cooperate to hunt a stag (high reward but requires both to participate) or individually hunt a hare (lower reward but guaranteed). The stag hunt captures a fundamental social dilemma: cooperation produces the best outcome but only if BOTH parties cooperate—and you can't be sure the other will. Unlike the Prisoner's Dilemma (where defection dominates), the stag hunt has two equilibria: mutual cooperation (stag) and mutual safety (hare). The challenge isn't incentive alignment—both prefer the stag—but trust and coordination.
- **How it helps:** Recognize stag hunts in your business and personal life: situations where cooperation produces the best outcome but requires trust that the other party will also cooperate. Joint ventures, strategic partnerships, team projects with shared accountability, and organizational change all have stag hunt dynamics. The intervention: build trust through small, verifiable cooperative acts before the big stag hunt. Signal your commitment credibly. Reduce the cost of cooperation failure (safety nets). Make the cooperative choice as safe as possible while preserving its benefits.
- **When to use:** When cooperation would produce the best outcome but trust is insufficient; when both parties want to cooperate but fear the other will defect to safety; when designing partnerships or team structures that require mutual commitment; when the barrier to collaboration is trust rather than incentives.
- **Unique value:** Distinguishes the trust problem (stag hunt) from the incentive problem (prisoner's dilemma). Many situations mistakenly analyzed as prisoner's dilemmas—where defection seems rational—are actually stag hunts, where both parties WANT to cooperate but can't trust that the other will. The intervention is completely different: build trust, don't change incentives.
- **Limits:** Real-world situations rarely map perfectly to the clean 2×2 stag hunt matrix. Trust is hard to build and easy to destroy. Some stag hunts have asymmetric risks that the simple model doesn't capture. The model assumes both parties equally prefer cooperation—in practice, preferences may differ.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in game theory and Rousseau's stag hunt parable in 'Discourse on Inequality' (1754). Draws on Brian Skyrms's analysis ('The Stag Hunt and the Evolution of Social Structure', 2004), coordination game theory, and social contract theory. Connects to collective action theory, trust research, and evolutionary game theory.

### Prisoner's Dilemma (extended) · #1057 · `high` · strategy · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The foundational game theory model where two rational agents, each acting in self-interest, produce an outcome that's worse for BOTH than if they had cooperated. Each prisoner can cooperate (stay silent) or defect (betray). If both cooperate, both get light sentences. If both defect, both get heavy sentences. If one cooperates and the other defects, the defector goes free and the cooperator gets the worst sentence. Individual rationality dictates defection—but mutual defection is worse than mutual cooperation. The dilemma captures the fundamental tension between individual incentives and collective welfare that pervades business, politics, and social life.
- **How it helps:** Recognize prisoner's dilemma dynamics in competitive and collaborative situations. The critical factors that shift outcomes toward cooperation: (1) Repeated interactions (Axelrod's tournaments showed tit-for-tat wins in repeated games). (2) Reputation (defection in one game spreads to other relationships). (3) Communication (discussing intentions before deciding). (4) Enforceable agreements (converting the dilemma into a coordination problem). In business: most competitive dynamics that feel like prisoner's dilemmas are actually REPEATED games where reputation matters—which dramatically changes the optimal strategy from defection toward conditional cooperation.
- **When to use:** When competitive dynamics are producing mutual harm; when two parties would both benefit from cooperation but neither trusts the other to cooperate; when designing agreements, partnerships, or market structures that need to overcome self-interested defection; when evaluating whether a competitive interaction is one-shot (favors defection) or repeated (favors cooperation).
- **Unique value:** The single most important model in strategic interaction: it reveals why individually rational behavior produces collectively irrational outcomes, and identifies the conditions (repetition, reputation, communication, enforcement) that shift equilibria toward cooperation. Understanding when you're in a prisoner's dilemma versus when you're not is the foundation of strategic thinking.
- **Limits:** Real-world interactions are rarely as clean as the 2×2 matrix. The model assumes rational actors with clear payoffs—reality is messier. Some 'prisoner's dilemmas' are actually coordination games or stag hunts with different optimal strategies. The model can justify preemptive defection ('they'll defect anyway'). Not all cooperation failures are prisoner's dilemmas.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in game theory (Tucker, 1950) and repeated game theory (Folk Theorem). Draws on Axelrod's cooperation research, mechanism design theory, and behavioral economics on reciprocity and punishment. Connects to institutional economics on contract enforcement, collective action theory, and evolutionary game theory on social norms.
- *Secondary roles:* — · *Collection:* mental-model-canon|game-theory

### SWOT Analysis · #1064 · `high` · strategy · CLAIM

While ubiquitous, SWOT is most valuable as a conversation starter, not a decision tool—it identifies factors but doesn't weight them or suggest strategy. The common failure is listing factors without analyzing interactions: the most strategic insights come from matching strengths to opportunities and identifying weaknesses that expose you to threats.
- **How it helps:** Provides frameworks for navigating competitive landscapes, allocating resources under uncertainty, and making high-stakes decisions with incomplete information.
- **When to use:** When facing strategic decisions under uncertainty; when multiple stakeholders have competing interests; when the stakes are high and reversibility is low.
- **Unique value:** Provides a specific strategic lens that reframes competitive dynamics. 'SWOT Analysis' reveals options and constraints that are invisible from conventional perspectives.
- **Limits:** Strategic models assume rationality that real actors may not exhibit. Competitive situations are unique—historical patterns may not repeat. Over-analysis can paralyze action.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in strategic planning and business policy, originating with the Harvard Business School (Andrews, 1971). Draws on industrial organization economics (Porter's five forces), resource-based view (Barney), and PEST analysis. Connects to strategic management theory, competitive analysis frameworks, and organizational capability assessment.
- **Recognition cue:** "Which quadrant is this factor?"
- **Worked example:** Strategic planning, competitive analysis, project review

### Satisficing vs. Maximizing · #1065 · `high` · strategy · CLAIM

### Negative-Sum Game · #1312 · `llm-high` · strategy · CLAIM
- **What it is:** An interaction where total value is destroyed—all parties collectively end up with less than they started. War, litigation that costs more than the dispute, price wars that destroy industry margins, and arms races that consume resources without changing relative position are all negative-sum games. Unlike zero-sum (redistribution) or positive-sum (value creation), negative-sum games leave everyone poorer. They typically emerge from escalation dynamics, mutual distrust, or the absence of coordination mechanisms.
- **How it helps:** Recognize when you're in a negative-sum dynamic—before it escalates to the point where exit costs exceed continuation costs. The warning signs: both parties are spending more to compete than the prize is worth; the competition itself is degrading the asset being competed over; retaliation spirals are escalating without resolution. The exit strategy: unilateral de-escalation (risky but can break the spiral), third-party mediation, or reframing the interaction as positive-sum.
- **When to use:** When competition is destroying more value than it's capturing; when escalation dynamics are consuming resources disproportionate to the stakes; when both parties in a conflict are getting worse off over time; when designing institutional structures to prevent destructive competition.
- **Unique value:** Adds a critical category beyond the zero-sum/positive-sum dichotomy. Many situations labeled 'competitive' are actually value-destroying for all participants. Naming the category makes it visible, which is the first step toward avoiding or exiting these dynamics.
- **Limits:** Sometimes participating in a negative-sum game is necessary to prevent a worse outcome (deterrence). Identifying a game as negative-sum requires calculating total costs across all parties, which may not be visible. The label can be used to pressure parties into unilateral concession. Some short-term negative-sum dynamics lead to long-term positive-sum outcomes (creative destruction).
- **Foundations:** Formal game theory (von Neumann & Morgenstern) classifying games by payoff structure. Negative-sum outcomes arise from transaction costs, conflict destruction, or value dissipation. Extends Nash equilibrium analysis to situations where aggregate welfare declines through strategic interaction.
- *Secondary roles:* PROPERTY · *Collection:* game-theory

### Win-Lose · #1313 · `llm-high` · strategy · CLAIM
- **What it is:** A framing in which one party's gain is the other's loss—a zero-sum game. While some situations are genuinely zero-sum (dividing a fixed pie, competitive markets with fixed demand), most real-world situations have potential for positive-sum outcomes that win-lose framing obscures. The danger of win-lose thinking isn't that it's always wrong—it's that it becomes a self-fulfilling prophecy. When you approach a negotiation as win-lose, you behave competitively, which triggers competitive behavior in the other party, creating the zero-sum dynamic you assumed.
- **How it helps:** Before any negotiation or competitive interaction, ask: is this genuinely zero-sum, or am I imposing win-lose framing on a situation that could be positive-sum? Most negotiations have zones of mutual gain that adversarial framing prevents discovering. The test: could we expand the pie before dividing it? If the answer is 'maybe,' win-lose framing is costing you value. Reserve win-lose thinking for genuinely zero-sum situations and approach everything else as potential win-win.
- **When to use:** When entering negotiations and need to assess whether the situation is zero-sum or positive-sum; when competitive framing is preventing creative solutions; when a conflict seems intractable—reframing from win-lose to potential win-win often reveals new options; when designing incentive structures that pit parties against each other unnecessarily.
- **Unique value:** Exposes how framing determines strategy. In genuinely zero-sum games, competitive strategy is rational. But the most common error is treating positive-sum games as zero-sum, which destroys potential value for everyone. The model's value is in the diagnostic question: is this really zero-sum?
- **Limits:** Some situations genuinely are zero-sum—denying this is naive. In competitive markets, your gain often is your competitor's loss. Over-applying win-win thinking can lead to weak negotiation where you concede value unnecessarily. The skill is correctly identifying which situations are zero-sum and which aren't.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in zero-sum game theory (von Neumann & Morgenstern) and distributive bargaining theory. Draws on competitive strategy frameworks and resource-based economics where one party's gain requires another's equivalent loss. Foundational to negotiation research by Raiffa and Lax & Sebenius.
- *Secondary roles:* PROPERTY · *Collection:* game-theory
