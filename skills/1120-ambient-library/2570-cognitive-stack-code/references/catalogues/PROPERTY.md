### Relational Ambivalence (Wanting Contradictory Things Simultaneously) · #364 · `llm-high` · behavioral · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The recognition that people can simultaneously hold contradictory feelings and desires in close relationships—wanting intimacy AND independence, craving security AND excitement, loving someone AND being furious with them. Ambivalence isn't confusion or weakness; it's the normal state of complex human attachment. Gottman's research shows that all relationships contain irreconcilable differences—roughly 69% of conflicts in lasting partnerships are perpetual, rooted in fundamental personality and value differences that never resolve, only get managed.
- **How it helps:** Stop trying to eliminate ambivalence and learn to hold it. In partnerships: 'I want closeness AND space' isn't a problem to solve—it's a polarity to manage. In professional relationships: ambivalence about a colleague (valuable AND difficult) is normal and manageable. The intervention isn't choosing one feeling and suppressing the other—it's acknowledging both and making decisions that honor the most important needs of the moment. Attempting to resolve ambivalence by eliminating one pole usually backfires.
- **When to use:** When relationships feel contradictory and you're trying to figure out which feeling is 'real' (both are); when recurring relationship conflicts refuse to resolve (they may be perpetual); when the urge to simplify complex feelings into a clear answer is strong; when making relationship decisions during emotionally charged moments.
- **Unique value:** Normalizes the most common source of relationship anxiety: contradictory feelings. Most people interpret ambivalence as a sign something is wrong. This model reveals it as the universal condition of complex attachment, which reframes the goal from 'resolve the contradiction' to 'manage it wisely.'
- **Limits:** Some ambivalence masks genuine incompatibility that should lead to separation. Permanent management of severe ambivalence can be exhausting. The model can be used to justify staying in harmful relationships ('all relationships have conflict'). The distinction between healthy ambivalence and toxic dynamics requires honest assessment.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in existential philosophy (Kierkegaard's either/or), psychodynamic object relations theory, and dialectical behavior therapy's dialectical tension framework. Draws on attachment theory's approach-avoidance conflict and social psychology research on ambivalence in close relationships.

### Love Maps (Deep Knowledge of Partner's Inner World) · #368 · `llm-high` · behavioral · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Gottman's concept that strong relationships are built on detailed knowledge of each other's inner world—dreams, fears, preferences, stressors, aspirations, history, and current concerns. A 'love map' is the cognitive map you carry of your partner's psychological landscape. Couples with detailed love maps navigate conflict better because they understand each other's triggers, can give context to reactions, and maintain fondness during disagreements. Love maps erode through neglect—when partners stop updating their knowledge of each other's evolving inner world, they become strangers living together.
- **How it helps:** Actively maintain and update your knowledge of the people most important to you—partners, key team members, close collaborators. Ask questions about their current concerns, aspirations, and stressors. The business application: leaders with detailed 'maps' of their team members' motivations, career aspirations, and personal circumstances lead more effectively because they can tailor support, anticipate reactions, and build genuine connection. The practice: regular one-on-ones that go beyond task updates to include personal check-ins.
- **When to use:** When relationships feel distant despite regular contact (love maps have gone stale); when conflict escalates because partners or team members feel unknown or misunderstood; when onboarding new team members or deepening existing professional relationships; when designing relationship maintenance practices for personal or professional contexts.
- **Unique value:** Operationalizes 'knowing someone deeply' into a specific, improvable practice: building and updating a detailed cognitive map of their inner world. Most relationship advice is about behavior (do this, don't do that); love maps addresses the knowledge foundation that makes appropriate behavior possible.
- **Limits:** People change, and love maps require constant updating—a map of who someone was five years ago may be inaccurate. Deep knowledge of someone's inner world doesn't guarantee good behavior toward them. The concept was developed for romantic partnerships and may need adaptation for professional contexts. Some people are private and resist having their inner world mapped.
- **Foundations:** Based on Gottman's research on cognitive room—mental maps of partners' inner worlds. Rooted in social cognition theory, mentalizing/theory of mind research (Baron-Cohen, Frith), and intimacy theory. Draws on Aron's self-expansion model and relationship science on empathic accuracy.

### Differentiation in Intimacy (Schnarch): Holding Yourself Together · #942 · `llm-high` · behavioral · CLAIM
- **What it is:** David Schnarch's framework where the capacity for genuine intimacy requires DIFFERENTIATION: the ability to maintain your own identity, values, and emotional equilibrium while in close emotional contact with a partner—especially when they're anxious, angry, or pressuring you to change. Undifferentiated people: lose themselves in relationships (fusion), avoid conflict to maintain peace (emotional fusion), can't tolerate their partner's anxiety without being destabilized, and either conform to the partner's wishes or withdraw entirely. Differentiated people: can stay connected during disagreement, tolerate their own and their partner's discomfort, maintain their position without attacking the other's, and grow toward each other through honest self-confrontation rather than accommodation.
- **How it helps:** Practice 'holding yourself together' when your partner's emotional state pressures you to abandon your position or lose your emotional center. The growth edge: instead of either capitulating (to reduce their anxiety) or withdrawing (to reduce your own anxiety), STAY PRESENT while maintaining your position. This requires self-soothing: managing your own anxiety rather than requiring your partner to manage it for you. Schnarch's insight: the person most willing to tolerate discomfort in the relationship has the most capacity for growth—because they can stay engaged with difficult truths rather than fleeing into comfort.
- **When to use:** When relationship growth requires tolerating discomfort rather than seeking immediate comfort; when emotional fusion is preventing authentic intimacy; when the capacity for honest self-disclosure during conflict needs development; when understanding that differentiation—not merger—produces genuine intimacy.
- **Unique value:** Overturns the romantic ideal that intimacy means merging or losing yourself in the other. Schnarch shows the opposite: genuine intimacy requires STRONG SELFHOOD (differentiation) because only a differentiated person can remain present during the discomfort that real intimacy inevitably produces. Undifferentiated people flee into fusion or withdrawal—neither produces genuine closeness.
- **Limits:** Differentiation is a lifelong developmental process, not an achievable end state. Some relationship contexts (abuse, severe dysfunction) require external support rather than individual differentiation work. The framework can be used to pathologize healthy interdependence. Cultural context shapes appropriate differentiation levels.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in family systems theory (Bowen), object relations psychoanalytic theory (Fairbairn, Winnicott), and Schnarch's synthesis of Bowenian differentiation with sexual therapy. Draws on attachment theory and existential psychology's emphasis on self-definition as the foundation of genuine intimacy and desire.

### Ambiguous Loss (Boss) · #1460 · `llm-high` · behavioral · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Pauline Boss identified a devastating form of grief that occurs when loss is unclear—when someone is physically present but psychologically absent (dementia, addiction, traumatic brain injury) or physically absent but psychologically present (missing persons, estranged family, ambiguous relationship endings). Unlike clear-cut death, ambiguous loss resists closure because the loss cannot be verified or ritualized. The bereaved are frozen: they can't grieve because the person isn't definitively gone, and they can't move forward because the person isn't definitively present. Boss found this produces uniquely high levels of anxiety, depression, and family conflict because social systems have no scripts for ambiguous loss—no funerals, no sympathy cards, no bereavement leave.
- **How it helps:** This model is transformative for knowledge workers dealing with organizational ambiguity—restructurings where your role 'might' be eliminated, leaders who are technically present but emotionally checked out, projects in permanent limbo. Use it to: (1) name why ambiguous professional situations feel disproportionately stressful, (2) understand why 'not knowing' is often worse than bad news, (3) advocate for clear communication in organizations (even painful clarity beats comfortable ambiguity), (4) recognize the unique toll of caring for someone with cognitive decline while still working full-time, (5) give language to the grief of estrangement or ghosting in professional relationships.
- **When to use:** When a loss lacks clear boundaries—a relationship that faded without ending, a role that's being slowly hollowed out, a leader who's physically present but disengaged. When you can't access traditional grief support because the loss isn't recognized by others. When organizational ambiguity about the future is causing disproportionate team distress.
- **Unique value:** Names a universal but previously unrecognized form of suffering. Explains why ambiguous situations produce more distress than clear losses. Uniquely bridges personal grief (dementia, estrangement) with organizational dysfunction (zombie projects, checked-out leaders).
- **Limits:** Therapeutic interventions for ambiguous loss are less developed than for bereavement. The model describes the phenomenon better than it prescribes solutions. 'Building tolerance of ambiguity' is the recommended coping strategy, but this can feel dismissive to those in acute distress.
- **Foundations:** Founded on Pauline Boss's family stress theory and her clinical research on ambiguous loss. Draws on systems family therapy, boundary ambiguity theory, and sociological concepts of liminal status applied to relationships where loss lacks social acknowledgment or clarity.

### Core Competency · #43 · `llm-high` · business · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Coined by C.K. Prahalad and Gary Hamel, a core competency is a deeply embedded organizational capability that provides access to multiple markets, contributes significantly to customer value, and is difficult for competitors to imitate. The strategic implication: companies should build strategy around their core competencies rather than around products or markets. The common mistake is confusing things you do frequently with things you do uniquely well—a true core competency passes the test of being valuable, rare, and hard to replicate.
- **How it helps:** Directly applicable to building, launching, and scaling products and businesses. Helps prioritize actions, identify market opportunities, and build sustainable competitive advantages.
- **When to use:** When building or iterating on products; when evaluating market opportunities; when deciding resource allocation; when scaling operations.
- **Unique value:** Directly actionable for product and business decisions. 'Core Competency' provides a specific framework that has been validated in real-world startup and enterprise contexts.
- **Limits:** Business models are context-dependent—what worked in one market may fail in another. Survivorship bias colors our understanding. Execution matters as much as strategy.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in Prahalad and Hamel's resource-based view of the firm (1990). Draws on Penrose's theory of firm resources, dynamic capabilities theory (Teece), and strategic management. Informed by Barney's VRIN framework. Foundation for inside-out strategy formulations and make-vs.-buy decisions in corporate strategy.

### Career Optionality · #1792 · `high` · career · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Career optionality applies real options theory from finance to career decisions. An 'option' is a future possibility you can exercise but aren't obligated to. High-optionality moves include: joining fast-growing companies, building transferable skills, maintaining broad networks, and keeping financial reserves. Low-optionality moves include: hyper-specialization, geographic immobility, and golden handcuffs. In uncertain environments, maintaining options often exceeds the value of committing to a single path. However, optionality has diminishing returns—at some point, commitment creates more value than keeping options open.
- **How it helps:** This adds a dimension most career analyses miss: not just 'is this good?' but 'does it increase or decrease future options?' When evaluating offers, consider: does this give access to multiple future paths? Practically: (1) early career, favor optionality—breadth, diverse network, financial reserves, (2) as clarity increases, selectively close options by committing deeply, (3) always maintain minimum optionality (runway, transferable skills, external relationships) as insurance. Prevents premature over-specialization and golden-handcuffs traps.
- **When to use:** When evaluating career opportunities under uncertainty, when deciding how specialized to become, when feeling the pull of a high-paying but narrow role, or when advising early-career professionals on strategic career design.
- **Unique value:** Imports the rigorous 'real options' framework from finance into career strategy, providing quantitative intuition for the value of keeping doors open. Uniquely balances the tension between optionality and commitment.
- **Limits:** Excessive optionality becomes paralysis—perpetually keeping doors open prevents depth. Can rationalize indecision as 'strategy.' Career options are hard to price like financial ones. Some of the best outcomes come from irreversible commitments.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in real options theory from finance (Dixit & Pindyck), decision theory under uncertainty, and career capital research. Draws on strategic flexibility literature, satisficing theory (Simon), and optionality concepts from derivatives pricing applied to career decision-making.

### Curiosity Instinct · #730 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The intrinsic human drive to seek novel information and experiences—a motivation as fundamental as hunger or fear. Curiosity is not a personality trait that some people have and others don't; it's a biological drive that can be activated or suppressed by environmental conditions. Berlyne's research identified two types: diversive curiosity (seeking novelty and stimulation—the drive to explore) and specific curiosity (seeking answers to particular questions—the drive to understand). Both types are essential for learning and innovation, and both can be systematically cultivated or systematically killed by organizational and educational environments.
- **How it helps:** Design environments that activate rather than suppress curiosity. Curiosity killers: punishment for asking questions, rigid processes that don't allow exploration, time pressure that eliminates reflection, and evaluation systems that reward knowing over discovering. Curiosity activators: information gaps (presenting partial information that creates the urge to complete), autonomy (freedom to explore), psychological safety (asking 'stupid' questions is welcomed), and exposure to novelty. In your own practice: follow threads that interest you even when they seem tangential—curiosity is the brain's navigation system for finding important unknowns.
- **When to use:** When organizational innovation has stalled despite talented people; when learning environments are producing compliance but not genuine understanding; when personal motivation is driven entirely by external rewards rather than intrinsic interest; when designing environments, products, or experiences that need to engage sustained attention.
- **Unique value:** Reframes curiosity from a personality trait (some people are curious, others aren't) to a biological drive (everyone has it, environments activate or suppress it). This shifts the intervention from 'hire curious people' to 'create conditions where curiosity thrives'—a much more actionable approach.
- **Limits:** Unconstrained curiosity can produce distraction and lack of focus. Some tasks require disciplined execution, not exploration. Diversive curiosity without specific curiosity produces shallow breadth. Organizational constraints on curiosity sometimes reflect real resource limitations, not cultural failure.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in evolutionary psychology's information-foraging theory and optimal arousal/information-gap theory (Loewenstein, 1994). Connected to intrinsic motivation research (Deci and Ryan's self-determination theory), neuroscience of dopaminergic novelty-seeking, and developmental psychology.

### Language Instinct · #780 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Steven Pinker's argument that language is not a cultural invention but a biological adaptation—an instinct as natural to humans as web-spinning is to spiders. Children don't learn language by memorizing rules; they acquire it through an innate biological capacity that unfolds with minimal input. As a mental model: some human capabilities are not cultural products that need to be taught but biological endowments that need only to be triggered. The distinction matters because interventions for instinct-based capabilities (provide triggers) differ fundamentally from interventions for culturally-learned capabilities (provide instruction).
- **How it helps:** Distinguish between capabilities that are instinctual (language, social bonding, pattern recognition, basic numeracy) and those that are culturally learned (reading, mathematics, scientific reasoning, organizational skills). For instinct-based capabilities: create the triggering environment and get out of the way. For culturally-learned capabilities: provide explicit instruction, practice, and scaffolding. Most educational and organizational failures come from treating culturally-learned skills as if they were instinctual ('people should just know how to give feedback') or treating instinctual capabilities as if they need heavy instruction.
- **When to use:** When designing training programs and need to distinguish what needs teaching vs. what needs triggering; when expectations about 'natural' abilities are miscalibrated; when understanding why some skills develop effortlessly and others require deliberate practice; when evaluating claims about 'natural' human capabilities.
- **Unique value:** Provides the biological framework for distinguishing between capabilities humans are born to develop (with minimal input) and capabilities that require cultural transmission and deliberate practice. This distinction is the foundation of effective education and training design.
- **Limits:** The boundary between instinct and culture is debated—many capabilities are a mix of both. Pinker's specific claims about the 'language instinct' are contested by some linguists. Individual variation in instinctual capabilities is significant. Cultural context shapes even instinctual capabilities significantly.
- **Foundations:** Introduced by Steven Pinker (1994) drawing on Chomsky's generative grammar and universal grammar hypothesis. Rooted in evolutionary psychology, cognitive linguistics, and nativist language acquisition theory. Connected to Whorfian linguistic relativity debates and cognitive science of language.

### Locus of Control · #783 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Julian Rotter's concept measuring whether individuals believe outcomes are determined by their own actions (internal locus) or by external forces like luck, fate, or powerful others (external locus). Internal locus correlates with greater initiative, resilience, and achievement—people who believe they can influence outcomes are more likely to try. External locus correlates with learned helplessness and lower effort—why try if outcomes are determined externally? The key insight: locus of control is partly accurate perception and partly self-fulfilling prophecy. People with internal locus take more action, which gives them more influence over outcomes, which reinforces their internal locus.
- **How it helps:** Assess your own locus of control and deliberately shift it internal where accurate. For each challenge, ask: what CAN I influence? Even in situations with significant external constraints, there are almost always aspects within your control. The practice: focus attention and energy on controllable factors and accept what you can't control without expending emotional energy on it (Stoic dichotomy of control). For teams: cultivate internal locus by highlighting how team actions led to outcomes (both good and bad) rather than attributing results to luck or external forces.
- **When to use:** When learned helplessness is producing inaction ('there's nothing we can do'); when team morale suffers from external attribution of failures; when personal agency needs to be developed or recovered; when evaluating whether responses to setbacks are productive (internal locus) or defeatist (external locus).
- **Unique value:** Identifies the specific belief that mediates between circumstances and response: whether you believe you can influence the outcome. This belief, independent of actual capability, dramatically affects effort and therefore results. The self-fulfilling nature makes locus of control both a diagnostic (how does this person respond to adversity?) and an intervention point (shift the belief, shift the effort, shift the outcome).
- **Limits:** External locus is sometimes accurate—some outcomes really ARE beyond individual control. Excessive internal locus produces self-blame for genuinely external events. The construct is continuous, not binary. Cultural differences affect appropriate locus orientation. Telling people in genuinely constrained situations to 'take control' can be dismissive.
- **Foundations:** Developed by Julian Rotter (1966) in social learning theory. Connected to attribution theory (Weiner), self-efficacy theory (Bandura), learned helplessness research, and organizational behavior on employee motivation, resilience, and performance management.
- *Secondary roles:* STRUCTURE · *Collection:* personality-framework

### Self-Knowledge · #1223 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The meta-cognitive capacity to accurately perceive one's own mental states, abilities, patterns, motivations, and blind spots. Self-knowledge is distinct from self-narrative (the story you tell about yourself, which may be inaccurate) and self-image (how you want to see yourself, which may be aspirational rather than actual). Genuine self-knowledge requires confronting the gap between who you THINK you are and how you ACTUALLY behave. As a mental model: self-knowledge is the foundation of all personal development because you can't improve what you can't accurately perceive—and the primary obstacle to self-knowledge is the ego's investment in maintaining a flattering self-image.
- **How it helps:** Build self-knowledge through external feedback and behavioral evidence rather than introspection alone (which is systematically biased). The practices: (1) Track your ACTUAL behavior (decisions, time use, emotional reactions) rather than your INTENDED behavior. (2) Seek candid feedback from people who observe you in different contexts. (3) Notice the gap between what you SAY you value and what your behavior REVEALS you value. (4) Use assessments calibrated against others (personality instruments, 360 reviews, skill tests) rather than self-assessment alone. For entrepreneurs: the most dangerous self-knowledge gaps are overestimating your strengths and underestimating your weaknesses.
- **When to use:** When accurate self-perception would improve decision-making and development; when the gap between self-image and actual behavior needs confronting; when external feedback would correct introspective bias; when personal development is stalled because the real growth area hasn't been accurately identified.
- **Unique value:** Identifies self-knowledge as the FOUNDATION that all personal development depends on—while revealing that the primary obstacle to self-knowledge is the ego's systematic distortion of self-perception. This creates the paradox: the people who most need self-knowledge are those whose ego most resists it.
- **Limits:** Complete self-knowledge may be impossible. Excessive self-focus can be counterproductive. Some self-deception is adaptive. External feedback can be biased or unhelpful. Cultural norms shape what self-knowledge is valued.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in ancient philosophy (Socratic self-examination, Delphic 'know thyself'), psychoanalytic introspection (Freud, Jung), and positive psychology (strengths research, Seligman). Draws on metacognition research (Flavell), Johari window in interpersonal psychology, and narrative identity theory (McAdams).

### Sexual Self-Schema · #1523 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Sexual self-schema theory, developed by Barbara Andersen and Jill Cyranowski, describes the cognitive generalizations about sexual aspects of the self that are derived from past experience and that guide the processing of sexually relevant information. Individuals with positive sexual self-schemas view themselves as passionate, open to sexual experience, and romantically inclined; they tend toward more sexual partners, more diverse sexual behaviors, greater sexual arousability, and higher sexual satisfaction. Those with negative sexual self-schemas view themselves as sexually conservative, inhibited, or undesirable; they experience more sexual avoidance, lower arousal, and less sexual satisfaction.
- **How it helps:** For knowledge workers who think systematically about self-concept, sexual self-schema provides a cognitive framework for understanding sexual satisfaction (or dissatisfaction) beyond behavior. Use it to: (1) identify your sexual self-schema and assess whether it serves your current relationship and values, (2) recognize that sexual self-schema formed in earlier life contexts (religious upbringing, past relationships, early experiences) may not fit your current context, (3) deliberately work to update sexual self-schemas that are producing shame, inhibition, or dissatisfaction, (4) understand that body image concerns function through sexual self-schema—negative body schema suppresses.
- **When to use:** When sexual satisfaction is lower than behavior alone would predict—the schema may be negative. When past experiences (religious background, negative sexual experiences, body image issues) are interfering with current sexual well-being. When cognitive patterns about sexuality are more restrictive than actual values or desires.
- **Unique value:** Provides a cognitive-behavioral framework for sexual self-concept that enables systematic intervention. Explains the disconnect between sexual behavior and sexual satisfaction through schema theory. Identifies specific mechanisms (attentional bias, arousal accessibility) through which self-schema affects sexual experience.
- **Limits:** Schema change is difficult and typically requires therapeutic support. The positive/negative framework may oversimplify—schemas can be complex and domain-specific. Cultural and religious values may intentionally constrain sexual self-schema in ways that the model frames as negative. The research base is primarily from Western, heterosexual populations.
- **Foundations:** Draws on Andersen and Cyranowski's sexual self-schema research in cognitive social psychology. Integrates Bem's gender schema theory, self-concept psychology, and clinical sexology on how cognitive templates about one's sexuality shape attention, arousal, and relational behavior.

### Holding Environment (Winnicott) · #2040 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Donald Winnicott's concept of the holding environment describes the conditions a caregiver creates that allow a dependent person to develop safely — physically (literal holding, feeding, temperature regulation) and psychologically (emotional availability, containment of distress, predictable responsiveness). The 'good enough mother' doesn't respond perfectly to every need — she responds adequately most of the time, and the gaps between need and response are small enough for the infant to tolerate, building frustration tolerance and self-regulation capacity. Winnicott recognized that the holding environment applies beyond infancy: therapists hold clients' distress, managers hold teams' anxiety during uncertainty, and organizations hold employees through disruptive change.

### Deadweight Loss · #182 · `llm-high` · economics · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The economic inefficiency that occurs when a market doesn't produce the optimal quantity of a good or service—value that COULD have been created but ISN'T because of some market distortion. Taxes, price floors, price ceilings, monopoly pricing, and regulations all can create deadweight loss by preventing transactions that would benefit both buyer and seller. A $5 tax on widgets means some people who'd buy at $10 and sellers who'd sell at $8 don't transact—even though both would benefit. That uncaptured mutual benefit is deadweight loss. As a mental model: any friction, barrier, or distortion that prevents mutually beneficial exchanges creates invisible value destruction.
- **How it helps:** Identify where deadweight loss is occurring in your systems. In organizations: bureaucratic approval processes that prevent beneficial internal transactions (teams that could help each other but don't because of process friction). In markets: pricing structures that exclude willing buyers. In personal productivity: overhead (reporting, meetings, compliance) that prevents value-creating work. The intervention: reduce the friction, barrier, or distortion that's preventing the mutually beneficial exchange. Every reduction in deadweight loss creates value that both parties capture.
- **When to use:** When bureaucratic friction is preventing beneficial internal collaboration; when pricing excludes willing customers; when regulatory compliance costs exceed regulatory benefits; when any system is preventing value-creating exchanges through structural barriers.
- **Unique value:** Identifies the INVISIBLE cost of market distortions: not higher prices (which are visible transfers) but UNCREATED value from transactions that never happen. Deadweight loss is invisible because it represents what WOULD HAVE occurred but didn't—making it the most systematically underestimated cost of any market intervention.
- **Limits:** Some deadweight loss is acceptable to achieve other goals (taxes fund public goods, regulations prevent harm). Calculating deadweight loss precisely requires supply and demand curves that are rarely known exactly. Not all market interventions produce net negative outcomes. Zero friction isn't always desirable. The model assumes markets would be efficient without intervention, which isn't always true.
- **Foundations:** Foundational in welfare economics and price theory (Marshall, Pigou). Connects to Pareto efficiency analysis, public finance theory, monopoly welfare analysis, and cost-benefit analysis frameworks in regulatory economics.

### Efficient-Market Hypothesis · #188 · `llm-high` · economics · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Eugene Fama's theory that asset prices fully reflect all available information—making it impossible to consistently 'beat the market' through stock selection or market timing. Three forms: weak (prices reflect all past price data—technical analysis can't work), semi-strong (prices reflect all public information—fundamental analysis can't work), and strong (prices reflect ALL information including insider—nobody can beat the market). The practical implication: if markets are efficient, the best investment strategy is passive indexing rather than active management. The theoretical implication: market prices ARE fair prices at any moment. The empirical reality: markets are MOSTLY efficient, with exploitable inefficiencies that exist but are hard to capture.
- **How it helps:** Use the EMH as your DEFAULT assumption and require strong evidence before deviating. The practices: (1) Invest passively (index funds) unless you have a specific, articulable edge. (2) If you believe you can beat the market: specify your edge—what information do you have that the market doesn't? If you can't specify it, you probably don't have one. (3) Use EMH to evaluate claims: anyone claiming consistent market-beating returns should be scrutinized—most are experiencing luck, survivorship bias, or hidden risk. (4) Where EMH clearly fails (illiquid markets, small caps, complex securities): there may be genuine opportunities for those with specialized knowledge.
- **When to use:** When investment strategy needs a starting framework; when evaluating claims of market-beating ability; when understanding why most active managers underperform index funds; when identifying the specific conditions where markets may be inefficient enough to exploit.
- **Unique value:** Provides the most important question in investing: 'what do I know that the market doesn't?' If the answer is 'nothing'—index investing is optimal. The EMH doesn't claim markets are perfectly efficient; it claims they're efficient ENOUGH that most people can't consistently beat them. This single idea saves investors from the vast majority of poor active management strategies.
- **Limits:** Markets are NOT perfectly efficient—behavioral biases, information delays, and institutional constraints create real inefficiencies. The theory can't explain bubbles and crashes. Some investors DO beat the market consistently (Buffett, Renaissance Technologies). The EMH assumes rational actors, which behavioral economics challenges. Different market segments have different efficiency levels.
- **Foundations:** Developed by Eugene Fama (1970); rooted in financial economics and probability theory. Connects to random walk theory, information theory, rational expectations hypothesis, and behavioral finance critiques (Shiller, Thaler).

### Ergodicity · #189 · `llm-high` · economics · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The mathematical property that distinguishes systems where the time average equals the ensemble average (ergodic) from those where it doesn't (non-ergodic). In an ergodic system: what happens on average to 100 people also happens over time to one person. In a non-ergodic system: the average across 100 people is different from the experience of any single person. Russian roulette: the ensemble average (1 in 6 die) is irrelevant to the individual who plays repeatedly (certain death eventually). Ole Peters and Nassim Taleb argue that most real-world economic systems are NON-ERGODIC, making ensemble averages (expected value) misleading for individual decision-making.
- **How it helps:** Before accepting any 'expected value' calculation: ask 'is this system ergodic or non-ergodic for ME?' If non-ergodic (most life decisions with path-dependence, ruin risk, or compounding): the ensemble average is IRRELEVANT to your individual trajectory. A bet with positive expected value but a chance of total loss is not rational for a non-ergodic actor (you can't recover from ruin to 'average out'). The Kelly Criterion for bet sizing respects ergodicity: never bet enough to be wiped out, even if the expected value favors larger bets. In career: avoid paths where a single failure is unrecoverable, regardless of the 'average' outcome.
- **When to use:** When expected value calculations involve ruin risk; when path dependence means early outcomes constrain later ones; when the distinction between what happens 'on average' and what happens 'to me over time' matters; when Kelly Criterion or similar sizing approaches are needed to avoid ruin.
- **Unique value:** Identifies THE most important distinction in probability that most people miss: ensemble average ≠ time average for non-ergodic systems. Since most life decisions are non-ergodic (you can't replay from zero), expected value reasoning—the default in economics—is WRONG for individual decision-making. This single concept changes optimal strategy from maximizing expected value to maximizing the time average (which means avoiding ruin above all).
- **Limits:** The ergodic/non-ergodic distinction is clearer in mathematics than in real-world application. Some non-ergodic situations are approximately ergodic over relevant timescales. Excessive ruin-avoidance can prevent necessary risk-taking. The concept is technically demanding and easy to misapply.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in statistical physics and probability theory (Boltzmann). Extended to economics by Ole Peters; connects to expected utility theory, time-average vs. ensemble-average distinctions in stochastic processes, and Kelly criterion in decision theory.

### Price Elasticity · #217 · `llm-high` · economics · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The measure of how sensitive demand is to price changes—how much the quantity demanded changes when the price changes by 1%. Elastic demand (elasticity > 1): small price changes produce large demand changes (luxury goods, products with many substitutes). Inelastic demand (elasticity < 1): price changes produce small demand changes (necessities, addictive products, products with few substitutes). Price elasticity determines optimal pricing strategy: for elastic products, volume sensitivity dominates (lower prices increase revenue); for inelastic products, price insensitivity dominates (higher prices increase revenue). Elasticity is the single most important concept in pricing strategy.
- **How it helps:** Determine your product's price elasticity empirically—then set pricing strategy accordingly. For elastic products (many substitutes, discretionary purchase): compete on value, use loss leaders, be cautious with price increases. For inelastic products (few substitutes, essential, high switching costs): price to capture value, increase prices gradually, invest in maintaining the conditions that produce inelasticity (switching costs, brand loyalty, essentiality). The most valuable strategic move: make your product MORE inelastic by increasing switching costs, reducing substitutes, and deepening integration.
- **When to use:** When pricing strategy needs to account for demand sensitivity; when understanding why some price increases lose customers and others don't; when competitive strategy involves making demand less elastic; when evaluating market power and competitive position through the lens of price sensitivity.
- **Unique value:** Provides the single most important metric for pricing strategy: how much do customers respond to price changes? Every pricing decision—increases, discounts, bundling, versioning—should be guided by elasticity. Without knowing your elasticity, pricing strategy is guesswork.
- **Limits:** Elasticity varies across customer segments, time periods, and competitive conditions. Measuring elasticity precisely requires experimentation. The concept assumes smooth demand curves that may not exist. Elasticity can change rapidly with competitive entry or market shifts. The model is static while markets are dynamic.
- **Foundations:** Foundational in microeconomics and demand theory (Marshall). Connects to consumer theory, cross-price and income elasticity analysis, taxation incidence theory, and pricing strategy research in industrial organization.

### Purchasing Power Parity · #219 · `llm-high` · economics · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The economic theory that exchange rates between currencies should adjust so that identical goods cost the same in different countries when expressed in a common currency. A Big Mac costing $5 in the US should cost the equivalent of $5 in Japan when converted at the exchange rate. When PPP is violated: a currency is either overvalued (goods cost more than they should internationally) or undervalued (goods cost less). As a mental model: PPP provides the framework for understanding relative costs across contexts—including non-monetary comparisons. Your time is 'worth' different amounts in different activities, and the 'exchange rate' between activities should reflect their true relative value.
- **How it helps:** Apply PPP thinking when comparing values across different contexts. In business: international pricing should account for local purchasing power, not just exchange rates. For global analysis: compare countries using PPP-adjusted GDP rather than nominal GDP for more accurate welfare comparisons.
- **When to use:** When comparing costs or values across different markets, geographies, or contexts; when international business decisions need purchasing power context; when salary or cost-of-living comparisons need to account for price level differences; when resource allocation needs to account for different 'exchange rates' between activities.
- **Unique value:** Provides the framework for making meaningful comparisons across contexts with different price levels—revealing that nominal equality can mask real inequality (or vice versa). A dollar in rural India buys far more than a dollar in Manhattan, making nominal comparisons misleading without PPP adjustment.
- **Limits:** PPP rarely holds exactly in practice (transportation costs, tariffs, non-tradable goods). The theory applies best to tradable goods. PPP calculations depend on which goods are compared. Short-term exchange rates can deviate far from PPP. The concept is more useful for long-term comparisons than short-term.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in international economics and exchange rate theory (Cassel, 1918). Connects to law of one price, trade theory, monetary economics, and development economics' use of PPP adjustments for cross-country welfare comparisons.

- **What it is:** The number of patients who need to receive a treatment for one to benefit—the most intuitive measure of treatment effectiveness. NNT converts abstract statistics (relative risk reduction, p-values) into a concrete question: how many people must undergo this intervention for one to benefit?

This often reveals that impressive relative improvements ('50% reduction!') translate to disappointing absolute effects.
- **When to use:** When evaluating cost-effectiveness of any intervention applied at scale; when impressive-sounding relative statistics need conversion to absolute impact; when deciding between interventions with different efficacy profiles; when total cost of treating non-beneficiaries matters.
- **Unique value:** Converts statistical significance into practical significance. A treatment achieving p<0.001 but NNT of 500 is statistically significant but practically useless. NNT asks the question p-values don't: how much effort per unit of benefit?
- **Limits:** NNT doesn't capture benefit magnitude—NNT 10 for preventing death differs from NNT 10 for reducing headaches. NNT varies across populations. Very effective treatments for rare conditions can have misleadingly high NNTs. The model assumes binary outcomes.
- **Foundations:** Founded in clinical epidemiology (Laupacis, Sackett), evidence-based medicine, and decision theory. NNT translates abstract relative risk statistics into clinically intuitive denominators. Draws on Bayesian absolute risk framing and Gigerenzer's research on improving statistical literacy in medical decision-making.

### Mutual Information (Shared Knowledge Between Variables) · #296 · `llm-high` · mathematical · CLAIM
- **What it is:** An information theory measure of how much knowing one variable reduces uncertainty about another. High mutual information means the variables are predictably related; zero mutual information means they're completely independent. Unlike correlation (which measures linear relationships), mutual information captures any type of dependency—linear, nonlinear, or complex. In business: mutual information between customer behavior and purchase intent tells you how predictive your behavioral data actually is. If mutual information is low, your data isn't telling you what you think it is.
- **How it helps:** When evaluating predictive models, leading indicators, or data relationships, ask: how much does knowing X actually reduce uncertainty about Y? High correlation can mask low mutual information when the relationship is noisy. Conversely, variables with low linear correlation can have high mutual information if the relationship is nonlinear. The practical implication: before building predictive models on any variable, verify that it actually contains usable information about the target—not just statistical association.
- **When to use:** When evaluating whether data features are genuinely predictive; when building predictive models and need to select meaningful inputs; when assessing whether leading indicators actually lead; when determining whether two variables are truly independent or have hidden dependencies.
- **Unique value:** Provides a more rigorous measure of predictive value than correlation. In an era of big data, much apparent signal is actually noise with coincidental statistical association. Mutual information tells you whether one variable genuinely reduces uncertainty about another—the fundamental question in any prediction.
- **Limits:** Mutual information requires substantial data to estimate reliably, especially for continuous variables. It measures statistical dependency but not causation. High mutual information doesn't tell you the direction or nature of the relationship. Computing mutual information for high-dimensional data is technically challenging.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in Shannon's information theory and probability theory (joint distributions, conditional entropy). Draws on Bayesian statistics, signal processing, and statistical learning theory. Connected to Kullback-Leibler divergence, channel capacity theorems, and causal discovery methodology (Pearl).

### Blotto_ No Best Strategy · #379 · `llm-high` · mathematical · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The game-theoretic result that in Colonel Blotto games, there is NO single dominant strategy—the optimal allocation depends on your opponent's allocation, creating a strategic interdependence with no stable pure strategy solution. The best response to a spread allocation is concentration; the best response to concentration is spreading to win the undefended fronts. This produces mixed strategy equilibria: players must RANDOMIZE their allocations to be unpredictable. The practical implication: in multi-front competition, predictability is a vulnerability—if your competitor knows where you'll allocate, they can always counter-allocate optimally.
- **How it helps:** In multi-front competition: maintain strategic unpredictability. If you always concentrate in the same areas, competitors will learn and counter. If you always spread evenly, competitors will concentrate to dominate specific fronts. The practice: vary your competitive emphasis across periods and dimensions. For business: don't ALWAYS compete on the same dimensions—occasionally shift competitive emphasis to prevent opponents from optimizing against your known strategy.
- **When to use:** When competitive strategy needs unpredictability to prevent opponent optimization; when understanding why predictable competitive patterns are exploitable; when multi-front competition requires mixed rather than pure strategies; when game-theoretic analysis reveals the absence of a dominant strategy.
- **Unique value:** Proves mathematically that PREDICTABILITY IS A VULNERABILITY in multi-front competition: any predictable allocation can be countered with a superior counter-allocation. This legitimizes strategic variety and unpredictability as deliberate competitive tactics, not indecision.
- **Limits:** Real competition allows learning and adaptation. Pure randomization is difficult for organizations. The mixed strategy equilibrium is theoretical. Organizational execution benefits from consistency. Strategic unpredictability has coordination costs.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in game theory's minimax theorem and mixed-strategy Nash equilibria. Draws on probability theory, information theory, and military operations research. The 'no best strategy' result—requiring mixed strategies—derives from von Neumann's minimax theorem (1928) and has applications in cybersecurity and sports.

### False Positive · #1349 · `llm-high` · mathematical · CLAIM
- **What it is:** A test result that incorrectly indicates the presence of a condition—saying 'yes' when the true answer is 'no.' In medicine, a false positive means diagnosing a disease that isn't there. In business: flagging a legitimate transaction as fraud, identifying a good employee as underperforming, or concluding a marketing campaign works when the result was random noise. False positives are the cost of sensitive detection systems—the more aggressively you screen, the more false alarms you generate.
- **How it helps:** Every screening system you build—fraud detection, lead scoring, hiring filters, anomaly alerts—produces false positives. The question is always: what's the cost of a false positive vs. a false negative? In fraud detection, a false positive means annoying a good customer; a false negative means losing money. In hiring, a false positive means a bad hire; a false negative means missing a great candidate. Calibrating this trade-off is the core design challenge.
- **When to use:** When designing or evaluating any detection, screening, or classification system; when interpreting test results—medical, statistical, or business; when false alarms are eroding trust in a monitoring system; when deciding how aggressively to filter or screen.
- **Unique value:** Quantifies the cost of vigilance. In a world that rewards 'catching problems early,' false positives are the hidden tax. Understanding this cost prevents over-screening and the trust erosion that comes from too many false alarms (the boy who cried wolf, systematized).
- **Limits:** False positive rates depend on base rates—even accurate tests produce many false positives when the condition is rare (see Bayes' theorem). Reducing false positives typically increases false negatives and vice versa. In some contexts, the cost of false positives is negligible compared to false negatives (e.g., cancer screening).
- **Foundations:** Rooted in hypothesis testing theory (Neyman-Pearson framework) and signal detection theory (Green & Swets). Draws on statistical decision theory, Bayesian inference, and the psychology of judgment under uncertainty. False positives are central to the Type I/Type II error tradeoff in frequentist statistics.

### False Negative · #1350 · `llm-high` · mathematical · CLAIM
- **What it is:** A test result that incorrectly indicates the absence of a condition—saying 'no' when the true answer is 'yes.' In medicine, a false negative means missing a disease that's actually present. In business: failing to detect actual fraud, missing that a high-potential employee is struggling, or not recognizing a genuine market shift because your metrics didn't capture it. False negatives are the cost of specific detection systems—the more you optimize for avoiding false alarms, the more real problems you miss.
- **How it helps:** The hidden cost of conservative decision-making. Every time you raise the bar to reduce false positives (false alarms), you increase false negatives (missed realities). In hiring: raising interview standards reduces bad hires but increases missed talent. In product: conservative feature gates reduce bugs but delay valuable features. The question is always: which error is more expensive? Usually, the cost of missing something real is greater than the cost of a false alarm.

While false positives are annoying and visible, false negatives are invisible by definition: you don't see what you didn't detect. This asymmetry means false negatives are systematically underweighted in system design.
- **Limits:** Reducing false negatives typically increases false positives, and the optimal balance depends entirely on the relative costs. In some domains (spam filtering), more false negatives are acceptable; in others (medical screening), they're catastrophic. The base rate of the condition dramatically affects the practical impact of false negative rates.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in Neyman-Pearson hypothesis testing and signal detection theory. Draws on statistical power analysis, Bayesian decision theory, and medical screening literature. False negatives (Type II errors) are governed by statistical power (1-β) and are central to research design, diagnostic testing, and quality control.

### Cheap Talk (Costless Communication) · #999 · `llm-high` · strategy · CLAIM
- **What it is:** In game theory, communication that costs nothing to send and therefore can't be trusted as a credible signal—because lying has no penalty. 'I'll definitely refer business to you' costs nothing to say and therefore conveys no information about actual intent. In contrast, costly signals (investing time, money, or reputation) are credible because faking them has consequences. Cheap talk is ubiquitous in business: competitor threats, partnership promises, stated intentions, and customer commitments are all cheap talk until backed by costly action. The model explains why verbal agreements are weak and signed contracts are strong.
- **How it helps:** Evaluate all communication by its cost structure: is this person's statement backed by any costly commitment, or is it free to say? When someone says 'we should partner,' that's cheap talk. When they sign a letter of intent with a penalty clause, that's a costly signal. In your own communication: if you want to be believed, accompany words with costly actions. In negotiations: discount verbal commitments and focus on structural commitments (deposits, contracts, public announcements) that would be costly to renege on. The credibility of any statement is proportional to the cost of it being false.
- **When to use:** When evaluating the credibility of promises, threats, or stated intentions; when designing agreements that need to be enforceable; when distinguishing genuine commitment from performative enthusiasm; when analyzing whether competitive threats are credible.
- **Unique value:** Provides a precise framework for evaluating credibility: cost of lying determines credibility of the statement. - **Limits:** Some cheap talk IS informative—people often tell the truth even without penalties for lying. Reputation serves as an implicit cost to lying, even for 'cheap' statements. Draws on signaling theory (Spence), mechanism design (Hurwicz, Maskin, Myerson), and strategic communication research. Connects to credibility theory in monetary economics, negotiation research, and political science on legislative communication.

### Evolutionary Stable Strategy (ESS) · #1002 · `llm-high` · strategy · CLAIM
- **What it is:** John Maynard Smith's game theory concept: a strategy that, once adopted by a population, cannot be invaded by any alternative strategy—because any deviant strategy does worse against the established one. Hawks and doves: a population of all hawks fights too much (costly); a population of all doves is invadable by one hawk; the ESS is a specific RATIO of hawks and doves where neither can profitably increase. Applied to business: market equilibria where no firm can profitably deviate from the prevailing strategy. Cultural norms that are self-reinforcing because deviating from them is individually costly.
- **How it helps:** For any competitive environment: identify whether the current strategy mix is an ESS. If it is: deviation from the prevailing strategy will be punished (the market/culture will push you back). If it's not: the current mix is unstable and will shift toward an ESS. For strategy: you can either play the ESS (compete within the established rules) or try to shift the game to a new ESS where your strengths are better rewarded. The latter is much harder but produces lasting competitive advantage if successful. Disruption = establishing a new ESS.
- **When to use:** When competitive strategies have converged and deviation seems unprofitable; when understanding why markets reach stable configurations; when evaluating whether disruptive strategies can overcome the stability of current equilibria; when designing strategies that create new competitive equilibria.
- **Unique value:** Explains why competitive environments are stable: the ESS is self-reinforcing because any deviation is individually costly. This provides the formal framework for understanding why industries resist disruption (the current ESS punishes deviants) and what disruption actually requires (shifting to a new game with a different ESS).
- **Limits:** Real competitive environments are more complex than the models—multiple strategies can coexist. ESS analysis requires well-defined payoffs that real markets rarely have. The environment changes, shifting which strategies are evolutionarily stable. Finding a new ESS is much easier to describe than to accomplish.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in evolutionary biology (Maynard Smith and Price, 1973) and game theory. Draws on population dynamics, replicator dynamics in evolutionary game theory, and Nash equilibrium theory. Connects to behavioral economics on norms, cultural evolution research, and institutional economics on stable conventions.

### BATNA (Best Alternative to Negotiated Agreement) · #1034 · `llm-high` · strategy · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Roger Fisher and William Ury's concept from 'Getting to Yes': your best option if the current negotiation fails. - **When to use:** When facing strategic decisions under uncertainty; when multiple stakeholders have competing interests; when the stakes are high and reversibility is low.

- **Limits:** Strategic models assume rationality that real actors may not exhibit. Competitive situations are unique—historical patterns may not repeat. Over-analysis can paralyze action.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in negotiation theory and game theory (Fisher, Ury, and Patton's 'Getting to Yes', 1981; Nash bargaining solution). Draws on decision theory on outside options, game theory on credible threats, and behavioral economics on anchoring. ### Trade-offs · #1078 · `llm-high` · strategy · CLAIM

### Preserving Optionality · #1211 · `high` · strategy · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The strategic principle of maintaining FUTURE CHOICES by avoiding premature commitment—keeping options open when uncertainty is high and the cost of reversing decisions is significant. Under uncertainty: the value of an option increases because it preserves the ability to choose later with more information. Irreversible decisions made under high uncertainty sacrifice the option value of waiting. The option-value framework: every decision that closes a door has an OPTION COST equal to the value of the information you'd gain by waiting. Nassim Taleb, financial options theory, and real options analysis all formalize this: optionality is valuable precisely BECAUSE the future is uncertain.
- **How it helps:** When facing uncertain decisions: assess the option value of waiting versus the cost of delay. High uncertainty + high irreversibility = preserve optionality (wait, experiment, keep doors open). Low uncertainty + easily reversible = commit and execute (optionality has low value). The practices: (1) Prefer reversible decisions over irreversible ones under uncertainty. (2) Invest in experiments that generate information before committing. (3) Design decisions as stages rather than single commitments. (4) Recognize that premature commitment under uncertainty is COSTLY, not decisive. For entrepreneurs: stage investments, validate before scaling, preserve the ability to pivot.
- **When to use:** When uncertainty is high and decisions are difficult to reverse; when the value of waiting for information exceeds the cost of delay; when commitment should be staged rather than absolute; when the option value of maintaining flexibility needs explicit valuation.
- **Unique value:** Provides the formal framework for valuing FLEXIBILITY: optionality has quantifiable value that increases with uncertainty and irreversibility. This transforms 'keeping options open' from vague advice into CALCULABLE strategy—and reveals when commitment IS the right choice (low uncertainty, easily reversible).
- **Limits:** Preserving optionality can become analysis paralysis. Some situations reward bold early commitment. The cost of delay may exceed the option value. Optionality has carrying costs. Excessive option preservation prevents the learning that comes from commitment.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in financial options theory (Black-Scholes, Merton), real options analysis (Dixit & Pindyck), and decision theory under uncertainty. Draws on flexibility valuation in engineering economics, portfolio theory (Markowitz), and the economics of information with irreversible decisions (Arrow).
- *Secondary roles:* — · *Collection:* mental-model-canon

### Self-Confidence · #1269 · `llm-high` · strategy · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The strategic and psychological resource of CALIBRATED belief in one's own ability to execute, learn, and adapt—distinct from both arrogance (overconfidence disconnected from evidence) and self-doubt (underconfidence disconnected from track record). True self-confidence is EVIDENCE-BASED: grounded in past performance, demonstrated learning ability, and honest assessment of both strengths and gaps. As a mental model: self-confidence is not a feeling but a CALIBRATION—the degree to which your belief in your abilities matches your actual abilities. Overconfidence leads to reckless commitment; underconfidence leads to missed opportunities. Calibrated confidence optimizes risk-taking.
- **How it helps:** Build calibrated self-confidence through systematic evidence collection. The practices: (1) Maintain a track record of predictions and outcomes to calibrate your confidence. (2) Distinguish between DOMAIN confidence (confident in areas with demonstrated competence) and GENERAL confidence (confident regardless of domain—dangerous). (3) Use past performance as the base rate for future confidence. (4) Separate confidence in your ABILITY TO LEARN from confidence in your CURRENT KNOWLEDGE—the former is more robust. For entrepreneurs: confidence should be highest where you have the deepest track record and most honest feedback loops.
- **When to use:** When confidence calibration would improve decision quality; when overconfidence or underconfidence is producing suboptimal risk-taking; when domain-specific confidence needs separation from general confidence; when evidence-based self-assessment would replace both arrogance and self-doubt.
- **Unique value:** Reframes self-confidence from a TRAIT (you have it or you don't) to a CALIBRATION (how well does your belief match your ability?). This makes confidence improvable through better evidence collection and calibration rather than through motivational pep talks.
- **Limits:** Perfect calibration is impossible. Some situations benefit from slight overconfidence. Confidence affects performance (self-fulfilling prophecy). Domain expertise doesn't always transfer. Calibration itself requires metacognitive skill.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in social psychology of self-efficacy (Bandura, 1977), positive psychology (Seligman), and clinical psychology on self-esteem (Rosenberg). Draws on attribution theory, performance psychology, and courage research (Rachman). Related to identity economics and the distinction between self-esteem and self-efficacy.

### Leverage (Negotiation) · #1317 · `llm-high` · strategy · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The relative power each party has in a negotiation, determined primarily by four factors: (1) alternatives (BATNA—your best option if this deal falls through), (2) information asymmetry (knowing something the other side doesn't), (3) time pressure differentials (who needs a deal sooner), and (4) relative cost of no deal (who is hurt more by walking away). Leverage is more important than negotiation tactics—a strong position with poor tactics beats poor position with strong tactics every time.
- **How it helps:** Before any negotiation, assess leverage on all four dimensions. Your BATNA is the foundation: the better your alternative, the more leverage you have—because you can genuinely walk away. Information is the second lever: understanding the other party's constraints, timeline, and alternatives gives you an advantage. The practical discipline: never enter a negotiation without knowing your BATNA and having taken steps to improve it. The best negotiation preparation is often improving your alternatives, not rehearsing tactics.
- **When to use:** Before any negotiation—business deals, salary discussions, vendor contracts, partnerships; when assessing whether to negotiate or accept terms; when a negotiation feels stuck—analyzing leverage often reveals why; when deciding whether to invest in improving your position vs. accepting the current terms.
- **Unique value:** Shifts focus from negotiation techniques to negotiation structure. Most negotiation advice focuses on what to say; leverage analysis focuses on what's true about your position. This is more actionable because you can change your leverage (by developing alternatives, gathering information, managing timelines) before the negotiation even starts.
- **Limits:** Leverage is dynamic—it changes as negotiations progress and new information emerges. Over-leveraging a position can damage long-term relationships. Leverage analysis assumes rational actors; emotional decisions can override leverage calculations. In ongoing relationships, wielding leverage aggressively creates resentment that extracts a later cost.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in negotiation theory (Fisher & Ury's principled negotiation, Raiffa's analytical framework) and Schelling's bargaining theory. Draws on power asymmetry analysis from political science and the BATNA (Best Alternative to Negotiated Agreement) concept from Harvard Negotiation Project.

### Adjacent Possible (Stuart Kauffman) · #1083 · `llm-high` · systems · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Stuart Kauffman's concept (popularized by Steven Johnson) that innovation occurs at the boundary of what's currently possible—the set of all things that are ONE step away from existing combinations. You can't invent the microwave oven in the 1500s because too many prerequisite technologies don't exist yet. But once magnetrons, electrical grids, and consumer appliance manufacturing exist, the microwave becomes part of the adjacent possible—and multiple people will independently discover it. Innovation isn't random; it expands outward from what exists, and the adjacent possible defines WHAT can be invented next.
- **How it helps:** Map your adjacent possible: given your current capabilities, knowledge, relationships, and assets, what innovations are now ONE combinatorial step away? These are your highest-probability innovations. For entrepreneurs: don't try to skip five steps ahead—identify what's adjacent to existing capabilities and markets. For learning: the adjacent possible for your knowledge means learning is most effective when it builds incrementally from what you already know. For product development: what can you build by recombining what you already have in novel ways? The adjacent possible expands as you build—each innovation creates new adjacencies.
- **When to use:** When innovation strategy needs to balance ambition with feasibility; when evaluating whether an idea is ahead of its time (not yet in the adjacent possible) or timely (newly adjacent); when planning learning paths that build incrementally; when understanding why some innovations emerge in clusters.
- **Unique value:** Provides the specific mechanism explaining WHY innovation is incremental and WHY simultaneous invention occurs: the adjacent possible defines a landscape of feasible innovations that multiple people can independently discover. This converts innovation from mysterious inspiration to systematic expansion from existing capabilities.
- **Limits:** The adjacent possible is easier to identify in retrospect than in prospect. Some innovations DO skip multiple steps through genuine breakthroughs. The concept can justify overly conservative innovation strategies. In rapidly changing environments, the adjacent possible shifts faster than it can be mapped. Not all combinatorial possibilities are equally valuable.
- **Foundations:** Developed by theoretical biologist Stuart Kauffman from complexity science and origin-of-life research. Draws on evolutionary biology, thermodynamics, and systems theory. Related to Prigogine's dissipative structures, fitness landscape theory, and innovation economics. Philosophically connected to emergence and combinatorial creativity.

### Attractor States · #1085 · `llm-high` · systems · CLAIM
- **What it is:** In dynamical systems theory, states toward which a system naturally evolves and to which it returns after perturbation—like a marble rolling to the bottom of a bowl. Organizational cultures have attractor states: after disruption, they tend to return to their pre-disruption patterns. Personal habits have attractor states: after attempted change, behavior tends to revert to established patterns. Markets have attractor states: after bubbles and crashes, they tend toward long-run equilibria. Attractor states explain why change is so difficult: the system has a 'gravitational pull' toward its established state that resists perturbation.
- **How it helps:** When attempting change, identify and address the attractor state—not just the current behavior. Change that doesn't alter the underlying attractor will be temporary: the system will revert once the change force is removed. To create lasting change: reshape the attractor basin by changing the structural conditions (incentives, environment, relationships, identity) that make the current state stable. In organizational change: the attractor is the culture, and temporary programs that don't alter the cultural attractor produce temporary changes. In personal change: the attractor is the identity and environment, and behavioral changes that don't alter these foundations eventually revert.
- **When to use:** When change efforts produce temporary results that don't stick; when systems keep reverting to previous states despite repeated intervention; when understanding why some changes are stable and others aren't; when designing change interventions that need to be permanent rather than temporary.
- **Unique value:** Explains WHY change efforts fail with a precision that 'resistance to change' doesn't: the system has a structural attractor that pulls it back to the previous state. The intervention isn't 'push harder' (which produces temporary displacement that reverts) but 'reshape the basin' (which changes what state the system naturally evolves toward).
- **Limits:** Identifying attractor states in social systems is more metaphorical than mathematical. Not all system states are attractors—some are genuinely unstable. The model can justify fatalism ('the system always reverts'). Reshaping attractor basins is described but not easily operationalized. Some systems have multiple attractors, making the dynamics complex.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in dynamical systems theory and nonlinear mathematics (Lorenz, Poincaré). Draws on chaos theory, topology, and differential equations. Applied across physics, ecology, neuroscience (neural attractors), and social systems theory. Related to Prigogine's dissipative structures and complexity science's self-organization.
- *Secondary roles:* MECHANISM · *Collection:* complexity-concept

### Network Centrality (Betweenness, Closeness, Degree) · #1095 · `llm-high` · systems · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Three complementary measures of a node's importance in a network. Degree centrality: how many direct connections you have (the 'connector'). Closeness centrality: how few steps to reach anyone in the network (the 'accessible' person). Betweenness centrality: how many shortest paths between other nodes pass through you (the 'broker'—Burt's structural holes). Each measure captures a different type of influence: degree measures popularity, closeness measures reach, and betweenness measures control over information flow. A person can be high on one and low on others.
- **How it helps:** Map your network position on all three dimensions. High degree (many connections) doesn't guarantee high betweenness (control of information flow)—you might be deeply connected within one cluster but not bridging between clusters. For maximum strategic influence, optimize betweenness: position yourself between groups that don't otherwise connect. For maximum reach, optimize closeness: reduce the number of hops to anyone in the network. For maximum social capital, optimize degree: build many direct relationships. Different goals require different network strategies.
- **When to use:** When analyzing your own network position and strategic influence; when designing organizational communication structures; when identifying key people in a network (influencers, connectors, brokers); when network analysis would reveal hidden power structures or communication bottlenecks.
- **Unique value:** Provides three distinct, measurable definitions of 'importance' in a network, revealing that influence isn't one thing—it's at least three. A person who seems unimportant by one measure may be critical by another. This prevents the common error of equating network importance with popularity (degree) while missing brokerage (betweenness) and reach (closeness).
- **Limits:** Network centrality measures are static snapshots of dynamic relationships. Measuring centrality requires network data that's often unavailable or incomplete. High centrality in a network of low-value contacts is less useful than moderate centrality in a high-value network. The measures assume all connections are equal, which is rarely true.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in mathematical graph theory (Euler, Moreno's sociometry) and structural sociology (Burt, Freeman). Draws on social network analysis, information theory, and computational network science. Related to Granovetter's strength of weak ties, small-world research, and power/influence studies in organizations.

### Network Clustering Coefficient · #1096 · `llm-high` · systems · CLAIM
- **What it is:** A measure of how interconnected a node's neighbors are with each other—the probability that two of your contacts know each other. High clustering means your network is a tight-knit group where everyone knows everyone (strong bonds, high trust, but limited new information). Low clustering means your contacts don't know each other—you're the bridge between separate worlds (weaker bonds but access to diverse, non-redundant information). Granovetter's 'strength of weak ties' and Burt's 'structural holes' both describe the value of LOW clustering—bridging positions between dense clusters.
- **How it helps:** Assess your network's clustering coefficient intuitively: do your contacts mostly know each other (high clustering) or are they from different, unconnected worlds (low clustering)? For maximum information diversity and brokerage opportunity: deliberately maintain connections across multiple unconnected clusters. The strategic balance: enough high-clustering connections for trust and support (your inner circle), enough low-clustering connections for novel information and bridging opportunities (your boundary-spanning ties). Most people over-invest in high-clustering networks and under-invest in bridges.
- **When to use:** When evaluating whether your network provides information diversity or echo-chamber reinforcement; when building strategic network position for brokerage or innovation; when designing organizational structures and deciding how much inter-team connection to engineer; when assessing whether team composition provides sufficient cognitive diversity.
- **Unique value:** Quantifies the trade-off between network depth (high clustering = trust, support, redundancy) and network breadth (low clustering = novelty, brokerage, diversity). This converts vague networking advice into a measurable structural property with clear strategic implications.
- **Limits:** Measuring clustering coefficient requires network data that's often unavailable. The optimal clustering level depends on goals (innovation vs. execution). Very low clustering can mean isolation rather than brokerage. The metric doesn't capture relationship quality or strength. Network structure interacts with individual personality—not everyone can effectively bridge clusters.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in mathematical graph theory and social network analysis. Developed formally by Watts and Strogatz in small-world network research. Draws on sociology's triadic closure concept (Simmel, Granovetter) and structural balance theory. Related to community detection algorithms and social capital theory (Coleman).
