### Anniversary Reactions · #1467 · `llm-high` · behavioral · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Anniversary reactions are predictable intensifications of grief, anxiety, or somatic symptoms that occur around the date of a significant loss or trauma. These reactions are mediated by multiple mechanisms: conscious anticipation of the date, unconscious body memory (the body 'remembers' even when the mind doesn't track dates), environmental cues (seasonal light, temperature, holiday preparations), and social triggers (others also remembering and discussing the loss). Research shows anniversary reactions can include not just emotional symptoms but physical ones—headaches, fatigue, immune suppression, and even cardiac events increase around loss anniversaries. The phenomenon extends beyond death—anniversary reactions occur around divorce dates, job terminations, medical diagnoses, and other pivotal losses.
- **How it helps:** For knowledge workers, awareness of anniversary reactions enables proactive management. Practical applications: (1) maintain a 'loss calendar' noting significant dates and plan reduced demands around them, (2) if you notice unexplained mood shifts or physical symptoms, check whether an anniversary is approaching, (3) preemptively schedule self-care and lighter workloads around known anniversary dates, (4) as a manager, be aware that team members may have predictable difficult periods, (5) use anniversary dates deliberately—rituals of remembrance can channel the reaction constructively rather than being blindsided by it.
- **When to use:** When approaching the anniversary of a significant loss or trauma. When experiencing unexplained mood or physical changes that might correlate with a date. When planning team schedules around known difficult periods. When designing rituals to mark organizational anniversaries of loss.
- **Unique value:** Transforms an unpredictable-seeming emotional pattern into a predictable, manageable one. Connects emotional grief to physical symptoms in a way that validates somatic experiences. Enables proactive rather than reactive grief management through calendar-based planning.
- **Limits:** Not everyone experiences anniversary reactions, and their absence doesn't indicate poor attachment or insufficient grieving. Over-anticipation of anniversary reactions can create self-fulfilling prophecies. The model doesn't specify how long anniversary reactions persist—some diminish quickly, others last decades.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in bereavement psychology, classical conditioning, and chronobiology research on cyclical emotional patterns. Draws on Pavlovian associative learning theory, clinical grief literature, and phenomenological research on how time itself becomes marked by loss.

### Metabolic Rate Scaling · #13 · `llm-high` · biology · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The biological law that metabolic rate scales with body mass to the 3/4 power—meaning larger organisms are MORE efficient per unit mass than smaller ones. A mouse burns more energy per gram than an elephant. This scaling law extends to organizations (Geoffrey West's research): larger companies are more efficient per employee in operational processes but LESS creative per employee. Larger cities produce more innovation per capita but also more crime per capita. The 3/4 scaling produces sublinear growth in operational needs (economies of scale) and, in cities, superlinear growth in innovation and social interaction. Size brings predictable tradeoffs.
- **How it helps:** Use scaling laws to predict the consequences of growth. As your organization grows: operational efficiency will improve sublinearly (good—economies of scale), but innovation per person will decrease (bad—bureaucratic drag). The intervention: design organizational structures that capture the efficiency benefits of scale while creating innovation structures that resist scale's creativity penalty. Small, autonomous teams within large organizations (Bezos's two-pizza teams) are an attempt to get both. For personal career decisions: larger organizations offer stability and resources but reduced per-person creative output.
- **When to use:** When planning organizational growth and need to anticipate what changes with scale; when evaluating the tradeoffs of company size; when designing organizational structures that need to preserve innovation despite growth; when understanding why scaling produces predictable patterns of efficiency gain and creativity loss.
- **Unique value:** Provides the quantitative LAW governing how organizational properties change with scale—not just 'bigger is different' but specifically HOW things change and at what rate. The 3/4 power law means that doubling organizational size produces LESS than double the operational needs (efficiency gain) but also less than double the creative output (innovation loss). Both are mathematically predictable.
- **Limits:** West's organizational scaling laws are less precisely established than biological ones. Individual organizations can deviate significantly from the scaling predictions. The model describes statistical tendencies, not deterministic outcomes. Organizational design can partially override scaling tendencies. The 3/4 exponent is debated even in biology.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in Kleiber's law (1932) and allometric scaling theory in physiology. Draws on West, Brown, and Enquist's metabolic theory of ecology. Informed by fractal geometry, network transport theory, and thermodynamics. Applied to organizational scaling through analogies between biological and social metabolic rates.

### Simultaneous Invention (Multiple Discovery) · #919 · `llm-high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The historical pattern where the same invention or discovery is made independently by multiple people at roughly the same time: calculus (Newton and Leibniz), evolution (Darwin and Wallace), telephone (Bell and Gray, same day), oxygen (Scheele, Priestley, Lavoisier). Multiple discovery isn't coincidence—it reveals that inventions are less about individual genius and more about when the necessary preconditions (prior knowledge, available technology, cultural readiness) converge. The 'adjacent possible' (Kauffman/Johnson) creates a landscape where certain discoveries become inevitable once prerequisites are in place.
- **How it helps:** Shift your innovation strategy from 'find a genius' to 'identify what's now possible that wasn't before.' The adjacent possible framework: what new combinations of existing knowledge, technology, and capabilities have JUST become feasible? Those are where simultaneous invention will occur—and where competitive advantage goes to the fastest executor, not the most original thinker. For entrepreneurs: the fact that someone else has a similar idea isn't a threat—it's VALIDATION that the adjacent possible has opened. Speed of execution matters more than originality of conception.
- **When to use:** When evaluating whether an innovative idea is truly novel (it probably isn't—check for parallel efforts); when designing innovation strategy around timing rather than originality; when assessing competitive threats from simultaneous independent development; when the 'adjacent possible' framework would help identify where innovation is most likely to emerge.
- **Unique value:** Overturns the heroic inventor narrative by showing that most major innovations were independently conceived by multiple people simultaneously—meaning the innovation was a property of the ENVIRONMENT (preconditions converging) rather than the individual. This shifts innovation strategy from finding genius to recognizing timing.
- **Limits:** Not all innovations fit the multiple discovery pattern—some ARE genuinely singular insights. The 'adjacent possible' is easier to identify in retrospect than prospect. Speed of execution isn't always the determining factor (patents, network effects, and capital also matter). The model can devalue individual creativity.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in the sociology of science (Merton's multiple discovery research, Kuhn's paradigm theory) and information theory. Draws on evolutionary epistemology and the history and philosophy of science literature on simultaneous independent discovery (Newton/Leibniz, Darwin/Wallace). Connected to network effects and knowledge diffusion theory.

### Wright's Law (Learning Curve) · #921 · `high` · cognitive · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Theodore Wright's empirical observation that the cost of producing something decreases by a consistent percentage every time cumulative production doubles—typically 10-25% cost reduction per doubling. Originally observed in airplane manufacturing, the pattern has proven robust across industries: solar panels, batteries, semiconductors, and many manufactured goods. Wright's Law is experience-driven: costs decline because organizations learn by doing—finding efficiencies, improving processes, and developing expertise through cumulative production. Unlike Moore's Law (time-based), Wright's Law is production-based: more output → lower cost per unit.
- **How it helps:** For any product or process where you're investing in scale, project cost declines using Wright's Law: estimate your learning rate (cost reduction per doubling of cumulative production), then forecast costs at future production volumes. This transforms 'when will this be cost-competitive?' from speculation into calculation. Strategic implication: invest aggressively in production volume early, even at a loss, because each unit produced makes future units cheaper. The leader in cumulative production has the lowest costs—creating a compounding advantage that late entrants can't match without massive investment.
- **When to use:** When projecting future production costs for scaling businesses; when deciding whether to invest in volume to drive costs down the learning curve; when evaluating whether a currently expensive technology will become cost-competitive; when competing in industries where cumulative experience determines cost position.
- **Unique value:** Provides a predictive model for cost decline that's more actionable than Moore's Law because it's tied to production volume (which you control) rather than time (which you don't). Wright's Law says: if you produce more, costs WILL decline at a predictable rate—making volume investment a strategic choice rather than a hope.
- **Limits:** Learning rates vary by industry and aren't always predictable. Physical limits eventually constrain cost reduction regardless of cumulative production. The law applies to production processes, not to all business costs. Competing on the learning curve requires capital to sustain losses during the early high-cost phase. Some cost reductions come from scale economies, not learning—the mechanisms are different.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in industrial engineering and learning curve research (Wright, 1936), endogenous growth theory in economics (Romer), and technology adoption research. Draws on cumulative experience theory and operations management research on production efficiency. Connected to experience curve consulting frameworks (BCG) and innovation economics.
- **Recognition cue:** "Is the cost of this following a learning curve, not a calendar?"
- **Worked example:** Solar PV, lithium batteries, aircraft, semiconductors
- *Secondary roles:* — · *Collection:* named-law

### Diversity Prediction Theorem · #393 · `llm-high` · mathematical · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Scott Page's mathematical theorem: Collective Error = Average Individual Error - Diversity of Predictions. The crowd's prediction accuracy equals the average individual's accuracy MINUS the diversity of their predictions. This means: increasing diversity (more varied predictions) improves collective accuracy just as much as improving individual accuracy. A group of diverse mediocre predictors can outperform a group of similar excellent predictors—because diversity reduces collective error through cancellation of uncorrelated individual errors. The theorem applies to forecasting, problem-solving, and any aggregation of independent judgments.
- **How it helps:** To improve group prediction or decision quality: invest in DIVERSITY of judgment as much as QUALITY of judgment. The practices: (1) Recruit predictors/advisors from different backgrounds, using different models. (2) Collect predictions INDEPENDENTLY before discussion (to preserve diversity). (3) Average or aggregate predictions rather than selecting one. (4) Weight by track record but don't eliminate low-performing diverse predictors—their diversity contribution may exceed their individual accuracy deficit. The meta-principle: the next person to add to your advisory group should be the one most DIFFERENT from the existing group, not the one most individually accurate.
- **When to use:** When collective prediction accuracy needs improvement; when team composition for forecasting needs to balance individual accuracy with diversity; when understanding mathematically why diverse groups outperform homogeneous expert groups; when prediction aggregation strategy needs optimization.
- **Unique value:** MATHEMATICALLY PROVES that diversity contributes to collective accuracy on equal footing with individual ability: Collective Error = Average Error - Diversity. This is not an opinion or a social argument—it's a theorem. The implication: organizations underinvesting in cognitive diversity are PROVABLY leaving prediction accuracy on the table.
- **Limits:** The theorem assumes independent errors, which discussion and social influence violate. Diversity without competence doesn't help. Measuring diversity of predictions is difficult. The theorem applies to aggregation, not to individual decisions.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in Page's Diversity Prediction Theorem (2007): crowd error = average individual error minus diversity. Draws on statistical aggregation theory, wisdom of crowds research (Galton, Surowiecki), and ensemble methods in machine learning. Rooted in variance decomposition mathematics and epistemic diversity research.

### Random Walks · #436 · `llm-high` · mathematical · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Scott Page's formal model of processes where each step is random and independent of previous steps—like a drunk person stumbling left or right with equal probability. In finance: the random walk hypothesis suggests stock price movements are unpredictable (each movement is independent of previous movements). In innovation: discovery processes often follow random walks through possibility space—you can't predict which combination will produce a breakthrough. The key insight: random walks produce DIFFUSION over time—the range of possible outcomes expands with the square root of the number of steps. This means: random processes create far wider outcome distributions than intuition suggests, and long-term outcomes from random walks are highly unpredictable.
- **How it helps:** Identify which processes in your domain are closer to random walks (unpredictable, independent steps) versus which are directional (trending, predictable). For random walk processes: don't try to predict specific outcomes—instead, manage the RANGE of outcomes (position sizing, diversification, stop losses). For directional processes: prediction and trend-following are legitimate strategies. The diagnostic: does past performance predict future performance in this domain? If not (as in many financial markets): the process is closer to random walk, and strategies based on pattern detection will fail.
- **When to use:** When distinguishing between predictable and unpredictable processes in a domain; when financial markets exhibit random-walk characteristics that undermine pattern-based strategies; when understanding the diffusion property of random processes (outcomes spread wider over time); when the unpredictability of innovation processes needs to be acknowledged in planning.
- **Unique value:** Provides the formal model for understanding why some processes resist prediction despite appearing patterned: if the process is a random walk, ANY pattern in historical data is noise, not signal. This is the mathematical foundation for index investing and for accepting irreducible uncertainty in innovation.
- **Limits:** Few real processes are pure random walks—most have some structure. Financial markets show some predictability at certain timescales. Random walk models can justify excessive passivity. The model is better as a null hypothesis than as a description of reality.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in probability theory (Pearson's random walk, Einstein's Brownian motion 1905), stochastic processes, and financial mathematics (Bachelier 1900). Draws on martingale theory, the efficient market hypothesis (Fama), and physics of diffusion. Foundational to quantitative finance and statistical physics.
- *Secondary roles:* MECHANISM · *Collection:* statistical-concept

### The Replicator Equation · #457 · `llm-high` · mathematical · CLAIM
- **What it is:** A mathematical framework from evolutionary game theory describing how the frequency of different STRATEGIES (or types) in a population changes over time based on their relative FITNESS. If strategy A produces higher payoffs than the population average, its frequency INCREASES; if lower, it DECREASES. The equation: dx_i/dt = x_i(f_i - f̄), where x_i is the proportion of strategy i, f_i is its fitness, and f̄ is population average fitness. As a mental model: the Replicator Equation formalizes COMPETITIVE DYNAMICS—strategies that outperform the average grow; strategies that underperform shrink. This applies to biological evolution, cultural evolution, market competition, and idea spread. The equilibrium points reveal which strategies survive long-term coexistence.
- **How it helps:** Apply replicator dynamics thinking to competitive environments. For business: strategies (business models, practices, products) that outperform the market average will grow in prevalence; those that underperform will shrink. The key insight: fitness is RELATIVE to the average, not absolute—a strategy can be good in absolute terms but shrink if it's below the population average. For markets: predict which approaches will grow and which will decline by assessing their relative fitness. For culture: ideas that replicate faster than average spread; those that replicate slower decline.
- **When to use:** When competitive dynamics need mathematical formalization; when predicting which strategies will grow or decline based on relative fitness; when understanding why below-average strategies are eliminated even if they're 'good enough' in absolute terms; when evolutionary game theory would illuminate competitive coexistence.
- **Unique value:** Provides the MATHEMATICAL ENGINE of evolution: strategies grow or shrink based on relative fitness. This single equation captures the core dynamic of all competitive systems—biological, economic, and cultural—making competitive outcomes formally predictable.
- **Limits:** Real competitive dynamics include factors beyond relative fitness (noise, drift, spatial structure). The equation assumes infinite, well-mixed populations. Fitness is hard to measure in practice. The model doesn't account for innovation (new strategies entering). Equilibrium predictions may not match dynamic reality.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in evolutionary game theory (Maynard Smith's ESS concept 1973), population genetics (Fisher's selection equations), and mathematical biology. The replicator equation (Taylor-Jonker 1978) formalizes how strategy frequencies evolve based on fitness differentials. Draws on Price equation and game-theoretic stability.
- *Secondary roles:* MECHANISM · *Collection:* evolutionary-principle

### Price's Law (Square Root of Contributors Do Half the Work) · #502 · `llm-high` · mathematical · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Derek de Solla Price's observation that in any productive group, roughly the square root of the total number of contributors produces half the output. In a company of 100, about 10 people produce half the value. This has profound implications for scaling: as organizations grow, the percentage of high contributors shrinks (square root of 10,000 is only 100, or 1%). This explains why productivity per person typically declines as organizations scale.
- **How it helps:** Provides quantitative frameworks for analysis, prediction, and optimization. Turns intuitions into testable models and vague ideas into precise tools.
- **When to use:** When analyzing data; when building predictive models; when evaluating statistical claims; when quantifying uncertainty.
- **Unique value:** Provides a specific analytical lens through 'Price's Law' that reveals patterns and dynamics invisible from conventional perspectives.
- **Limits:** All models are simplifications that can mislead when applied beyond their valid domain. Context matters. Multiple models should be used together for robust analysis.
- **Foundations:** Derives from Lotka's bibliometric law (1926) and Zipf's law, situating it within scientometrics and power law distributions. Connects to preferential attachment models in network science (Barabási) and Matthew Effect (Merton) in sociology of science. Square-root concentration reflects multiplicative compounding of advantage.

### Mythical Man-Month · #1360 · `llm-high` · organizational · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Fred Brooks's insight that adding people to a late software project makes it later. Communication overhead grows quadratically with team size (n people require n(n-1)/2 communication channels), while productive capacity grows only linearly. A task that takes one person ten months cannot be done by ten people in one month because the coordination cost overwhelms the additional capacity. The deeper insight: some work is partitionable (can be divided) and some isn't. Pregnancy takes nine months regardless of how many people you assign.
- **How it helps:** Before adding headcount to a delayed project, ask: is the bottleneck capacity or coordination? If the team is already spending significant time in meetings, handoffs, and alignment, adding people increases coordination costs without proportional output gains. The counter-intuitive solution is often subtracting: smaller, more focused teams with fewer communication channels frequently outperform larger teams. Apply this to any knowledge work, not just software.
- **When to use:** When a project is behind schedule and the instinct is to add people; when team size is growing but output isn't scaling proportionally; when meeting load is consuming productive hours; when deciding between one large team and multiple small teams.
- **Unique value:** Provides the mathematical explanation for why bigger teams aren't better teams. The quadratic growth of communication channels means that doubling team size more than doubles coordination cost while only doubling potential capacity. This explains the consistent finding that small teams outperform large ones per capita.
- **Limits:** Some projects genuinely need more people—not all work is coordination-bound. The model applies most strongly to knowledge work with high interdependencies. In parallel, independent work (like harvesting), adding people does scale linearly. The model can be used to justify under-resourcing projects that genuinely need more capacity.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in Fred Brooks's software engineering empiricism (The Mythical Man-Month, 1975). Draws on transaction cost economics (communication overhead as coordination cost), Amdahl's Law from computer science, and organizational design research on team size and communication complexity (n(n-1)/2 channels).

### Requisite Variety (Ashby's Law) · #1091 · `llm-high` · systems · CLAIM
- **What it is:** W. Ross Ashby's cybernetic law: a controller must have at least as much variety (range of responses) as the system it's trying to control. A thermostat with only on/off can control temperature to a range; one with variable output can control it precisely. Applied to management: a leader with only one leadership style (command-and-control) cannot effectively manage a team that requires multiple approaches (mentoring, coaching, delegating, directing). An organization with a single strategy cannot navigate an environment that presents multiple types of challenges. Requisite variety demands that your response repertoire match the complexity of your environment.
- **How it helps:** Audit the variety gap: how complex is the environment you're managing, and does your response repertoire match? If your market is highly variable but your strategy is monolithic, you lack requisite variety. If your team has diverse needs but your management style is uniform, you lack requisite variety. The interventions: (1) Expand your response repertoire (learn new skills, strategies, approaches). (2) Reduce environmental variety to match your capacity (simplify, focus, choose markets you can handle). (3) Build organizational variety through diverse teams, decentralized decision-making, and flexible processes.
- **When to use:** When management approaches aren't working despite being 'right' for some situations (they may lack variety for the full range of situations); when organizations struggle with environmental complexity; when designing systems that need to handle diverse inputs; when leadership development needs a framework for what capabilities to build.
- **Unique value:** Provides the cybernetic LAW (not guideline) that explains why simplistic approaches fail in complex environments: they literally cannot contain enough variety to match the environment's complexity. This converts vague 'be adaptable' advice into a specific diagnostic: does your response variety match your environmental variety?
- **Limits:** Maximizing variety is expensive—you can't prepare for everything. Some environments are simpler than they appear, and excessive variety is wasteful. The 'variety' in complex systems is hard to quantify. Ashby's Law comes from engineering cybernetics and maps imperfectly to social systems. Sometimes reducing environmental variety (choosing simpler markets) is better than expanding response variety.
- **Foundations:** Developed by cybernetician W. Ross Ashby in his foundational work on cybernetics. Rooted in information theory, control theory, and systems theory. Draws on Shannon's communication theory and mathematical analysis of regulatory systems. Applied widely in management science, organizational design, and cognitive science.

### Scale-Free Networks (Power Law Degree Distribution) · #1098 · `high` · systems · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Networks where a few nodes have enormously more connections than the average, following a power law distribution rather than a bell curve. The internet (a few sites get most traffic), social networks (a few people have millions of followers), and citation networks (a few papers get most citations) are all scale-free. The mechanism is preferential attachment: new nodes preferentially connect to already well-connected nodes ('the rich get richer'), creating extreme inequality in connectivity. Scale-free networks are robust to random failure (most nodes don't matter) but vulnerable to targeted attack on hubs.
- **How it helps:** Identify whether the networks you operate in are scale-free, and strategize accordingly. In scale-free networks: becoming a hub is exponentially more valuable than being a typical node. Marketing: reaching one hub (influencer) can be worth reaching 10,000 typical nodes. Recruitment: one highly connected hire brings an entire network. Risk: your dependence on a few hub nodes (key clients, critical suppliers, star employees) creates concentration risk. Protect your hubs and don't over-rely on any single one.
- **When to use:** When a few nodes (customers, partners, employees) account for disproportionate value; when designing marketing, sales, or networking strategies; when assessing system vulnerability to targeted attack; when evaluating whether network effects in your market create winner-take-most dynamics.
- **Unique value:** Reveals that most real-world networks are NOT democratic—they're dominated by a few hubs. This overturns the intuitive assumption that network participants are roughly equal. In scale-free networks, strategy must account for extreme inequality: hubs matter exponentially more than average nodes.
- **Limits:** Not all networks are scale-free—some genuinely have more equal distributions. The power law fit is often debated for specific networks. Preferential attachment isn't the only mechanism; other factors create hubs. Scale-free network theory describes static structure but real networks are dynamic. Hub identification requires data that may not be available.
- **Foundations:** Developed by Barabási and Albert, building on preferential attachment theory. Rooted in statistical physics, graph theory, and stochastic processes. Draws on Pareto distributions, fractal geometry (Mandelbrot), and Zipf's law in linguistics. Applied across internet topology, biological networks, and social systems.
- *Secondary roles:* — · *Collection:* complexity-concept

### S-Curve · #1245 · `high` · systems · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The characteristic shape of growth in most natural and business systems: slow initial growth (struggling for traction), rapid exponential growth (hitting product-market fit), then gradual deceleration and plateau (market saturation). Every technology, product, and organization follows some version of the S-curve. Knowing where you are on the curve determines whether to invest more, harvest, or prepare for the next curve.
- **How it helps:** The most important growth diagnostics framework. Prevents misinterpreting early-stage slowness as failure (you may be pre-inflection) and mid-stage deceleration as crisis (you may be approaching natural saturation). Guides resource allocation across the lifecycle.
- **When to use:** When evaluating growth trajectory. When growth is slowing and you need to diagnose why. When deciding whether to double down on current product or invest in next-generation. When a market seems either impossibly slow or unsustainably fast.
- **Unique value:** Provides a single visual framework that integrates product lifecycle, market adoption, and resource allocation decisions. Where you are on the S-curve changes everything about optimal strategy.
- **Limits:** Real growth curves are messier than idealized S-curves. Multiple S-curves often overlap. Identifying your position on the curve in real-time is much harder than in retrospect.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in innovation diffusion theory (Rogers), technology lifecycle models (Moore's crossing the chasm), and evolutionary biology (sigmoid growth curves). Draws on Bass diffusion model in marketing, Kondratieff waves in economics, and Carlota Perez's techno-economic paradigm theory.
- **Recognition cue:** "Adoption looks flat right now, but is it about to inflect?"
- **Worked example:** Smartphone adoption 2007-2015. Solar PV cost decline

### Zawinski’s Law · #1193 · `llm-high` · technology · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Jamie Zawinski's observation that 'every program attempts to expand until it can read mail. Those programs which cannot so expand are replaced by ones which can.' More broadly: software (and organizations, and products) inevitably accumulates features until it becomes bloated, trying to be everything to everyone. Zawinski's Law describes feature creep as a gravitational force—not a design flaw but an emergent property of successful systems responding to user demands. Each individual feature addition seems reasonable; the cumulative effect is a system that does many things poorly rather than one thing well.
- **How it helps:** Recognize Zawinski's Law as an ever-present force and build active countermeasures. Product discipline means saying no to features that seem individually reasonable but collectively bloat the product. The test: does this feature serve our CORE use case, or is it expanding our scope toward 'reading mail'? Organizational version: does this initiative serve our core mission, or are we expanding to justify our existence? The countermeasure is active pruning—regularly removing features, programs, and initiatives rather than only adding them.
- **When to use:** When products, organizations, or processes have accumulated scope beyond their original purpose; when feature requests seem individually reasonable but collectively dilute the core value proposition; when designing product strategy and need to resist scope creep; when evaluating whether software or organizational bloat is undermining core effectiveness.
- **Unique value:** Names the specific gravitational force that pulls products and organizations toward bloat. Unlike vague warnings about 'scope creep,' Zawinski's Law identifies the mechanism: each expansion seems rational individually, making the cumulative bloat invisible until the system is already compromised. The law makes the invisible force visible and therefore resistible.
- **Limits:** Some expansion is genuinely valuable—platforms that expand to adjacent features can capture more value. The 'original purpose' isn't always the best purpose. User demands for features sometimes reflect genuine needs. Overly rigid scope can prevent necessary evolution. The law is an observation about tendency, not a prohibition on expansion.
- **Foundations:** Derived from software engineering empirics and systems theory. Zawinski's observation reflects Gall's Law on system complexity and concept creep in product development. Draws on innovation management literature on scope creep, feature bloat, and the second-system effect described by Brooks in The Mythical Man-Month.
- *Secondary roles:* MECHANISM · *Collection:* named-law

### Social Contagion of Habits · #1733 · `medium` · behavioral · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Christakis and Fowler's Framingham Heart Study network data revealed behaviors spread through social networks: if a friend becomes obese, your risk increases 57%. A friend's friend's obesity increases your risk 20%. Even three degrees of separation show measurable influence. This pattern holds for smoking, happiness, loneliness, and alcohol consumption. The mechanism combines social norm calibration (adjusting 'normal' based on your network), mimicry (mirror neuron-driven imitation), and selection/influence spirals (choosing friends who reinforce behaviors, who further influence you). Your behavior is significantly determined by people up to three degrees away—most of whom you've never met.
- **How it helps:** This is a powerful, underappreciated behavior change lever: deliberately curate your social environment. Want to exercise more? Join an exerciser community. Want to build a company? Surround yourself with entrepreneurs. For leaders, this explains why culture is organizational destiny: behavioral norms propagate through networks regardless of formal policies. Auditing your five closest contacts' habits predicts your own trajectory better than your stated intentions.
- **When to use:** When individual willpower-based change keeps failing. When organizational culture doesn't match stated values. When evaluating the behavioral influence of social groups, communities, or networks. When designing community-based behavior change interventions.
- **Unique value:** Quantifies the specific, measurable influence that social networks exert on individual behavior up to three degrees of separation, providing evidence for the folk wisdom that 'you are the average of your five closest friends' and making social environment design a first-class behavior change strategy.
- **Limits:** Observational data makes it difficult to fully separate social influence from shared environment and self-selection effects. The specific transmission mechanisms are debated. Network effects vary by behavior type, tie strength, and cultural context. Deliberately changing social networks raises ethical questions about instrumentalizing friendships.

### Peak Performance Curve (Yerkes-Dodson Law) · #917 · `medium` · cognitive · CLAIM

- **Foundations:** Rooted in Yerkes and Dodson's 1908 animal learning experiments, cognitive psychology research on arousal and performance, and neuroscience of cortisol/norepinephrine effects on prefrontal functioning. ### Bathtub Curve (Failure Rate Over Lifecycle) · #2052 · `medium` · design · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The bathtub curve describes how failure rates change over a product's lifecycle in three phases. Infant mortality (early life): high failure rates from manufacturing defects, installation errors, and weak components that are weeded out during burn-in or early use. Useful life (middle): low, approximately constant failure rate from random, unpredictable causes. Wear-out (end of life): rising failure rates from fatigue, corrosion, degradation, and accumulated damage. Practitioners use this model to set maintenance strategies: aggressive inspection during infant mortality, condition-based monitoring during useful life, and proactive replacement before wear-out. The curve also informs warranty design — warranties cover infant mortality failures, which are the manufacturer's responsibility.
- **How it helps:** The bathtub curve applies to any system with a lifecycle. New employees have high 'failure rates' (mistakes, misunderstandings) during onboarding, low rates during productive tenure, and increasing issues as burnout or skill obsolescence sets in. New software releases follow the same pattern — many bugs initially, stability in the middle, increasing fragility as technical debt accumulates. Understanding which lifecycle phase you're in determines whether to focus on screening (infant mortality), monitoring (useful life), or replacement planning (wear-out). Attempting to fix wear-out failures with the same tools that address infant mortality is a common and costly mistake.
- **When to use:** When planning maintenance or support strategies for any system over its lifecycle, when distinguishing between early-life failures and end-of-life degradation, when setting warranty or support periods, or when deciding whether to repair or replace aging components.
- **Unique value:** Maps three distinct failure regimes (infant mortality, random, wear-out) to three distinct management strategies (screening, monitoring, replacement), preventing the common error of applying one-size-fits-all reliability approaches across different lifecycle phases.
- **Limits:** Not all systems follow the classic bathtub shape — some have no infant mortality phase, and modern electronics may have flatter curves. The transitions between phases are gradual, not sharp. The model describes populations, not individual units. Complex systems with many components may show composite curves that obscure individual component behavior.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in reliability engineering (Weibull analysis, life data statistics), electrical engineering (early semiconductor research), and actuarial science. Draws on probability theory, Bayesian reliability estimation, and failure mode analysis. Influenced by military standards for component qualification and warranty economics.

(4) Recognize that the factors driving later-life happiness (acceptance, selectivity, emotional regulation) can be cultivated deliberately.

### Contagion Models 1 Diffusion · #388 · `medium` · mathematical · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Mathematical models of how things spread through populations: ideas, behaviors, diseases, technologies, and information. Simple diffusion: each 'infected' node spreads to uninfected neighbors with some probability. The key parameters: transmission rate (probability of spread per contact), recovery rate (probability of stopping spread), and network structure (who contacts whom). The basic reproduction number R₀: the average number of new infections per infected individual. If R₀ > 1: the contagion spreads exponentially. If R₀ < 1: the contagion dies out. Diffusion models explain why some innovations go viral and others don't—the product of transmission rate and network structure determines R₀.
- **How it helps:** For any spreading process: identify and optimize the parameters. To INCREASE spread (marketing, adoption): increase transmission rate (make it easier to share, more compelling to try) and target network hubs (people with many connections). To DECREASE spread (disease, misinformation): reduce transmission rate (barriers, verification) and break network connections (quarantine, content moderation). The R₀ framework: calculate whether your product/idea/disease is above or below the spread threshold—and focus intervention on the parameter that's most addressable.
- **When to use:** When viral spread needs to be understood, promoted, or contained; when R₀ analysis would determine whether a contagion will grow or die; when network structure's role in spreading processes needs analysis; when marketing or public health interventions need to target the right parameter.
- **Unique value:** Provides the mathematical framework for understanding ALL spreading processes through the same parameters: transmission rate × network structure → R₀ → spread or die. Whether it's a virus, an idea, or a product, the same math applies—making contagion modeling universally applicable.
- **Limits:** Simple diffusion models assume homogeneous mixing. Real networks are heterogeneous. Parameters change over time. Behavioral responses modify spread dynamics. Multiple contagions can interact.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in epidemiological modeling (Kermack-McKendrick SIR model 1927), network diffusion theory (Bass model), and complex systems science. Draws on graph theory, social influence research (Rogers' diffusion of innovations), and mathematical biology of contagion spread through heterogeneous populations.

### Contagion Models 2 SIS Model · #389 · `medium` · mathematical · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The Susceptible-Infected-Susceptible (SIS) model: individuals can be infected, recover, and be REINFECTED—cycling between susceptible and infected states indefinitely. Unlike the SIR model (where recovery produces immunity), SIS captures diseases and behaviors that provide no lasting immunity: common colds, fashion trends, and recurring organizational problems. The SIS model produces endemic equilibrium: if R₀ > 1, the contagion never fully disappears but reaches a steady state where a constant proportion of the population is infected at any time. The implication: some problems are ENDEMIC rather than solvable—they require ongoing management rather than one-time fixes.
- **How it helps:** Identify whether your 'contagion' follows SIS dynamics (recurring, no immunity) or SIR dynamics (one-time, immunity after recovery). For SIS problems (recurring organizational issues, cyclical cultural problems): stop expecting permanent solutions and design ongoing management systems. The steady-state infection rate depends on R₀: reduce transmission rate or increase recovery rate to lower the endemic level. For business: some customer churn is SIS—customers leave, come back, and leave again. The strategy isn't zero churn but optimal management of the endemic cycle.
- **When to use:** When recurring problems need management rather than solution; when distinguishing between one-time fixable issues and endemic dynamics; when the steady-state level of a recurring problem needs to be reduced rather than eliminated; when understanding why some organizational problems never fully resolve.
- **Unique value:** Identifies the crucial distinction between problems that can be SOLVED (SIR—fix once, immunity) and problems that must be MANAGED (SIS—recurring, no immunity). This prevents the frustration of expecting permanent solutions to structurally endemic problems, and redirects effort toward reducing the endemic level.
- **Limits:** Real contagions are more complex than SIS or SIR. The model assumes homogeneous population. Parameter estimation is difficult. The distinction between SIS and SIR isn't always clear. Some SIS problems can be structurally converted to SIR.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in mathematical epidemiology (SIS/SIR compartmental models), differential equations, and network science. The SIS (susceptible-infected-susceptible) model captures endemic equilibria. Draws on basic reproduction number (R0) theory, bifurcation analysis, and applied probability in disease modeling.

### Pareto Principle · #499 · `medium` · mathematical · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Vilfredo Pareto's observation that approximately 80% of effects come from 20% of causes—a power-law distribution found across remarkably diverse domains. 80% of revenue from 20% of customers. 80% of bugs from 20% of code. 80% of results from 20% of effort. The exact ratio varies but the pattern holds: a SMALL number of inputs produce a DISPROPORTIONATELY LARGE share of outputs. As a mental model: the Pareto Principle reveals that inputs are NOT equally productive—and that identifying and focusing on the vital few (the productive 20%) while managing or eliminating the trivial many (the unproductive 80%) is the fundamental leverage strategy. The principle is fractal: within the top 20%, a further 20% produces 80% of THAT output (the 4% that produces 64%).
- **How it helps:** Identify the vital 20% in every domain and allocate disproportionately. The practices: (1) For revenue: identify your top 20% of customers and invest in retaining and growing them. (2) For productivity: identify the 20% of activities that produce 80% of results and protect them from interruption. (3) For problems: identify the 20% of causes producing 80% of issues and fix those first. (4) Apply the fractal principle: within the top 20%, find the top 20% again—this is the 4% that produces ~64% of total results. For entrepreneurs: your most leveraged work is finding and focusing on the vital few in every domain.
- **When to use:** When resource allocation needs to prioritize the vital few over the trivial many; when identifying the highest-leverage inputs would dramatically improve output; when 80/20 analysis would reveal where disproportionate effort is being wasted; when the fractal application of Pareto would identify the ultra-high-leverage activities.
- **Unique value:** Reveals the UNIVERSAL pattern of unequal productivity: in virtually every domain, a small number of inputs produce a disproportionate share of outputs. This single insight redirects strategy from 'do more' to 'do the RIGHT more'—making input identification more valuable than input quantity.
- **Limits:** The 80/20 ratio is approximate, not exact. Some domains don't follow Pareto distributions. Over-focus on the top 20% can neglect important long-tail activities. The vital few can change over time. Pareto analysis requires data to identify the vital few.
- **Foundations:** Originates in Pareto's empirical observations on land distribution in Italy (1896) and connects to power law mathematics and self-organized criticality. Draws on operations research, quality management (Juran's quality control), and complexity science. The ratio varies—strict 80/20 is a heuristic, not a law.
- **Recognition cue:** "Most of the X comes from a few of the Y"
- **Worked example:** 80% of revenue from 20% of customers; 80% of bugs from 20% of code

### Half-life · #644 · `medium` · physics · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The time it takes for half of a substance to decay or disappear—originally from nuclear physics but applicable to any exponential decay process. Information has a half-life: scientific papers lose relevance, news becomes stale, skills become outdated. Knowledge half-life varies enormously: physics principles (centuries), programming languages (years), stock tips (hours). Shane Parrish popularized this application: the value of what you learn depends not just on its initial usefulness but on how long it remains useful. Mental models (centuries-long half-life) > programming paradigms (decades) > specific frameworks (years) > specific tools (months) > news (hours). The same hour invested in a long-half-life topic (first principles, human psychology, mathematics) produces orders of magnitude more lifetime value than an hour invested in a short-half-life topic (current platform features, trending frameworks). - **Limits:** Some short-half-life knowledge is necessary for immediate practical needs. Half-life of knowledge is hard to estimate in advance. In rapidly changing fields, short-half-life knowledge may be all that's available. The model can justify ignoring practical skills in favor of abstract knowledge. Some long-half-life knowledge is never applied.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in nuclear physics and radiometric decay theory. ### Conservation Laws (What's Conserved When Things Change) · #653 · `llm-medium` · physics · CLAIM
- **What it is:** In physics: certain quantities (energy, momentum, charge) cannot be created or destroyed—they can only be transformed or transferred. Energy isn't lost when a ball stops bouncing; it's converted to heat. As a mental model: in any transformation, something is conserved—and identifying what's conserved reveals the true nature of the change. When an organization 'restructures,' the total work doesn't disappear—it's redistributed. When a market 'disrupts,' the total demand doesn't vanish—it's redirected. When a habit 'changes,' the underlying need doesn't disappear—it finds a new expression. Conservation laws tell you: look for what moved, not what disappeared.
- **How it helps:** When observing any change: ask 'what's conserved?' A company that cuts a department hasn't eliminated the work—where did it go? (Other departments, outsourcing, or it's not getting done.) A person who stops one habit hasn't eliminated the underlying need—what's now satisfying it? (A different habit, or nothing, which creates tension.) In market disruption: the customer need is conserved even when the product form changes—identify the conserved need and you identify the new opportunity. Conservation thinking prevents the illusion that things simply disappear.
- **When to use:** When organizational changes seem to eliminate functions (they've been redistributed, not eliminated); when understanding market disruption requires identifying what customer need persists despite product changes; when personal habit change needs to address the underlying need, not just the surface behavior; when any transformation seems to create or destroy something that actually transforms.
- **Unique value:** Provides the most fundamental analytical principle for understanding change: something is always conserved. This prevents the two most common errors in change analysis—believing something was created from nothing (it was transformed from something) and believing something disappeared (it was transferred somewhere). Conservation thinking reveals the true structure of any transformation.
- **Limits:** In social systems, 'conservation' is metaphorical—work, need, and demand are not precisely conserved the way energy is. Some things DO genuinely diminish or emerge in complex systems. The model can produce overly mechanical thinking about human systems. Identifying what's conserved requires understanding the system deeply enough to track the transfer.
- **Foundations:** Grounded in Noether's theorem and classical physics conservation laws (energy, momentum, charge). Draws on symmetry principles in physics, thermodynamics' first law, and systems theory's steady-state analysis. Related to invariant analysis in mathematics, accounting identities in economics, and constraint identification in systems.

### Self-Organized Criticality (Bak) · #1093 · `medium` · systems · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Per Bak's discovery that many complex systems naturally evolve toward a 'critical state'—a state of marginal stability where small perturbations can trigger cascading events of any size. The classic model: a sand pile where grains are added one at a time. The pile self-organizes to a critical angle where each new grain might cause nothing, a small slide, or an avalanche—and the size distribution of events follows a power law (many small events, few large ones, no characteristic scale). Applied to markets, earthquakes, forest fires, and organizational failures: these systems are always near criticality, making large cascading events inevitable but unpredictable in timing.
- **How it helps:** Design systems assuming large cascading events WILL occur. Build circuit breakers, reserves, and recovery capability rather than trying to prevent every small perturbation. The Bak insight: large events aren't caused by large perturbations—they're caused by small perturbations in critically-loaded systems.
- **When to use:** When systems seem stable but suddenly produce large cascading events; when the search for specific causes of large failures is less useful than understanding the critical state that enabled them; when designing systems that need to be resilient to power-law distributed events; when understanding why catastrophic failures seem to emerge from trivial triggers.
- **Unique value:** Explains why large system failures are inevitable yet unpredictable: the system self-organizes to criticality where ANY small event CAN trigger a cascade of ANY size. This shifts focus from prediction (impossible) to resilience (essential) and from trigger-hunting (misleading) to system-state assessment (useful).
- **Limits:** Not all systems exhibit self-organized criticality. The sand pile model is a simplification. Identifying criticality in real systems is difficult. The model can justify fatalism ('big events are inevitable'). Some large events DO have identifiable, preventable causes.
- **Foundations:** Developed by physicist Per Bak and colleagues. Rooted in statistical physics, specifically critical phenomena and phase transitions. Draws on cellular automata, sandpile dynamics, and power law statistics. Related to fractal geometry (Mandelbrot), chaos theory, and complexity science's edge-of-chaos concept.

### Beer Distribution Game (Bullwhip Effect) · #1102 · `medium` · systems · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Jay Forrester and Peter Senge's simulation game demonstrating the bullwhip effect: in a supply chain, small fluctuations in consumer demand produce increasingly amplified oscillations as you move upstream—retailers order slightly more, wholesalers order much more, distributors order even more, and manufacturers swing wildly between overproduction and underproduction. The bullwhip effect is a systemic phenomenon: each rational actor in the chain, responding to local information with reasonable decisions, produces system-level instability that no actor intended or desires. The game demonstrates that system structure, not individual decision quality, produces the dysfunction.
- **How it helps:** In any chain where decisions ripple through multiple stages: look for bullwhip dynamics. The symptoms: oscillating boom-bust cycles, persistent inventory problems, and overreactions to demand signals. The structural fixes: (1) Share end-consumer demand data throughout the chain (reducing information lag). (2) Reduce order batching (smaller, more frequent orders reduce amplification). (3) Stabilize pricing (eliminating forward-buying incentives). (4) Shorten the supply chain (fewer stages = less amplification). The meta-lesson: most supply chain dysfunction is structural, not behavioral—fixing individual decisions without fixing the structure changes nothing.
- **When to use:** When supply chains exhibit oscillating overstock/understock patterns; when small demand changes produce large upstream disruptions; when understanding why rational individual decisions produce irrational system outcomes; when designing information systems that reduce amplification across organizational chains.
- **Unique value:** Provides the definitive demonstration that system structure can override individual rationality: perfectly rational actors in a bullwhip-structured supply chain INEVITABLY produce instability. This proves that some problems are structural, not behavioral—and that behavioral solutions (train better decision-makers) won't work without structural change.
- **Limits:** Real supply chains are more complex than the game. Information technology has reduced some bullwhip effects. Not all supply chain instability is bullwhip-driven. The game's simplifications may not capture industry-specific dynamics. Structural solutions to the bullwhip effect require coordination that competing firms may resist.
- **Foundations:** Developed by Jay Forrester and popularized by Sterman at MIT Sloan. Rooted in system dynamics, operations research, and supply chain management theory. Draws on control theory, feedback systems, and behavioral decision theory. Related to Holt-Winters forecasting and the bullwhip effect (Lee, Padmanabhan, Whang).

### Feedback Loops · #1121 · `medium` · systems · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Circular causal mechanisms where a system's output loops back to influence its input—either REINFORCING (positive feedback: output amplifies input, producing exponential growth or decline) or BALANCING (negative feedback: output counteracts input, producing stability and homeostasis). Reinforcing loops: viral growth (more users → more content → more users), bank runs (more withdrawals → more panic → more withdrawals), compound interest. Balancing loops: thermostat (too hot → cooling activates → temperature drops), market equilibrium (high price → reduced demand → price drops). As a mental model: feedback loops are the ENGINES of system behavior—understanding which loops are operating and whether they're reinforcing or balancing reveals why systems grow, stabilize, oscillate, or collapse.
- **How it helps:** Map the feedback loops in any system to understand and predict its behavior. The diagnostic: (1) Identify the key variables. (2) Trace how each variable influences others. (3) Follow the chain back to the original variable. (4) Classify: does the loop AMPLIFY (reinforcing) or COUNTERACT (balancing) changes? For strategy: (1) Reinforce positive feedback loops that serve your goals (network effects, brand reputation). (2) Install balancing loops where runaway dynamics are dangerous (spending limits, quality controls). (3) Watch for DELAYS in feedback loops—they produce oscillation and overshoot.
- **When to use:** When system behavior needs explanation through circular causation; when reinforcing loops need identification to leverage or interrupt; when balancing loops need strengthening for stability; when feedback delays are producing oscillation or overshoot.
- **Unique value:** Reveals that linear cause-and-effect thinking SYSTEMATICALLY fails for systems with feedback loops—because effects become causes through the loop. This makes feedback loop identification the FIRST step in systems analysis, before any linear cause-effect reasoning.
- **Limits:** Real systems have many interacting loops. Identifying all relevant loops is difficult. Loop dynamics change over time. Delays make loop behavior hard to predict. Not all systems are well-described by feedback loops.
- **Foundations:** Rooted in cybernetics (Wiener), control theory, and systems theory. Foundational to systems dynamics (Forrester, Meadows). Draws on electrical engineering, biology (homeostasis), and economics. Related to positive/negative feedback in thermostats, population models, and market dynamics. Core to Senge's Fifth Discipline.
- *Secondary roles:* — · *Collection:* mental-model-canon

### Metcalfe's Law (Extended: Value of Networks) · #1175 · `medium` · technology · CLAIM
- **What it is:** The foundational network economics principle that the value of a telecommunications network is proportional to the square of the number of its users. With n users, there are n(n-1)/2 possible connections, which grows roughly as n². This creates a positive feedback loop: more users → more value → more users. Metcalfe's Law explains why network businesses exhibit winner-take-most dynamics, why they're worth investing in before profitability, and why network effects create the strongest competitive moats in technology. It also explains why networks that lose users can collapse rapidly—the feedback loop works both ways.
- **How it helps:** Apply Metcalfe's thinking to any product or service with network properties. The key strategic question: does adding one more user make the product more valuable for ALL existing users? If yes, you have network effects and should invest aggressively in growth. If no, you have a regular product and should focus on unit economics. For competing against network incumbents: you can't out-scale them, so compete on a dimension where network size doesn't matter (niche quality, specific use case, different network topology).
- **When to use:** When evaluating businesses with network properties; when deciding between growth investment and monetization for network products; when analyzing competitive dynamics in platform markets; when assessing the defensibility of competitive moats.
- **Unique value:** The quantitative foundation for all network effect strategy. Before Metcalfe's Law, 'more users = more value' was intuitive but vague. The n² formulation makes the increasing returns precise and reveals the winner-take-most structure of network markets—the bigger network isn't just better, it's quadratically better.
- **Limits:** The n² formula assumes all connections are equally valuable, which is never true. Beyond a certain size, marginal users add less value. The law describes value creation but not value capture (how much the network operator extracts). Some networks have local rather than global network effects, limiting the n² scaling. The law says nothing about quality of the network experience.
- **Foundations:** Developed by Robert Metcalfe for Ethernet networks; extended by subsequent network economics research. Rooted in combinatorics (n² connections), network externalities theory (Katz & Shapiro), and telecommunications economics. Related to Reed's Law, platform economics, and winner-take-all dynamics in digital markets.

### Moore's Law · #1177 · `medium` · technology · CLAIM
- **What it is:** Gordon Moore's 1965 observation that the number of transistors on a microchip doubles approximately every two years, producing exponential growth in computing power at declining cost. For over 50 years, this held remarkably stable, driving the entire information technology revolution. Moore's Law isn't a physical law but a self-fulfilling economic prediction: the industry organized around achieving it, which made it happen. As a mental model, it represents the broader phenomenon of exponential technological improvement—and the human inability to intuit exponential growth, which causes consistent underestimation of technological change.
- **How it helps:** Use Moore's Law as a proxy for exponential thinking in technology-driven domains. What's expensive and clunky today will be cheap and elegant in 5-10 years. What seems impossible today may be trivial in a decade. Strategic implications: don't optimize for today's technology constraints—they'll relax exponentially. Build platforms and architectures that can absorb dramatic improvements in underlying technology. The most common strategic error in technology: assuming today's limitations are permanent when they're actually temporary barriers on an exponential curve.
- **When to use:** When making long-term technology investment decisions; when current technological limitations feel permanent (they probably aren't); when evaluating whether a technology-dependent opportunity is 'too early' (it probably is, but less than you think); when calibrating expectations about technology-driven change.
- **Unique value:** The single most important model for understanding technology-driven change: exponential improvement is counterintuitive and consistently underestimated. The human brain thinks linearly; technology evolves exponentially. This mismatch explains why technologists and futurists consistently surprise everyone else—they've internalized exponential thinking.
- **Limits:** Moore's Law is slowing as transistors approach physical limits. Not all technology follows exponential curves. The model can create complacency ('technology will solve it'). Exponential improvement in hardware doesn't automatically translate to exponential improvement in software or in solving hard problems. The 'everything is exponential' mindset can produce unrealistic expectations.
- **Foundations:** Observed empirically by Intel co-founder Gordon Moore in integrated circuit density. Rooted in semiconductor physics, manufacturing engineering, and technology forecasting. Draws on learning curves (Wright's Law), economies of scale in production, and technology S-curve theory. Related to Kurzweil's law of accelerating returns and Jevons Paradox.
- *Secondary roles:* — · *Collection:* named-law
